A grant application takes months to prepare and months to be decided, and its budget has to follow both the funder's rules and the RI-MUHC's. The [Pre-Awards Office](mailto:pre-awards.rimuhc@muhc.mcgill.ca) helps researchers find the right competition, build the budget and submit the application.

## Get help from the RI-MUHC with the application

Write to the [Pre-Awards Office](mailto:pre-awards.rimuhc@muhc.mcgill.ca) as soon as you find a funding opportunity, and at least eight weeks before the deadline. Its help matters most for a first application, and for revising an application that was not funded. A request made at the last minute limits what the office can do.

The Pre-Awards Office can help with:

- The funding strategy: which competitions fit, and how to align the application with the funder's priorities.
- The grant budget, built on the RI-MUHC's costs: overhead, salary scales and equipment.
- A review of the draft application for completeness and for the funder's requirements.
- Letters of support, and proposals of peer reviewers.
- Medical writers and strategic consultants.
- Institutional signatures, and submissions to portals that require the institution's authorization.

For a grant application of \$50,000 or more a year, the RI-MUHC requires an internal scientific review, which the Pre-Awards Office organizes. The review is there to strengthen the application, and it is not an ethics requirement.

Every proposal also requires the completion of the RI-MUHC's [Pre-Awards Checklist](/kb/funding/budgets-contracts#include-overhead-in-the-budget).

## Get help with the design and the data plan

The [Biostatistics Consulting Unit (BCU)](/kb/design-support/bcu) supports grant applications with:

- Study design.
- Sample size and power calculations.
- The statistical analysis plan.
- Letters of support for the methods section.

The BCU charges for its consultations, with [some hours free for early career researchers](/kb/design-support/bcu#fees). A well-justified sample size and statistical plan can make the difference in a competitive review.

[Capture and Optimization of Research Data (CORD)](/kb/design-support/cord) can review the data management plan in a grant application. It can also provide letters of support that confirm REDCap and the RI-MUHC's data management infrastructure are available.

All grant proposals submitted to the federal research agencies should include methodologies that reflect best practices in research data management. For certain funding opportunities, the agencies require a [data management plan](https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/interagency-research-funding/policies-and-guidelines/research-data-management/tri-agency-research-data-management-policy) with the application, as the call for proposals sets out, and the plan is considered in the adjudication.

## Plan the study around the grant calendar

Grant deadlines and study plans don't line up. In [CIHR's fall 2026 Project Grant competition](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/49051.html), applications are due Sept. 9, 2026, CIHR announces its decisions on Jan. 28, 2027, and funding starts on April 1, 2027, about seven months after the deadline. Consider these points while the application is under review:

- For a study that needs the approval of the Research Ethics Board (REB), consider preparing the protocol during the review rather than waiting for the decision.
- Salaries, equipment costs and service fees quoted in the application may change before the funds arrive. Consider building reasonable inflation into a budget that runs over several years.
- Co-investigators and key staff named in the application may not be free a year later. Consider confirming their commitments as the start date approaches.

## Build the grant budget

A grant budget differs from an industry-sponsored budget. Funders expect a detailed justification for every line. The usual categories:

- Personnel: the salaries and benefits of research staff, from the RI-MUHC's salary scales, with the [benefits rate](/kb/funding/budgets-contracts#estimate-staff-costs) and annual cost-of-living increases where the funder allows them.
- Equipment. Funders often restrict or cap equipment. Some exclude it, and others allow it only in the first year, so check the funder's policy.
- Supplies: laboratory supplies, reagents, specimen collection materials and printing.
- Services: BCU consultations, CORD's data management fees, pharmacy services and imaging.
- Participant costs: compensation, travel reimbursement and parking.
- Knowledge mobilization: publication fees, conference presentations and patient engagement activities.
- Overhead: whether the funder pays it in addition, or it comes out of the grant total.

[The RI-MUHC's overhead rate depends on the funding source](/kb/funding/budgets-contracts#include-overhead-in-the-budget), and no overhead is added to the Tri-Agency portion of a grant, which the federal Research Support Fund covers. Include the rate that applies in the grant budget.

CIHR and the FRQ set requirements for equity, diversity and inclusion (EDI) in applications, including sex- and gender-based analysis in the study design and attention to the team's composition. [Equity, Diversity and Inclusion](/kb/team/edi#funder-edi-requirements) explains them.

## Apply for career and salary awards

Project and team grants fund studies. Career awards fund the investigator: they protect research time and pay a salary supplement or replacement, so that clinician-scientists can keep a research program alongside their clinical duties. The two have different applications, review criteria and calendars, and researchers often apply for a career award with a project grant or just before one.

### FRQ career awards

The FRQ runs the main career awards for Quebec clinician-scientists in health research:

| Award | Stage | What it funds | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chercheur-boursier Junior 1 | Early career: 0 to 3 years of research experience, as the FRQS counts it | Salary support, partial or full | It can be renewed at Junior 2. |
| Chercheur-boursier Junior 2 | Established early career: 4 to 7 years of research experience | Salary support at a higher level | A strong record of productivity, with competitive national and international publications. |
| Chercheur-boursier Senior | Mid to late career: 8 to 11 years of research experience | Salary support for sustained research leadership | Documented mentorship of trainees and a significant funding record. |
| Research Professionals Excellence | Research professionals who are not physicians or PhDs and hold research leadership roles | Salary support | Less common. Open to senior coordinators, research coordinators and data scientists in leadership roles. |

Each chercheur-boursier applicant chooses one of four research areas: basic science, clinical and epidemiological science, health and society, or health and technologies. Applicants must devote at least 75 per cent of their working hours to research. Health care professionals licensed in Quebec may apply if they spend at least 50 per cent of their working hours on research.

FRQ career awards are renewable and can be held with CIHR funding. FRQ competitions typically open in the fall. Check the [FRQ's site](https://frq.gouv.qc.ca), and ask the Pre-Awards Office for the current calendar.

### Federal career and salary awards

| Program | For | Notes |

| Canada Postdoctoral Research Award (CPRA) | Postdoctoral researchers | Administered by CIHR, NSERC and SSHRC. \$70,000 a year for 24 months. It replaced the Banting Postdoctoral Fellowships, which no longer accept applications. |

| Canada Research Chairs, Tier 1 and Tier 2 | Researchers acknowledged as world leaders in their fields (Tier 1) and exceptional emerging researchers (Tier 2) | Each eligible institution receives an allocation of chairs and nominates a researcher for each. At the federal level, research time is mostly protected through a salary buyout in a Project or Team Grant budget, or through a Canada Research Chair. Consider discussing your career stage and funding strategy with the [Pre-Awards Office](mailto:pre-awards.rimuhc@muhc.mcgill.ca).

### Bridge funding

When a grant is not renewed or a competition is missed, bridge funding can keep a research program going. Sources at the RI-MUHC include:

- The MUHC Foundation's internal competitions, open to RI-MUHC investigators. Ask the [Pre-Awards Office](mailto:pre-awards.rimuhc@muhc.mcgill.ca) about the current cycles.
- Departmental bridge funds, which some clinical departments keep. Ask your department chief.
- CIHR's priority announcements: targeted competitions on shorter cycles that run between the major grant cycles. Check CIHR's funding opportunities for open calls.
- Foundations for specific diseases, which run their competitions apart from the federal agencies' calendar and often decide faster.

## Write the knowledge mobilization plan

Many funders ask for a knowledge mobilization plan with the application: how the findings will reach practice, policy or the public, beyond publication. CIHR calls this knowledge translation (KT).

### Integrated and end-of-grant knowledge translation

CIHR distinguishes two approaches, and applications use its terms:

- Integrated knowledge translation works with knowledge users, such as clinicians, patients, policy-makers and health system decision-makers, through the whole study, from the research question to the interpretation and the dissemination of the results. It is part of the study's design, not something added after data collection. For CIHR Team Grants and SPOR grants, it is often a requirement or a rated criterion.
- End-of-grant knowledge translation covers what happens after the research is complete: publications, conference presentations, briefings for knowledge users and policy briefs. It is the baseline expectation for every CIHR grant.

For a Project Grant, applicants describe at least their end-of-grant activities. Integrated knowledge translation sets an application apart where the research question and the relationships with knowledge users support it.

### The knowledge-to-action cycle

[Graham and colleagues' knowledge-to-action framework](https://doi.org/10.1002/chp.47) describes two parts. In knowledge creation, research is synthesized and turned into tools. In the action cycle, the knowledge is adapted to its setting and applied, then its use is monitored, evaluated and sustained. Reviewers know the framework well, so it helps to frame the plan in its terms:

- Knowledge creation: systematic reviews, synthesis and the development of knowledge tools.
- The action cycle: identifying the problem, adapting the knowledge, assessing barriers and facilitators, implementing, evaluating and sustaining use.

For most clinical trials, the plan says where the study sits in the cycle and which steps of the action cycle its activities address.

### What a CIHR knowledge mobilization plan includes

Consider building the plan in five parts:

1. Name the knowledge users: who needs to act on the research. Name them precisely, such as the clinical specialty, the health authority or the body that will use the findings, not "health care providers" in general.
2. Describe the activities: publications, practice guidelines, policy briefs, summaries for patients, conference presentations, meetings with knowledge users and media work. Say what you will do, not what you hope to do.
3. Show how knowledge users take part. If you have partners, such as patient partners, clinical champions or policy advisors, name them and describe their role. Letters of support strengthen the application.
4. Give the dissemination timeline: when the findings will be ready, and how that fits with policy windows or the update cycles of practice guidelines.
5. Budget the activities: conference travel, knowledge translation materials, patient partners' time and open-access publication fees. CIHR treats these costs as eligible, and reviewers expect to see them in the budget.

The [Research Facilitator](mailto:research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca) and the [Pre-Awards Office](mailto:pre-awards.rimuhc@muhc.mcgill.ca) can review the plan before you submit. For patient-oriented research, [Patient Engagement in Research](/kb/design/patient-engagement) explains how to set up partnerships with patients.

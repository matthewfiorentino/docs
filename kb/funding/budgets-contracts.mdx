Every research budget at the RI-MUHC includes both the study's direct costs and its institutional costs, also called overhead. The principal investigator, who leads the study at the MUHC, is accountable for preparing the budget and approves it. The principal investigator is also responsible and accountable for adhering to the budget and for meeting the study's contractual commitments.

No institutional office reviews a study budget. For a study run by the Centre for Innovative Medicine (CIM), the CIM's budget specialists review it before the principal investigator approves it.

The terms the budget is built on go into the study's agreements, which the [Research Agreements Office](mailto:contracts@muhc.mcgill.ca) reviews. What an agreement needs, and how it is signed, is on [Research Agreements](/kb/funding/clinical-trial-agreements).

## Start from the Clinical Trial Budget Template

The RI-MUHC's Clinical Trial Budget Template, launched in January 2026, is the recommended starting point for building a study budget. It includes staff salary tables based on the institutional salary scales, the time and effort for each study visit, and the costs of MUHC services. From these, the template produces both a project cost summary and a budget in the format of the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR).

The template is on the RI-MUHC Portal. Write to the [Research Facilitator](mailto:research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca) about it.

## Include overhead in the budget

The RI-MUHC applies overhead to eligible direct costs to recover its institutional costs. Its standard rate is 35 per cent, unless the funder, the sponsor or an institutional policy sets another rate. Every proposal and agreement must include the highest overhead rate the funder or sponsor allows.

Include overhead when you prepare the budget, before you submit the proposal or negotiate with the sponsor. Every proposal also requires the completion of the RI-MUHC's Pre-Awards Checklist, which the [Pre-Awards Office](mailto:pre-awards.rimuhc@muhc.mcgill.ca) provides.

Consult the RI-MUHC's Research Services on the budget and its compliance with the policy. Make sure that the discussions before an agreement reflect the policy's requirements.

| Funding source | Overhead rate |
|---|---|
| Industry-funded clinical trials | 35 per cent of all eligible direct costs. |
| Grants from the federal Tri-Agency: the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR), the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council (NSERC) and the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council (SSHRC) | No added overhead on the Tri-Agency portion, whose indirect costs the federal Research Support Fund covers. Other portions, such as matching funds, carry the highest rate their funder allows. |
| US National Institutes of Health (NIH) | 8 per cent of direct costs, excluding equipment. |
| US Department of Defense | The RI-MUHC's negotiated rate, renewed each year. Ask the Pre-Awards Office for the current rate. |
| Other US government funding | The RI-MUHC's rate for foreign institutions. Ask the Pre-Awards Office for the current rate. |
| Unrestricted donations and fundraising transfers from an MUHC foundation | 15 per cent. |
| Industry funds transferred from an MUHC foundation with a charitable receipt | 35 per cent. |
| Other funders and sponsors, such as charities and other foundations | The highest rate the funder allows, or 35 per cent if it sets none. |

For a source not listed here, such as a transfer from an MUHC department, ask the [Pre-Awards Office](mailto:pre-awards.rimuhc@muhc.mcgill.ca).

Researchers may not waive, reduce or negotiate the overhead on their own. An exception needs the prior written approval of the RI-MUHC's Executive Director. A temporary rate adjustment must be approved and documented in writing before the proposal is submitted. If a budget leaves overhead out or reduces it, the RI-MUHC can decline the project. The RI-MUHC can also cover the overhead by reducing the project's other direct costs, where the funder's guidelines and the RI-MUHC's priorities allow.{/* MF, Nov. 15, 2026 (ledger row 202, answer 2, "Exec Director"): the page names the Executive Director. Where a funder's direct costs can include them, research grant applications and agreements must include these costs as direct costs:

- The RI-MUHC's research services, such as grant staff, finance staff, patent costs and regulatory or compliance services, where they are eligible.
- The salary recovery of RI-MUHC researchers.

Ask the [Pre-Awards Office](mailto:pre-awards.rimuhc@muhc.mcgill.ca) which costs are eligible direct costs. Flag start-up costs, such as initiation visits, site set-up and the first regulatory submissions, separately when you submit to the [Research Agreements Office](mailto:contracts@muhc.mcgill.ca), and ask the Pre-Awards Office whether they count as eligible direct costs.

## Plan what the budget must cover

A study's financial agreements may cover:

- Fees for the sponsor-investigator, the principal investigator, the sub-investigators and other members of the research team.
- Payment per participant or per visit, and payment for participants who complete the study.
- The payment schedule.
- Reimbursement of participants' expenses, such as transportation and parking.
- Reimbursement of costs for patient referrals, or for remote trial conduct by sub-investigators at satellite sites.
- Reimbursement of the MUHC departments and other service providers that support the study, such as nursing, pharmacy, laboratories and radiology.

The site must be aware of the financial implications of every study it takes part in, including the direct and indirect costs of using its facilities. The sponsor must also address the costs of treating participants for trial-related injuries, in line with the regulatory requirements that apply.

Budget the costs of keeping the study's records for [the retention period the RI-MUHC's standard operating procedures (SOPs) set](/kb/close-out/close-out#supply-reconciliation-and-archiving), and negotiate them before the study starts.

## Estimate staff costs

The RI-MUHC calculates staff costs from its institutional salary scales:

- One full-time employee works 1,820 hours a year (35 hours a week for 52 weeks).
- Benefits add 19 per cent to the base salary.
- The cost to the study is the base salary times 1.19, times the share of a full-time position the study funds.
- For a study that runs over several years, add a cost-of-living increase of 5 per cent a year, compounded.

## Consider adding a contingency

Consider adding a contingency to the staff and services subtotal, before overhead and the Research Ethics Board (REB) fees. Consider scaling it to the study's complexity:

| Complexity | Range | Typical study types |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 8 to 10 per cent | Observational, retrospective and simple cross-sectional studies. |
| Moderate | 10 to 12 per cent | Prospective interventional studies, phase II and III trials, studies with many visits. |
| High | 12 to 15 per cent | Phase I, first-in-human and complex interventional trials. |

## Budget the REB and authorization fees

Quebec's public health institutions charge private enterprise for the review and authorization of research projects. The fees are set in a schedule of Quebec's health ministry, the Ministère de la Santé, de la Prévention et des Aînés, issued as an annex to directive 2023-016 of the Ministère de la Santé et des Services sociaux (MSSS), the ministry's former name.

The schedule is indexed every April 1 to the pension index of the Quebec Pension Plan. The amounts below are in force from April 1, 2026.

| Fee item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Scientific review | \$647 |
| Ethics review | \$6,475 |
| Authorization | \$1,942 per institution |
| Annual ethics monitoring | \$647 per participating institution, for up to 10 institutions, at each renewal |
| Annual authorization monitoring | \$647 per institution, at each renewal |
| Major amendment | \$647 per amendment |

The scientific review and the ethics review are each billed once per project, and only when a committee of the institution does them. A single-site study whose two reviews are done by the MUHC's committees costs \$9,064 in its first year: the scientific review, the ethics review and one authorization.

The MUHC REB bills any project funded wholly or partly by private enterprise, where the private funding exceeds the exemption amount in the schedule (\$19,420 from April 1, 2026). The REB also bills, among others:

- Clinical trials funded and sponsored by a company that makes a drug, a medical device, software or another therapeutic product.
- Investigator-initiated clinical trials with private or mixed funding for which the RI-MUHC is the sponsor.

The REB counts every legal person governed by private law as private enterprise, not-for-profit organizations included. A project can be billable before any contract is signed. Each review is billed whatever the REB decides, and the fees are not refunded.

When you submit the study, identify every source of funding in the submission form. If private enterprise funds the study wholly or partly, give the private enterprises' billing contacts and the budgets. If you do not give the billing contact information at submission, follow up with the [Research Agreements Office](mailto:contracts@muhc.mcgill.ca), the RI-MUHC's finance department or both.{/* REB-SOP 350.002, section 5.1.3, says "the contracts office and/or the finance department of the RI-MUHC"; the Contracts Office is the Research Agreements Office's former name (FACTS "The Research Agreements Office"). When you close or abandon the project, [notify the REB](/kb/close-out/close-out#notifying-the-reb-and-health-canada). Every REB action before the REB confirms the project's closure is billable.

## Negotiate the budget with an industry sponsor

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Industry-sponsored studies</span></div>

An industry sponsor usually proposes the budget for a clinical trial, and the site negotiates it. In an industry-sponsored study, the principal investigator negotiates the budget with the sponsor while the [Research Agreements Office](mailto:contracts@muhc.mcgill.ca) negotiates the agreement's clauses. The Research Agreements Office does not negotiate or approve the budget. The budget respects the rates the hospital departments set.

The research contract must give the total amount granted, and a detailed budget with the cost of each examination, test or other procedure the protocol requires.

For a study run by the CIM, the [CIM's budget specialists](mailto:nadia.lombardo@muhc.mcgill.ca) can help prepare and negotiate the budget, and they estimate the cost of the CIM services a study uses. Consider contacting them during feasibility, before the clinical trial agreement is signed. Most budget problems come from engaging too late. If the protocol uses CIM services, hospital departments or outside vendors, consider putting their costs in the first budget, before the clinical trial agreement is signed.

The industry sponsor pays the start-up fees, which are invoiced after the service has been provided. Pharmacy fees are invoiced as needed. Start-up, REB and pharmacy fees are not refunded, and researchers cannot waive them.

The RI-MUHC is a hospital authority that is a registered charity, and the clinical trial services it provides to industry sponsors are exempt from the goods and services tax and the harmonized sales tax (GST and HST), so the budget includes neither.

### Prepare the negotiation

Consider these checklists when you prepare the negotiation with the sponsor:

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Documents to gather before you negotiate">
    - The protocol, draft or final. Its schedule of assessments is the basis of the budget, and every procedure it lists needs a budget line.
    - The consent form. The form gives the visit details, the procedures described to participants and what participants are promised for compensation.
    - The sponsor's proposed budget, the starting point of the negotiation.
    - The exhibit, or financial appendix. The exhibit sets the payment terms: the schedule, the holdback, the invoicing instructions, the currency and the archiving terms.
    - Quotes from the departments and vendors the study will use. Nursing, pharmacy, ophthalmology, pathology, imaging and laboratory services each set their own costs.

    Consider comparing the protocol's schedule of assessments with the sponsor's proposed budget, procedure by procedure. A procedure the protocol lists and the budget leaves out is a cost the site absorbs unless it is added during the negotiation.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Costs to check in the sponsor's budget">
    Every industry-sponsored budget covers four kinds of cost: site costs, site management costs, patient costs and patient administration costs. The items most often missing from a sponsor's budget are marked "often missing".

    Site costs:

    - Start-up fees: the REB submission, the pharmacy's set-up, the regulatory documents, the preparation for the site qualification and site initiation visits, the team's training on the protocol and the time spent negotiating the contract.
    - The [REB and authorization fees](#budget-the-reb-and-authorization-fees).
    - Pharmacy fees: start-up, dispensing at each visit, drug accountability, storage, on-call pharmacist services and annual maintenance.
    - The RI-MUHC's [overhead](#include-overhead-in-the-budget).
    - Archiving: storage, security and retrieval of the documents over [the retention period](/kb/close-out/close-out#supply-reconciliation-and-archiving). Archiving happens at the end of the study and is budgeted at the start.
    - Laboratory and imaging set-up fees, for the one-time set-up of a department's workflow (often missing). Get the departments' quotes before signing.
    - Lead site submission fees, if the site leads a multi-site submission in Canada.

    Site management costs:

    - Pre-study and qualification visits: the team's time for the sponsor's visits during feasibility.
    - Monitoring visits: preparing and hosting on-site and remote visits, retrieving documents and following up.
    - Protocol amendments: re-training, re-consent, revised source documents and the REB submission for each amendment (often missing from the sponsor's first budget). The average industry trial has several amendments.
    - Re-consent: the coordinator's and the principal investigator's time for each active participant, for each amendment to the consent form (often missing).
    - Annual REB renewals: preparing the submission, and the fees, for each year the study is open.
    - Document translation, for participants who speak neither English nor French, or for Quebec regulatory submissions (often missing).
    - The close-out visit: preparation, hosting, data cleaning, queries, final drug accountability and regulatory submissions. The visit happens at the end of the study and is budgeted at the start.
    - Audit support: the team's time to prepare for and take part in an audit by the sponsor, Health Canada, another regulator or the institution (often missing).

    Patient costs, by visit:

    - Screening visits: the consent process, the eligibility assessment and every screening procedure.
    - Treatment and follow-up visits: every assessment and procedure the protocol requires, priced by the visit's complexity. A 3-hour infusion visit costs more than a 30-minute follow-up.
    - Screen failures, paid for every procedure completed (often missing).
    - Re-screening, needed if the protocol allows a participant who failed screening to be screened again (often missing).
    - Unscheduled visits: the principal investigator's and the coordinator's time, and the participant's stipend (often missing).
    - Tests that are not standard of care, done only for the research.
    - Third-party costs: imaging, central and local laboratory work, pathology and ophthalmology. Local laboratories, pathology and imaging cover every protocol procedure an MUHC department outside the research team performs. Get the departments' quotes before you negotiate.
    - Overnight stays, for phase I or intensive monitoring protocols.
    - Participant reimbursement: transportation, including taxis for participants who cannot drive, parking, meals and any stipend or reimbursement the consent form promises.
    - Dry ice and specimen shipping, packaging included, for each shipment of samples to a central laboratory (often missing).
    - Participant transportation, if the site is to arrange participants' travel (often missing).

    Patient administration costs:

    - The principal investigator's fees: oversight, medical review, sign-off and availability for questions about the protocol, by visit or by participant.
    - The study coordinator's fees: screening, consent, visits, data entry, queries, source document verification, monitoring visits, serious adverse event reports and regulatory upkeep. The coordinator's time is the line most often underestimated.
    - The coordinator's and the principal investigator's fees on every participant visit: screening, re-screening, unscheduled and follow-up visits as well as treatment visits.
    - Research nurse fees, for procedures that need a nurse, such as intravenous administration, blood draws, vital signs and electrocardiograms.
    - Data entry fees: case report forms, electronic data capture, queries and data cleaning.
    - Pharmacy administration: dispensing at each visit, accountability and annual fees.
    - Serious adverse event reporting: the coordinator's and the principal investigator's time to prepare, submit and follow up each report, which adds up in large trials (often missing).
    - Reviews of the sponsor's safety reports: the principal investigator's time to review and acknowledge each report (often missing).

    Other lines that often become costs no one budgeted:

    - Advertising and recruitment costs, if the site is to recruit actively.
    - The administrative time for uploading invoices to the sponsor's invoicing portal.
    - A [contingency](#consider-adding-a-contingency), scaled to the study's complexity.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Payment terms in the exhibit">
    The exhibit, or financial appendix, sets how and when the site is paid. Most of the negotiation happens here.

    Industry-sponsored trials in Canada are funded in one of two ways:

    - Activity-based funding, the most common. The parties agree a fixed fee for each visit or procedure, paid as milestones are completed, such as enrolment, a completed visit or data entry. Most sponsors propose it.
    - Position-based funding. The sponsor pays a coordinator's annual cost up front, and specific procedures and pass-through costs are billed separately. Position-based funding is more common in academic and investigator-initiated research, and some sponsors propose it for large or complex trials.

    Consider confirming which model the sponsor proposes before you negotiate the individual lines. Consider also negotiating these terms:

    - Start-up fees payable once the clinical trial agreement is fully executed, not when the first participant enrols, written in the exhibit.
    - Whether payments are automatic, triggered by milestones in the electronic data capture system, or invoiced. If they are invoiced, confirm the process, the portal and the mailing address.
    - Payment within 30 to 60 days of a correct invoice.
    - Monthly or quarterly payment, on a schedule that suits the study's enrolment. Infrequent payments strain the cash flow of a study that enrols slowly.
    - The currency, Canadian or US dollars, settled before signing, since it affects every line. For a budget in US dollars, how changes in the exchange rate are handled.
    - One full participant visit paid up front, refundable if no one enrols, which protects the site while enrolment is slow.

    Many sponsors hold back part of each payment until data cleaning or database lock.

    The practice began when data travelled on paper between sites and sponsors, and electronic data capture and real-time monitoring make it harder to justify.

    Consider asking the sponsor to remove the holdback. If it stays, consider negotiating it down and asking for release participant by participant, as each participant's data are cleaned, rather than all at once at the end of the study. Consider also settling what triggers the release: database lock can come months or years after the last participant's last visit.

    Sponsors often cap the payment for screen failures at a fixed ratio, for example one screen failure paid for every three participants randomized. Every screen failure uses the site's resources, however many participants are randomized in the end. Consider negotiating to remove the ratio.

    If the ratio stays, consider basing it on the realistic screen failure rate for your population: rates differ widely between conditions, and the published rates are in [Set a realistic recruitment target](/kb/recruitment/recruitment-enrollment#set-a-realistic-recruitment-target). Consider making sure a screen failure is paid for every procedure completed, not a flat fee. If a participant who failed screening had laboratory tests, imaging and an electrocardiogram, each is paid.

    Consider checking the rest of the exhibit too:

    - The payee's address is the RI-MUHC's, not a personal address.
    - The invoicing instructions say where and how to send invoices. Some sponsors require invoices through a portal, in a set format.
    - Every message carries the file number the Research Agreements Office gives the agreement.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Common mistakes">
    - Underestimating the coordinator's time. Every procedure takes coordinator effort, and it is the cost most often underestimated at Canadian sites.
    - Accepting the same payment for every visit, whatever its length.
    - Leaving out the cost of amendments. The average industry trial has several.
    - Forgetting the departments' fees: pharmacy dispensing, laboratory processing, imaging, dry ice, specimen shipping and participant transportation.
    - Vague terms for unscheduled visits, which leave the site absorbing their cost.
    - Accepting terms that strain the cash flow: payment after 90 days, a large holdback or infrequent payments, especially for a site with several trials at once.
    - Overlooking archiving, a real cost over the whole retention period.
    - No payment up front. If the ethics review or enrolment is slow, the site is in deficit before the first participant enrols.
    - Keeping a study that does not enrol open. A study that does not enrol uses the coordinator's time, the pharmacy and regulatory upkeep without revenue, so consider building in a way to renegotiate or close it.
    - Not benchmarking the rates. Provincial and commercial benchmarking data exist for common procedures. Consider asking the CIM's budget specialists whether rate data are available for your type of study.
    - Adding tax the site does not charge.
  </Accordion>

    | University of British Columbia (UBC) | 40 per cent | All industry funding. Government and non-profit funding carries 25 per cent. |

    Sponsors pay no tax on clinical trial services at a Canadian non-profit research institute, while a for-profit contract research organization must charge it.

### Finalize the budget before the agreement is signed

When the negotiation is over, the sponsor sends clean final copies of the budget and the exhibit. Consider checking that they match what you agreed, not an earlier draft. Then reply to the sponsor with a copy to the [Research Agreements Office](mailto:contracts@muhc.mcgill.ca). Attach the approved documents as the final version, and give the agreement's file number.

The Research Agreements Office reviews the whole clinical trial agreement before it is signed. Consider checking one last time that the budget attached to the agreement is the version you approved. An error found after signing is costly to correct.

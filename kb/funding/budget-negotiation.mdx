An industry sponsor usually proposes the budget for a clinical trial, and the site negotiates it. At the RI-MUHC, the principal investigator, who leads the study at the MUHC, negotiates the budget and approves it. The negotiation runs while the [Research Agreements Office](mailto:contracts@muhc.mcgill.ca) negotiates the clauses of the clinical trial agreement, and the budget respects the rates the hospital departments set.

The RI-MUHC's overhead and the other costs every study budget must include are on [Study Budgets](/kb/funding/budgets-contracts), with the Research Ethics Board (REB) fees that a study funded wholly or partly by private enterprise pays. For a study run by the Centre for Innovative Medicine (CIM), the [CIM's budget specialists](#get-help-from-the-cim-budget-specialists) review the budget and can help prepare and negotiate it.

## Gather the documents before you negotiate

Consider gathering these documents before you meet the sponsor:

| Document | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| The protocol, draft or final | Its schedule of assessments is the basis of the budget. Every procedure it lists needs a budget line. |
| The consent form | The form gives the visit details, the procedures described to participants and what participants are promised for compensation. |
| The sponsor's proposed budget | The sponsor's budget is the starting point of the negotiation. |
| The exhibit, or financial appendix | The exhibit sets the payment terms: the schedule, the holdback, the invoicing instructions, the currency and the archiving terms. |
| Quotes from the departments and vendors the study will use | Nursing, pharmacy, ophthalmology, pathology, imaging and laboratory services each set their own costs. |

Consider comparing the protocol's schedule of assessments with the sponsor's proposed budget, procedure by procedure. A procedure the protocol lists and the budget leaves out is a cost the site absorbs unless it is added during the negotiation.

## Know the Canadian and Quebec context

The RI-MUHC's [overhead rate for industry-funded clinical trials](/kb/funding/budgets-contracts#include-overhead-in-the-budget) is not negotiable. Other Canadian institutions publish their own rates for industry-funded trials:

| Institution | Overhead rate | Applies to |
|---|---|---|
| Vancouver Coastal Health Research Institute (VCHRI) | 30 per cent | Industry-funded clinical trials. |
| University of Calgary | 30 per cent | Clinical trials. Other industry-funded research carries 40 per cent. |
| McMaster University | 40 per cent | Industry-sponsored clinical trials. |
| University of British Columbia (UBC) | 40 per cent | All industry funding. Government and non-profit funding carries 25 per cent. |

Across Canada, rates run from 25 to 40 per cent, and 30 per cent is the most common. Canadian rates are lower than at comparable institutions in the United States, which helps Canada compete for clinical trials.

The RI-MUHC is a hospital authority that is a registered charity, and the clinical trial services it provides to industry sponsors are exempt from the goods and services tax and the harmonized sales tax (GST and HST), so the budget includes neither.

Sponsors pay no tax on these services at a Canadian non-profit research institute, while a for-profit contract research organization must charge it. Canadian hospital authorities can also claim rebates of part of the GST and HST they pay on research inputs.

Health Canada charges no fee for a Clinical Trial Application (CTA), or for operational requests about clinical trials. The REB and authorization fees are separate, and [Quebec's health ministry sets them](/kb/funding/budgets-contracts#budget-the-reb-and-authorization-fees).

In Quebec, CATALIS Québec, a not-for-profit organization mandated by the Government of Quebec, runs the [FAST TRACK evaluation service](/kb/design/multi-centric#catalis-fast-track) for industry-sponsored studies, and Quebec's health ministry released the [Provincial Model Clinical Trial Agreement](/kb/funding/clinical-trial-agreements#read-the-clauses-of-a-clinical-trial-agreement) to speed up the signing of agreements. CATALIS also maintains provincial reference tools, such as standard consent form clauses and regulatory templates.

## Check the sponsor budget for every cost

Every industry-sponsored budget covers four kinds of cost: site costs, site management costs, patient costs and patient administration costs. Consider checking the sponsor's budget for the items most often missing from it:

| Line item | Kind of cost | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Laboratory and imaging set-up fees | Site costs | One-time set-up of a department's workflow. Get the departments' quotes before signing. |
| Protocol amendments | Site management | Often absent from the sponsor's first budget. |
| Re-consent | Site management | The coordinator's and the principal investigator's time for each active participant, for each amendment to the consent form. |
| Document translation | Site management | Translation for participants who speak neither English nor French, or for Quebec regulatory submissions. |
| Audit support | Site management | Staff time for an audit by the sponsor, Health Canada or the institution. |
| Screen failures | Patient costs | Payment for every procedure completed. |
| Re-screening | Patient costs | Often left out. Needed if the protocol allows a participant who failed screening to be screened again. |
| Unscheduled visits | Patient costs | The principal investigator's and the coordinator's time, and the participant's stipend. |
| Dry ice and specimen shipping | Patient costs | The cost of each shipment of samples to a central laboratory. |
| Participant transportation | Patient costs | Needed if the site is to arrange participants' travel. |
| Serious adverse event reporting | Patient administration | The coordinator's and the principal investigator's time for each event. |
| Reviews of the sponsor's safety reports | Patient administration | The principal investigator's time to review and acknowledge each report. Often missing. |

The detail of each kind of cost:

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Site costs">
    - Start-up fees: the REB submission, the pharmacy's set-up, the regulatory documents, the preparation for the site qualification and site initiation visits, the team's training on the protocol and the time spent negotiating the contract.
    - The [REB and authorization fees](/kb/funding/budgets-contracts#budget-the-reb-and-authorization-fees).
    - Pharmacy fees: start-up, dispensing at each visit, drug accountability, storage, on-call pharmacist services and annual maintenance.
    - The RI-MUHC's [overhead](/kb/funding/budgets-contracts#include-overhead-in-the-budget).
    - Archiving: storage, security and retrieval of the documents over [the retention period](/kb/close-out/close-out#supply-reconciliation-and-archiving).
    - Laboratory and imaging set-up fees.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Site management costs">
    - Pre-study and qualification visits: the team's time for the sponsor's visits during feasibility.
    - Monitoring visits: preparing and hosting on-site and remote visits, retrieving documents and following up.
    - Protocol amendments: re-training, re-consent, revised source documents and the REB submission for each amendment. The average industry trial has several amendments.
    - Re-consent: the coordinator's and the principal investigator's time for each active participant when an amendment changes the consent form.
    - Annual REB renewals: preparing the submission, and the fees, for each year the study is open.
    - Document translation.
    - The close-out visit: preparation, hosting, data cleaning, queries, final drug accountability and regulatory submissions.
    - Audit support: the team's time to prepare for and take part in an audit by the sponsor, Health Canada or another regulator.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Patient costs, by visit">
    - Screening visits: the consent process, the eligibility assessment and every screening procedure. A screening visit often takes 2 to 3 hours.
    - Treatment and follow-up visits: every assessment and procedure the protocol requires, priced by the visit's complexity. A 3-hour infusion visit costs more than a 30-minute follow-up.
    - [Screen failures](#negotiate-screen-failure-payments).
    - Re-screening, when the protocol allows it.
    - Unscheduled visits, with the principal investigator's, the coordinator's and the participant's parts.
    - Tests that are not standard of care, done only for the research.
    - Third-party costs: imaging, central and local laboratory work, pathology and ophthalmology.
    - Overnight stays, for phase I or intensive monitoring protocols.
    - Participant reimbursement: transportation, including taxis for participants who cannot drive, parking, meals and any stipend the consent form promises.
    - Dry ice and specimen shipping, packaging included.
    - Participant transportation the site arranges.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Patient administration costs">
    - The principal investigator's fees: oversight, medical review, sign-off and availability for questions about the protocol, by visit or by participant.
    - The study coordinator's fees: screening, consent, visits, data entry, queries, source document verification, monitoring visits, serious adverse event reports and regulatory upkeep. The coordinator's time is the line most often underestimated.
    - Research nurse fees, for procedures that need a nurse, such as intravenous administration, blood draws, vital signs and electrocardiograms.
    - Data entry fees: case report forms, electronic data capture, queries and data cleaning.
    - Pharmacy administration: dispensing at each visit, accountability and annual fees.
    - Serious adverse event reporting: preparing, submitting and following up each report.
    - Reviews of the sponsor's safety reports.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Negotiate the budget

### What the sponsor must pay

Some costs belong in every industry-sponsored budget, and researchers cannot waive them:

- Start-up, REB and pharmacy fees, which are not refunded. The industry sponsor pays the start-up fees, which are invoiced after the service has been provided. Pharmacy fees are invoiced as needed.
- The RI-MUHC's [overhead on all eligible direct costs](/kb/funding/budgets-contracts#include-overhead-in-the-budget).

### What to add if the sponsor leaves it out

Consider adding these lines if the sponsor's budget leaves them out:

- The coordinator's and the principal investigator's fees on every participant visit: screening, re-screening, unscheduled and follow-up visits as well as treatment visits.
- Re-consent, when an amendment requires participants to sign a new consent form.
- Local laboratories, pathology and imaging: every protocol procedure an MUHC department outside the research team performs. Get the departments' quotes before you negotiate.
- Archiving and the close-out visit, which happen at the end and are budgeted at the start.
- Participants' stipends and reimbursements, as the consent form describes them.

Consider also these lines, which often become costs no one budgeted:

| Line item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Lead site submission fees | If the site leads a multi-site submission in Canada. |
| On-site and remote monitoring fees | The team's time to prepare for and host monitoring visits. |
| Amendment processing fees | Re-training, re-consent and the REB submission for each amendment. |
| Serious adverse event reporting fees | The coordinator's and the principal investigator's time for each event, which adds up in large trials. |
| Screen failure and re-screening fees | See [screen failure payments](#negotiate-screen-failure-payments). |
| Unscheduled visit fees | The principal investigator's, the coordinator's and the participant's parts. |
| Advertising and recruitment costs | If the site is to recruit actively. |
| Invoice uploading to the sponsor's portal | The administrative time for the sponsor's invoicing system. |
| [Contingency](/kb/funding/budgets-contracts#consider-adding-a-contingency) | Scaled to the study's complexity. |

## Negotiate the payment terms

The exhibit, or financial appendix, sets how and when the site is paid. Most of the negotiation happens here.

### Payment models

Industry-sponsored trials in Canada are funded in one of two ways:

- Activity-based funding, the most common. The parties agree a fixed fee for each visit or procedure, paid as milestones are completed, such as enrolment, a completed visit or data entry. Most sponsors propose it.
- Position-based funding. The sponsor pays a coordinator's annual cost up front, and specific procedures and pass-through costs are billed separately. Position-based funding is more common in academic and investigator-initiated research, and some sponsors propose it for large or complex trials.

Consider confirming which model the sponsor proposes before you negotiate the individual lines.

### Payment timing

Consider negotiating these terms:

- Start-up fees payable once the clinical trial agreement is fully executed, not when the first participant enrols, written in the exhibit.
- Whether payments are automatic, triggered by milestones in the electronic data capture system, or invoiced. If they are invoiced, confirm the process, the portal and the mailing address.
- Payment within 30 to 60 days of a correct invoice. Across the industry, 23 per cent of sites report payment delays of 90 days or more.
- Monthly or quarterly payment, on a schedule that suits the study's enrolment. Infrequent payments strain the cash flow of a study that enrols slowly.
- The currency, Canadian or US dollars, settled before signing, since it affects every line. For a budget in US dollars, how changes in the exchange rate are handled.
- One full participant visit paid up front, refundable if no one enrols, which protects the site while enrolment is slow.

### Holdback

Many sponsors hold back part of each payment until data cleaning or database lock. About 76 per cent of clinical trial agreements still have a holdback clause.

The practice began when data travelled on paper between sites and sponsors, and electronic data capture and real-time monitoring make it harder to justify.

Consider asking the sponsor to remove the holdback. If it stays, consider negotiating it down and asking for release participant by participant, as each participant's data are cleaned, rather than all at once at the end of the study. Consider also settling what triggers the release: database lock can come months or years after the last participant's last visit.

### Negotiate screen failure payments

Sponsors often cap the payment for screen failures at a fixed ratio, for example one screen failure paid for every three participants randomized.

Every screen failure uses the site's resources, however many participants are randomized in the end. Consider negotiating to remove the ratio.

If the ratio stays, consider basing it on the realistic screen failure rate for your population: rates differ widely between conditions, and the published rates are in [Set a realistic recruitment target](/kb/recruitment/recruitment-enrollment#set-a-realistic-recruitment-target). Consider making sure a screen failure is paid for every procedure completed, not a flat fee. ### Other terms of the exhibit

Consider checking that:

- The payee's address is the RI-MUHC's, not a personal address.
- The invoicing instructions say where and how to send invoices. Some sponsors require invoices through a portal, in a set format.
- Every message carries the file number the [Research Agreements Office](mailto:contracts@muhc.mcgill.ca) gives the agreement.

### Reference figures

Consider these figures as reference points during the negotiation, not as fixed rates:

| What | Typical range | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Screen failure payment | \$50 to \$250 or more per screen failure | Depends on the procedures completed. |
| Screen failure ratio a sponsor imposes | 1 for 3 to 1 for 5 participants randomized | Push to remove it, or to base it on the real rate. |
| Holdback | 5 to 14 per cent of earned payments, typically 10 to 14 | Push for 5 per cent, or none, and release participant by participant. |
| Payment terms | 30 to 60 days | Push back on anything longer than 60 days. |
| Cost of processing an amendment | About \$2,500 per amendment | Re-training, re-consent and the REB submission. Budget for at least two or three. |

## Finalize the budget

When the negotiation is over, the sponsor sends clean final copies of the budget and the exhibit. Then:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Check the final documents">
    Check that they match what you agreed, not an earlier draft.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Send them to your Contract Officer">
    Reply to the sponsor with a copy to your Contract Officer at the [Research Agreements Office](mailto:contracts@muhc.mcgill.ca). Attach the approved documents as the final version, and give the agreement's file number.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Wait for the final review of the agreement">
    The Contract Officer reviews the whole clinical trial agreement, your final budget included, before the signatures.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check the budget before the agreement is signed">
    Check one last time that the attached budget is the version you approved. An error found after signing is costly to correct.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Avoid the common mistakes

Most budget problems come from engaging too late. If the protocol uses CIM services, hospital departments or outside vendors, consider putting their costs in the first budget, before the clinical trial agreement is signed.

The mistakes seen most often:

- Underestimating the coordinator's time. Every procedure takes coordinator effort, and it is the cost most often underestimated at Canadian sites.
- Accepting the same payment for every visit, whatever its length.
- Leaving out the cost of amendments. The average industry trial has several.
- Forgetting the departments' fees: pharmacy dispensing, laboratory processing, imaging, dry ice, specimen shipping and participant transportation.
- Vague terms for unscheduled visits, which leave the site absorbing their cost.
- Accepting terms that strain the cash flow: payment after 90 days, a large holdback or infrequent payments, especially for a site with several trials at once.
- Overlooking archiving, a real cost over the whole retention period.
- No payment up front. If the ethics review or enrolment is slow, the site is in deficit before the first participant enrols.
- Keeping a study that does not enrol open. Such a study uses the coordinator's time, the pharmacy and regulatory upkeep without revenue, so consider building in a way to renegotiate or close it.
- Not benchmarking the rates. Provincial and commercial benchmarking data exist for common procedures. Consider asking the CIM's budget specialists whether rate data are available for your type of study.
- Adding tax the site does not charge (see [the tax status of trial services](#know-the-canadian-and-quebec-context)).

## Get help from the CIM budget specialists

The CIM's budget specialists estimate the cost of the CIM services a study uses. For a study run by the CIM, they also review the budget and can help prepare it and negotiate it with the sponsor. The principal investigator remains responsible for the budget and approves it after their review. For other studies, the principal investigator negotiates the budget, and no institutional office reviews it.

For a study run by the CIM, consider contacting the [CIM's budget specialists](mailto:nadia.lombardo@muhc.mcgill.ca) during feasibility, before the clinical trial agreement is signed.

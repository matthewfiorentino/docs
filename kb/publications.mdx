## Authorship criteria

Authorship of a publication carries credit, academic weight, and accountability. Adding someone as an author who did not meaningfully contribute — or omitting someone who did — are both integrity issues with real consequences. The [ICMJE (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors)](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html) criteria are the standard applied by virtually all biomedical journals, including those most relevant to clinical research at the RI-MUHC.

### The four criteria

To qualify as an author, an individual must meet **all four** of the following:

- Substantial contributions to the **conception or design** of the work, or to the **acquisition, analysis, or interpretation of data**;
- **Reviewing the work critically** for important intellectual content (and having the opportunity to make changes if warranted);
- **Final approval** of the version to be published; and
- **Agreement to be accountable** for all aspects of the work, including ensuring that questions about accuracy or integrity are appropriately investigated and resolved.

All four criteria must be met. Meeting only some of them does not qualify a person for authorship — they should instead be acknowledged by name in the acknowledgements section with their contribution specified.

### What does not qualify for authorship

The following activities alone — without meeting the other criteria — do not qualify someone for authorship: obtaining funding; general supervision of the research group; administrative support; data collection without analytical involvement; and writing assistance, technical editing, or proofreading. These contributions warrant acknowledgement, not authorship.

<Warning>
**Ghost and gift authorship are integrity violations.** Ghost authorship — where someone who meets the criteria is omitted from the author list — and gift authorship — where someone who does not meet the criteria is included — both violate ICMJE guidelines and the Tri-Agency Responsible Conduct of Research framework. Either can result in correction or retraction of the published paper.
</Warning>

### Who typically qualifies at a clinical research site

At an RI-MUHC site, the <Tooltip tip="The physician responsible to the sponsor for the conduct of a clinical trial at the site. One QI per study per Canadian site." headline="Qualified Investigator (QI)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#qi">QI</Tooltip>/PI almost always qualifies — they contribute to study design, interpret results, and take accountability for the conduct of the study. Co-investigators who contribute to analysis and manuscript review typically qualify. CRCs, research nurses, and coordinators are more likely to be acknowledged contributors than authors, unless they have played a substantive role in data analysis or manuscript drafting. Discuss authorship expectations within the team early — ideally before data collection begins — not after the manuscript is written.

## Sponsor publication rights

For industry-sponsored trials, the Clinical Trial Agreement (<Tooltip tip="Application to Health Canada for authorization to conduct a drug trial under Division 5 of the Food and Drug Regulations." headline="Clinical Trial Application (CTA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#cta">CTA</Tooltip>) will contain publication clauses that govern how and when results can be disclosed. These clauses are negotiated before the study starts and are binding. The RI-MUHC SOPs state plainly: any publication related to the study requires the Sponsor's approval.

Common CTA publication provisions include:

- **Review period** — the Sponsor typically has 30–90 days to review a manuscript before submission, to protect confidential information and check for factual accuracy. This is not a veto right, but it can delay submission.
- **Right to remove confidential information** — the Sponsor may request redaction of information that constitutes confidential business information, such as unreleased safety data from other studies or proprietary manufacturing details.
- **Patent protection delay** — publication may be delayed for a defined period (commonly up to 90 days) if the Sponsor needs to file a patent application. This is limited in scope and duration.
- **Multi-site coordination** — for multi-centre trials, publication is often coordinated centrally to ensure consistency. Site investigators may not publish site-level results independently without Sponsor approval.

<Note>
**The practical picture for large multi-centre trials.** In most industry-sponsored multi-centre studies, a steering committee or sponsor-coordinated writing group manages the primary publication — including data analysis, manuscript drafting, and submission timing. Site investigators are listed as co-authors on that coordinated paper (if they meet ICMJE criteria), but they do not publish independently. Site-level data cannot typically be published separately without approval from the publications committee, and often not until after the main paper is out. "The right to publish" and "control over the publication process" are different things. If independent publication rights or timeline matter to you, these need to be negotiated in the CTA before signing — not assumed after data lock. The Research Agreements Office can advise on what language to ask for.
</Note>

<Warning>
**The Sponsor cannot suppress negative results indefinitely.** Under ICMJE policies and CIHR grant conditions, investigators retain the right to publish. A Sponsor review period is not a right to veto publication of accurate results. In Canada, publication rights are governed by contract law, not federal statute — the protection against suppression depends on what your CTA actually says. ICMJE principles and CIHR policies provide ethical and journal-policy backstop, but they are not regulations and carry no penalties against sponsors who violate them. If a Sponsor is seeking to block or significantly delay publication of valid findings, contact the Research Agreements Office and the Research Facilitator — this is a contract compliance matter.
</Warning>

For investigator-initiated and Sponsor-Investigator studies, the investigator holds all publication rights. There are no Sponsor review periods unless voluntarily agreed to with a co-funder.

## Trial registration and results disclosure

Registering a clinical trial and publicly disclosing its results are not the same obligation, but they are linked — and both apply to RI-MUHC researchers depending on study type and funding source.

### Trial registration

Registration must occur before the first participant is enrolled — not after. Under [TCPS2 Article 11.10](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter11-chapitre11.html), all clinical trials involving humans must be registered in a publicly accessible registry as a condition of <Tooltip tip="The ethics committee that reviews and approves research involving human participants. Also called CER at French-language institutions." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#reb">REB</Tooltip> approval. The MUHC REB applies this requirement broadly. The most commonly used registry at the MUHC for industry-sponsored and investigator-initiated trials is [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov). Any WHO-recognized registry or ICMJE-approved registry is acceptable.

### Choosing a registry

WHO-recognized registries all meet the ICMJE minimum standard. The choice depends on funder requirements, sponsor preference, and results-posting obligations.

| Registry | Study types | Who registers | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov) | All types | Sponsor, SI, or site team | Most widely indexed globally; required by CIHR and most industry sponsors; FDAAA 801 results-posting requirements apply to Applicable Clinical Trials (ACTs) — generally phase 2–4 drug, biological, and device studies seeking FDA approval — regardless of funding source |
| [ISRCTN](https://www.isrctn.com/) | All types; frequently used for observational and device studies | Study team (manual process) | WHO-recognized; some funders and journals prefer ISRCTN for non-drug studies |
| [WHO ICTRP](https://trialsearch.who.int/) | Search portal only | Feeds from primary registries | Not a registration destination — aggregates data from WHO-recognized registries for global search |
| Health Canada Clinical Trials Database | Drug trials under a CTA | Sponsor or SI | HC-managed; required for drug CTAs under the *Food and Drug Regulations*; does **not** satisfy TCPS2 or CIHR registration requirements |
| [EU Clinical Trials Register (EU CTR)](https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu/) | EMA-authorized trials | Sponsor or legal representative | Required for EUDAMED-registered devices and EMA-authorized drug trials; now publicly searchable |

**Which registry to use at RI-MUHC:** For most MUHC studies, ClinicalTrials.gov is the appropriate primary registry. ISRCTN is an acceptable alternative where the sponsor or journal prefers it. For studies with a Health Canada CTA, the sponsor handles the HC Clinical Trials Database filing separately — this does not substitute for ClinicalTrials.gov registration.

The registration number must appear in the abstract of any publication reporting trial results. This is an ICMJE requirement — journals will not accept trial manuscripts without it.

### CIHR-funded trials

For trials receiving CIHR grant funds on or after January 1, 2022, the following are mandatory conditions of funding:

- **Summary results** must be posted to the trial registry within **12 months** of the last visit of the last participant for collection of primary outcome data.
- **Publications** describing trial results must be **open access** from the date of publication. Open access publication fees should be included in the trial budget at the planning stage.
- All publications must include the **trial registration number** in the abstract.

CIHR monitors compliance annually and reports publicly on aggregate compliance rates. Non-compliance can result in withholding of new CIHR funding until the breach is remedied. These requirements derive from CIHR's 2020 signing of the WHO Joint Statement on Public Disclosure of Results from Clinical Trials. See the [CIHR Policy Guide](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/52820.html) for full requirements.

<Note>
**TCPS2 Article 11.10 and Tri-Agency Responsible Conduct of Research Framework** — prospective registration and timely results disclosure are ethical requirements under Canadian research policy, not only funder conditions. The REB applies these standards to all research involving participants at the MUHC regardless of funding source.
</Note>

### Health Canada direction

Health Canada has published [draft guidance on registration and results disclosure](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/programs/consultation-registration-clinical-trials-public-disclosure-results-new-guidance-public-search-portal/overview.html) for Health Canada-authorized trials and is developing regulations to formalize these requirements. The current framework is policy guidance rather than binding regulation, but sponsors of drug CTAs are expected to register before enrolment and to disclose results in a timely way. For Sponsor-Investigator studies, the QI/PI holds these obligations directly. This regulatory area is actively evolving — check Health Canada's clinical trials guidance page for current requirements.

## Reporting guidelines

Reporting guidelines specify what information must be included in a publication to allow readers to evaluate the validity of the methods and the reliability of the results. Journals and REBs increasingly require — or strongly recommend — adherence to the relevant guideline for the study design. The most relevant for clinical research at the RI-MUHC:

- [CONSORT](https://www.consort-statement.org) (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) — for randomized controlled trials. Covers randomization, blinding, participant flow, and outcome reporting. A CONSORT checklist is required by most major journals.
- [STROBE](https://www.strobe-statement.org) (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) — for cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional studies.
- [SPIRIT](https://www.spirit-statement.org) (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials) — for trial protocols submitted for publication.

The [EQUATOR Network](https://www.equator-network.org) maintains a comprehensive library of reporting guidelines by study type. Select the applicable guideline at the time of study design, not at the time of writing — it will inform how data are collected and analyzed.

## ICMJE 2024 updates

The ICMJE published updated recommendations in January 2024. The changes that most directly affect RI-MUHC researchers:

### AI use disclosure in manuscripts

ICMJE's position is unambiguous: AI tools and large language models cannot be listed as authors, because authorship requires accountability that an AI cannot hold. However, the January 2024 update added a requirement that authors **disclose in the methods section any use of AI tools in preparing the manuscript** — including writing assistance, literature review, image generation, or data analysis. Authors remain fully accountable for the accuracy and integrity of AI-generated content and must be able to verify any claims produced by an AI tool.

<Note>
ICMJE's AI disclosure requirement covers manuscript preparation, not the research itself. If AI tools were used in study design, data analysis, or clinical decision support, those uses should be described in the relevant methods section regardless of ICMJE — and should be discussed with the [Research Facilitator](mailto:Research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca) for compliance implications under E6(R3) and Law 25. See [AI in Clinical Research](/kb/ai-in-clinical-research) for the regulatory framework.
</Note>

### Authorship in global and LMIC contexts

The 2024 update reinforces obligations around authorship for researchers from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) who contribute substantively to a study. Teams that include LMIC co-investigators — common in CIHR-funded global health studies — should apply the four authorship criteria consistently, and should not substitute LMIC contributor names with acknowledgements when authorship criteria are met. This is an equity and integrity issue, not merely a courtesy.

### Funding and conflict of interest declarations

ICMJE 2024 clarified expectations for completeness in funding and conflict-of-interest disclosures. All funding sources — including in-kind support, pharmaceutical company materials, and non-monetary contributions — must be disclosed. Declarations should reflect contributions to the study, not only to the manuscript. Investigators should complete conflict of interest disclosures at the time of submission, and update them if circumstances change during peer review.

<Note>
**Trial registration dataset.** The ICMJE requires registration of clinical trials before enrolment as a condition of consideration for publication. The registration dataset — the minimum data elements required by WHO-recognized registries — is the [WHO Trial Registration Data Set v2.0](https://www.who.int/clinical-trials-registry-platform/network/who-data-set) (24 items), which ClinicalTrials.gov and other WHO-recognized registries implement. The dataset is a WHO standard, not an ICMJE publication requirement per se, though ICMJE links to it as the applicable reference.
</Note>

## Lay summaries

A lay summary is a plain-language summary of research findings written for a non-specialist audience — patients, the public, and community stakeholders rather than other researchers. Lay summaries are increasingly required and are good practice regardless of mandate.

### When a lay summary is required

| Requirement source | When it applies |
|---|---|
| **CIHR results disclosure** | For CIHR-funded trials with results posted to the registry, a plain-language summary is expected alongside technical results. CIHR's open-access policy reinforces the principle that publicly funded research should be publicly accessible — in format as well as availability. |
| **MUHC REB** | Some protocols — particularly those with patient partner involvement — include a lay summary as part of the results dissemination plan submitted for ethics review. Confirm with the REB at time of submission whether a lay summary commitment is captured in the protocol. |
| **Health Canada Modernized CT Regulations** | The draft framework contemplates plain-language results disclosure for trials conducted under the modernized framework. This requirement is not yet in force; see [Modernized Clinical Trial Regulations](/kb/governance/modernized-ct-regulations) for status. |
| **Patient partners** | Any study that includes patient partners in the research team should commit to providing lay-summary results to those partners as part of the patient engagement plan. This is a relationship obligation, not only a funder one. |

### What a lay summary should include

A lay summary is not a compressed abstract. It is written for someone without a clinical or scientific background. A standard structure for clinical research:

<Steps>
  <Step title="What we were trying to find out">
    State the research question in plain language, with context on why it matters to patients or the health system. Avoid acronyms and technical terms. If they are unavoidable, define them simply.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What we did">
    Briefly describe the study design — who was enrolled, what they received or did, and how long the study lasted. Do not describe statistical methods.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What we found">
    Describe the main results in plain language. Be honest about both what was found and what was not — including if the primary outcome was not met. Negative results are results.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What this means">
    Explain what the findings mean for patients or care — and what they don't mean. Avoid over-claiming. Note what questions remain open.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What happens next">
    Describe any planned next steps — follow-up studies, changes to practice being considered, or remaining open questions.
  </Step>
</Steps>

**Practical tips:** Target a grade 8–10 reading level. Use active voice. Keep sentences short. Have someone outside the research team read a draft before finalizing. The [Research Facilitator](mailto:Research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca) can advise on lay summary language if patient partners are involved in the review.

## Data sharing after publication

Sharing research data after publication is increasingly expected — and in some cases required. Tri-Agency policy, journal mandates, and funder conditions are converging on open data as a default.

### Tri-Agency Research Data Management Policy

The [Tri-Agency Research Data Management (RDM) Policy](https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/interagency-research-funding/policies-and-guidelines/research-data-management/tri-agency-research-data-management-policy) (effective March 1, 2021) applies to all CIHR, NSERC, and SSHRC grant-holders. It requires:

- Researchers to have a data management plan (DMP) for grant applications where required by the competition
- Researchers to deposit data in a suitable repository after publication, "where ethically and legally appropriate"

The RDM Policy does not require sharing participant-level health data where doing so would violate participant consent, ethics approvals, or applicable law. It does require documenting what data are available, where, and under what conditions — even when restricted access applies.

### FAIR principles

The [FAIR principles](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata201618) (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable — Wilkinson et al. 2016) are the internationally recognized standard for research data management and sharing:

- **Findable:** Data are assigned a persistent identifier (DOI or equivalent) and described with rich metadata
- **Accessible:** Data are retrievable by authorized users through a standard protocol; access conditions are clearly documented
- **Interoperable:** Data use standard formats and controlled vocabularies that allow cross-study analysis
- **Reusable:** Data and metadata are described thoroughly enough that another researcher could reproduce or build on the work

FAIR does not mean "open." Participant health data can be FAIR without being publicly accessible — a restricted-access repository with clearly documented access criteria satisfies the principles.

### Data repositories for clinical research

| Repository | Best for | Access model |
|---|---|---|
| [Vivli](https://vivli.org/) | IPD sharing from industry and academic trials; supports ICMJE IPD sharing requests | Controlled — requestors apply; sponsor or PI approves |
| [YODA Project](https://yoda.yale.edu/) | Johnson & Johnson–sponsored data; independent researcher access | Controlled with independent review board |
| [FRDR](https://www.frdr-dfdr.ca/repo/) (Federated Research Data Repository) | Canadian funder-compliant deposit; free for Canadian researchers; health research with appropriate controls | Open or embargoed |
| [OSF](https://osf.io/) (Open Science Framework) | Protocols, analysis plans, de-identified datasets; DOI assignable | Open or private |
| [CORD institutional support](/kb/cord) | RI-MUHC–based projects | Institutional policy governs |

### Individual participant data (IPD) sharing

ICMJE encourages but does not universally require sharing of individual participant data. Where IPD sharing is committed to in the registration record or the published methods, it creates an expectation that data will be made available on reasonable request.

For investigator-initiated trials, CIHR's open science framework encourages IPD sharing where consistent with consent, privacy law, and ethics approvals. Any IPD sharing must be consistent with what participants were told in the consent form. If the original consent form did not contemplate data sharing, sharing requires either a re-consent process or a TCPS2 Article 3.7A consent waiver reviewed by the REB.

<Note>
**Quebec Law 25 and cross-border data sharing.** If participant data collected at the RI-MUHC is to be deposited in a repository accessible to researchers outside Quebec, an ÉFVP (privacy impact assessment) is required before transfer. Contact the privacy office (efvp@muhc.mcgill.ca) before depositing data in any external repository. See [Privacy and Confidentiality in Quebec](/kb/privacy) for the ÉFVP process.
</Note>

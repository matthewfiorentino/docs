Some participants need added protection in a particular study, such as an adult who can't consent, someone who depends on a member of the research team, someone for whom even a modest payment would be hard to refuse or a patient in an emergency.

In their [*Tri-Council Policy Statement*](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter4-chapitre4.html) (<Tooltip tip="The federal research agencies' policy on the ethical conduct of research involving humans, 2022 edition." headline="TCPS 2" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tcps2">TCPS 2</Tooltip>), the federal research agencies ask researchers and the <Tooltip tip="The ethics committee that reviews and approves research involving human participants. Also called CER at French-language institutions." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#reb">Research Ethics Board (REB)</Tooltip> to recognize these circumstances in each study and to protect the people in them, without excluding anyone unfairly.

Quebec's Civil Code adds rules for adults who can't consent. For children, use [Pediatric Research Considerations](/kb/recruitment/pediatric-research).

## Recognize what may make participants vulnerable in your study

Vulnerability in research depends on a person's circumstances in the particular study. Belonging to a group doesn't by itself make someone vulnerable.

The agencies' examples include people who live in an institution, people in dependent situations and people whose poverty or poor health could make even a modest payment an inducement to take risks they would otherwise refuse. Limited capacity to decide whether to take part is another such circumstance. For clinical trials, the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) also names students and junior staff in a hierarchy, people in detention or nursing homes, and patients in emergencies.

As you design the study, look for these circumstances and plan a protection for each one you find:
- The condition, or its treatment, may affect participants' understanding or judgment, and with it their capacity to consent.
- Participants may depend on a member of the team, as patients, students or employees. Plan who will recruit them and who will obtain consent.
- A payment could be hard to refuse for people in financial hardship. [Keep payment from becoming an inducement](#keep-payment-from-becoming-an-inducement).
- You will recruit where people's choice may be limited, such as an emergency department, a psychiatric unit, a long-term care residence or a detention facility. Plan protections suited to that setting.

The REB requires special precautions when you recruit people in these circumstances, to protect their freedom to choose, and every recruitment and consent procedure must be described and justified in your submission. In the consent discussion, you should explain why the person is being invited, which added protections apply to them and what alternatives they have to taking part.

People whose circumstances may make them vulnerable should not be inappropriately included, or automatically excluded, on the basis of those circumstances. Base who takes part on criteria the research question justifies, and follow the [rules on including participants fairly](/kb/recruitment/consent#include-participants-fairly).

Circumstances can change during a study, for example when a student finishes your course, an employee changes jobs or a resident leaves the residence. Watch for changes that create, increase or reduce a participant's vulnerability, and adjust their protections. If you think their consent needs to be renewed, ask the REB.

### Include participants who are pregnant or breastfeeding

[The rules on including participants fairly](/kb/recruitment/consent#include-participants-fairly) also cover people who are pregnant or breastfeeding. If a participant in a trial of an <Tooltip tip="A drug, medical device, or natural health product being tested or used as a reference in a clinical trial." headline="Investigational Product (IP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#ip">investigational product</Tooltip> becomes pregnant, [collect and follow the exposure in utero reports](/kb/recruitment/consent#track-a-pregnancy-during-a-trial).

Based on: [TCPS 2](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter4-chapitre4.html), articles 4.1, 4.3 and 4.7 and [chapter 3, section C](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter3-chapitre3.html); the International Council for Harmonisation's guideline [ICH E6(R3)](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/applications-submissions/guidance-documents/international-council-harmonisation/guidelines.html), sections 1.2.1 and 2.8.10 and its glossary; the MUHC REB's [standard operating procedure (SOP) 404.001-2](https://muhc.ca/cae/muhc-research-ethics-board-reb-standard-operating-procedures-sops), section 5; [SOP-CR-012, section 5.4](/sops/cr-012#5-4-reporting-all-saes-to-the-sponsor-within-24-hours) (pregnancy in a trial).

## Obtain consent for an adult who cannot consent

[The Civil Code sets who may consent for an adult who can't consent](/kb/recruitment/consent#who-can-consent), and the REB decides, when it reviews the project, whether it meets the Civil Code's requirements. [The person who consents for a participant cannot be the researcher or any other member of the research team](/kb/recruitment/consent#who-can-consent). If the participant signed a research directive while they could still decide, setting out their wishes about taking part in research, you and the representative should be guided by it.

Involve the participant in the decision as far as possible, and document their assent. The consent form needs a line for the representative to sign and date, and a line on an assent portion where the participant, if capable, signs and dates. The Centre for Applied Ethics' [adult clinical-trial template](/kb/recruitment/consent-templates#choose-the-consent-form-template) has no assent line, but the Centre's pediatric templates include an assent-and-consent page for an adult who can't consent, with an assent line. Ask the REB which form to use.

All required signatures must be on the current REB-approved consent form before any screening procedure takes place. A procedure done even minutes early is a deviation.

<Warning>
An adult who cannot consent, and who understands the nature and consequences of the research and objects to taking part, cannot take part, whoever has consented for them. The federal research agencies add that if a participant who has some ability to understand the research dissents, in words or by their behaviour, they cannot take part.
</Warning>

The REB examines how the representative's consent will be obtained and documented. When you record the representative's name and their relationship to the participant, you should also note whether they consent as mandatary, as tutor or as the person qualified to consent to the participant's care. If the representative changes during the study, ask the REB whether the new one needs to consent.

A study with no therapeutic purpose may include participants who consent through a representative only if:
- Its objectives can't be met with participants who can consent for themselves.
- The foreseeable risks are low.
- The negative effect on the participant's well-being is minimized and low.
- The law doesn't prohibit it.
- You expressly ask the REB to approve including these participants, and its written approval covers them.

Based on: [Civil Code of Québec](https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/CCQ-1991), article 21; TCPS 2, articles 3.9 to 3.11; the MUHC REB's SOP 404.001-2, sections 5.2.6 and 5.7; [SOP-CR-008, sections 3.1](/sops/cr-008#3-1-requirements), [3.3](/sops/cr-008#3-3-signatures) (signatures before screening), [7](/sops/cr-008#special-populations) (research directives) and [7.2](/sops/cr-008#7-2-non-therapeutic); the Centre for Applied Ethics' adult clinical-trial template (version 4.2, July 15, 2024) and pediatric templates (Sept. 30, 2024).

## Assess capacity and plan for changes

Capacity to consent is the ability to understand the information about a study and to appreciate the consequences of deciding to take part or not. It can vary with how complex the decision is, the circumstances and the moment, and a person may be unable to make some decisions yet still able to decide about some research.

You should plan how you will assess capacity whenever a condition, or its treatment, may affect it, as in cognitive impairment, a psychiatric condition, an acquired brain injury or a neurodegenerative disease.

The protocol should say:
- Who will assess capacity, by role. Consider having someone other than the person who obtains consent do it, to keep the assessment separate from recruitment.
- How they will assess it, naming any structured tool, such as the MacArthur Competence Assessment Tool for Clinical Research (MacCAT-CR).
- If capacity may change during the study, as in delirium, a relapsing psychiatric illness or early dementia, how often you will reassess it and what will prompt a reassessment, such as a clinical deterioration, a change in treatment or a study milestone.
- What happens if a participant loses capacity during the study, for example consent from a representative, withdrawal or continued participation on conditions the REB approves.

For each assessment, you should record in the source documents the date, who assessed capacity, the method used and the result.

Consider adapting consent materials to participants' cognitive and communication abilities, for example with plainer language, large print or an audio version. Like all participant-facing material, adapted materials need the REB's approval and, unless the REB accepts a justification, [an English and a French version](/kb/recruitment/consent#prepare-the-consent-form). If the participant or their representative can't read, or the participant speaks neither English nor French, [the consent process needs an impartial witness or an interpreter](/kb/recruitment/consent#adapt-the-consent-process-when-needed).

A participant who regains capacity during the study [signs their own consent as soon as possible](/kb/recruitment/consent#keep-consent-current), as a condition of staying in it. The Centre for Applied Ethics' adult clinical-trial template has a signature page for this. You should note the reason for the re-consent in the source documents.

Based on: TCPS 2, chapter 3 (section C and articles 3.3 and 3.9) and article 4.6; [SOP-CR-006, section 5.3](/sops/cr-006#5-3-icf-content) (participant materials in English and French); [SOP-CR-008, sections 3.1](/sops/cr-008#3-1-requirements), [7](/sops/cr-008#special-populations) and [7.1](/sops/cr-008#7-1-assent); the Centre for Applied Ethics' adult clinical-trial template.

## Protect the free choice of participants who depend on the team

An employee recruited by their employer, a student by their teacher or a patient by their physician may feel unable to say no, even when no one pressures them. The agencies ask that this influence be judged from the participant's point of view, and it may be greater when the dependency is ongoing or substantial.

The REB reviews how, when, where and by whom potential participants will be contacted, and any power relationship, trust or dependency that could unduly influence them. Describe these in your submission.

As far as possible, keep your role as researcher separate from your role as their physician, teacher, supervisor or employer. If you act in both roles, you must tell them. Where you can, you should have someone with no authority over the participants recruit them and obtain consent.

Saying no, or withdrawing later, should not affect their care, studies or other services; tell them so. A physician should make sure that continued care isn't linked to taking part, and students who decline research for course credit should be offered a comparable alternative.

A clinician-researcher may approach their own patients about a study, but the patients should in no way feel pressured or obliged to take part. [The treating physician should not be the one who obtains consent](/kb/recruitment/consent#choose-who-obtains-consent), unless the REB approves. When participants are also the clinician-researcher's patients, [measures must be taken to minimize therapeutic misconception](/kb/recruitment/consent#choose-who-obtains-consent). Participants have that misconception when they don't understand that research aims mainly to produce knowledge and may bring them no therapeutic benefit.

If you plan to recruit relatives of team members, or anyone else whose recruitment raises similar concerns, you may need special measures to protect their free decision, their confidentiality and the research's integrity. Ask the REB for guidance on your case.

In long-term care residences, psychiatric units and similar settings, you should recruit in a way that doesn't single people out or make a refusal visible to staff or other residents.

Based on: TCPS 2, articles 3.1 and 3.2, and [chapter 11](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter11-chapitre11.html) (its sections on therapeutic misconception and on clinician-researchers' dual roles, and article 11.5); the MUHC REB's SOP 404.001-2, sections 5.1 and 5.2; [SOP-CR-008, sections 3.1](/sops/cr-008#3-1-requirements) and [3.2](/sops/cr-008#3-2-consent-discussion).

## Keep payment from becoming an inducement

Payment follows the [limits on compensation](/kb/recruitment/consent#compensate-participants-within-the-limits), including what a representative may accept on the participant's behalf. The agencies ask the REB, and you, to be sensitive to participants' economic circumstances. Justify the amount and the method of payment in your submission, giving the amounts, what each one compensates and when it's paid.

Based on: [Civil Code of Québec](https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/CCQ-1991), article 25; TCPS 2, articles 3.1 and 4.7; ICH E6(R3), sections 1.2.8 and 1.2.9; the MUHC REB's SOP 404.001-2, section 5.2.3.

## Plan for emergency research

When an adult suddenly can't consent and the research must start promptly, [the Civil Code sets who may consent for them](/kb/recruitment/consent#who-can-consent). The REB may also allow deferred consent, given afterwards by the participant or their representative, when no one can consent beforehand.

Before you plan for it, ask the REB whether and how deferred consent applies alongside the Civil Code's rule in your study. Where it applies, the REB may allow it only if you submit a satisfactory justification and the REB finds and documents that the conditions in [its standard operating procedure on recruitment and informed consent](https://muhc.ca/cae/muhc-research-ethics-board-reb-standard-operating-procedures-sops) for research in health emergencies are met.

Seek the participant's consent as soon as they regain capacity, or a representative's as soon as one is found, as a condition of continuing.

The REB sets its criteria before the research starts, and enrolment without prior consent should follow the measures in the protocol or another REB-approved document. Describe those measures in detail, for example which procedures will be done before consent, how soon consent will be sought and what happens if it can't be obtained.

Consider added safeguards. For example, you could identify likely participants in advance so that their consent can be sought before an emergency, consult former and prospective participants, or have a data safety monitoring board follow special monitoring procedures.

Based on: Civil Code of Québec, article 21; [TCPS 2, article 3.8](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter3-chapitre3.html#8); the MUHC REB's SOP 404.001-2, section 5.6.2; [SOP-CR-008, section 7.1](/sops/cr-008#7-1-assent).

## Show the REB your safeguards

Describe the safeguards in your first submission; adding them later takes an amendment.

[A new project that could interfere with the integrity of a minor or of an adult who can't consent cannot go through delegated review at its first review](/kb/submission/submission-overview#how-a-study-is-reviewed). For other research, the REB chair or their designee considers the recruitment practices and the participant population, among other things, when deciding whether it is eligible for delegated review.

Your submission should explain:
- If participants who can't consent will take part, why the research question can be answered only with them and, if the research can't benefit them directly, how it exposes them to no more than minimal risk and minimal burden.
- For other participants, how your inclusion and exclusion criteria follow from the research question.
- How you will identify each participant's representative, and obtain and record their consent.
- The protection you planned for each circumstance of vulnerability, including your capacity assessment plan where capacity may be impaired.
- Any monitoring of the consent process you propose.

The consent form should also state, among the risks, any that come from participants' vulnerability.

Based on: the MUHC REB's SOPs 402.001 (section 5.1) and 404.001-2 (section 5.2); TCPS 2, articles 3.9 and 4.6; [SOP-CR-008, section 7.3](/sops/cr-008#7-3-research-risks).

## Keep the safeguards working during the study

For research with more than minimal risk, the REB may require more extensive continuing review, which can include monitoring and reviewing the consent process. It may require continuing review more often than once a year, considering among other things the vulnerability of the population, and it sets the frequency when it first approves the study and may revise that frequency later. It may also impose special precautions.

At renewal, be ready to tell the REB how the safeguards are working, for example how many participants consented through a representative, whether a reassessment changed who consents for anyone, whether anyone objected or withdrew and whether any safeguard was missed.

Where consent is harder to secure freely, for example with residents of an institution or participants whose capacity fluctuates, you may propose that someone not otherwise involved in the study observe the consent discussion, check that it was done properly and, where appropriate, talk with the participant separately. You should record what they observe and resolve any concern they raise before you enrol the participant.

For an amendment, the REB chair or their designee decides whether full-board review is needed, considering the participant population and recruitment practices among other things. Explain why the change is needed and how it affects participants' protections.

A lapse in a safeguard the protocol describes, such as a missed capacity reassessment or consent obtained by someone other than the person the protocol designates, is a protocol deviation. [Record and report it](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/reportable-events#protocol-deviations) according to its severity. [Repeated or deliberate failure to obtain consent properly, or to document it, can lead the REB to suspend or end the study](/kb/recruitment/consent#obtain-consent-before-any-study-procedure).

If your study doesn't fit these steps, ask the REB about consent and review, or the Research Facilitator about RI-MUHC processes.

Based on: the MUHC REB's SOPs 402.001 (section 5.1) and 406.001 (sections 5.1 and 5.2); [TCPS 2, chapter 6](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter6-chapitre6.html), article 6.14; [SOP-CR-026, sections 5.3](/sops/cr-026#5-3-ongoing-study-submission-review-of-an-approved-study) (deviations) and 5.5 (suspension).

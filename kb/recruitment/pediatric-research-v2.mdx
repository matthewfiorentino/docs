The MUHC includes the Montreal Children's Hospital, and much of the RI-MUHC's research involves children. In research that could interfere with a child's integrity, Quebec's Civil Code decides who may consent for them. These rules may differ from those in other provinces.

## Obtain consent and assent for a child

[The person with parental authority, or the tutor, consents for a minor](/kb/recruitment/consent-v2#who-can-consent). A minor aged 14 to 17 may consent alone only if the <Tooltip tip="The ethics committee that reviews and approves research involving human participants. Also called CER at French-language institutions." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#reb">Research Ethics Board (REB)</Tooltip> finds that the research involves only minimal risk and that the circumstances justify it.

<Warning>
A minor who understands the nature and consequences of the research and objects to taking part cannot take part, whoever has consented for them. A child who has some ability to understand the research and dissents, in words or by their behaviour, cannot take part either. Record the objection, and do not perform any study procedure.
</Warning>

Ask the child for their assent as well. In their [*Tri-Council Policy Statement*](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter3-chapitre3.html) (<Tooltip tip="The federal research agencies' policy on the ethical conduct of research involving humans, 2022 edition." headline="TCPS 2" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tcps2">TCPS 2</Tooltip>), the federal research agencies require researchers to learn the wishes of a child who can't yet consent but has some ability to understand the research. The RI-MUHC's standard operating procedures (SOPs) treat obtaining assent as equivalent to obtaining consent, and require it to be documented.

The Centre for Applied Ethics' [pediatric templates](/kb/recruitment/consent-templates) record assent on the consent form's signature page; they have no separate assent form. Quebec law sets no age for assent. Judge each child's ability to understand, considering their maturity and psychological state, and ask the REB when unsure.

All required signatures must be on the current REB-approved consent form before any screening procedure takes place. A procedure done even minutes early is a deviation.

If a minor has been emancipated, ask the REB who may consent.

Based on: [Civil Code of Québec](https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/CCQ-1991), articles 21, 170, 175 and 176; [TCPS 2](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter3-chapitre3.html), articles 3.9 and 3.10; [SOP-CR-008, sections 3.1](/sops/cr-008#3-1-requirements), [3.3](/sops/cr-008#3-3-signatures) and [7.1](/sops/cr-008#7-1-assent); the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) guideline E11(R1), section 2.6.3; the Centre for Applied Ethics' pediatric templates (Sept. 30, 2024).

## Confirm who has parental authority

Both parents exercise parental authority together. When one parent acts alone, the law presumes, with regard to third persons in good faith, that they act with the other's consent. If the protocol or the REB asks for both parents' signatures, get both.

Whoever has custody, one parent or someone else, both parents keep the right to supervise the child's maintenance and education. If one parent dies, is deprived of parental authority by a court or can't express their will, the other parent exercises it. If the child has a tutor, the tutor may consent.

Record who consented and their relationship to the child. When it's unclear who has parental authority, or you know that the parents disagree about the child taking part, ask the REB before you go on.

Based on: Civil Code of Québec, articles 21, 600, 603, 605 and 606; [SOP-CR-008, section 3.1](/sops/cr-008#3-1-requirements).

## Explain the study in words the child understands

How you explain the study depends on the child. Use these ages as rough guides, not cut-offs:
- Around ages 7 and 8, explain what will happen simply and in concrete words, ask whether the child agrees, and record their answer, who explained the study and what was said.
- From around 9 to 12, a short written explanation read through with the child, with pictures where they help, can help the child decide. Aim for about a Grade 3 to 4 reading level.
- From around 13, explain the study as fully as you would to an adult, in words suited to the teenager. Aim for about a Grade 6 to 8 reading level.

A mature 8-year-old may understand a written explanation, and a teenager with a developmental delay may need a spoken one.

Whatever the child's age, the explanation should answer these questions in words they understand:
- What will happen to me? Describe the procedures in concrete words. For example, “We will take a small amount of blood from your arm with a needle” is clearer to a child than “A venipuncture will be performed”.
- How long will it take? Give the number of visits, how long each lasts and the total time in the study.
- Will anything hurt or be uncomfortable? Say so honestly, without playing down the unpleasant parts.
- Can I say no or change my mind? Say that taking part is voluntary, that the child can stop at any time and that saying no won't change their care.

Like all participant-facing material, a written explanation or a visual aid for children needs the REB's approval and, unless the REB accepts a justification, [an English and a French version](/kb/recruitment/consent-v2#prepare-the-consent-form).

Based on: TCPS 2, articles 3.2 and 3.3; ICH E11(R1), section 2.6.3; [SOP-CR-006, section 5.3](/sops/cr-006#5-3-icf-content) (participant materials in English and French).

## Show the REB what it looks for

The MUHC REB's Pediatrics panel reviews all projects involving minors, and [a new project that could interfere with a minor's integrity goes to full-board review at its first review](/kb/submission/submission-overview-v2#how-a-study-is-reviewed). Children must not be inappropriately excluded from research solely because of their age or developmental stage; follow the [rules on including participants fairly](/kb/recruitment/recruitment-enrollment-v2#include-participants-fairly).

Your submission should explain:
- Why the research needs children, and how the risk, given each child's health and condition, is not disproportionate to the benefit that can reasonably be expected.
- If the research is on a group and can't benefit the participating child directly, how it can benefit others of the same age or with the same disease or disability, at no more than minimal risk and burden.
- How you will obtain consent and assent, with the consent form and any written explanation for children attached at the first submission.
- How you will check each child's understanding, and respect and record an objection.
- How you will check at later visits that the child still wants to take part, and act on signs of reluctance or distress.
- If adults also take part, how each group consents.
- For a study of an investigational product, how you will monitor safety in children; for a drug, how you will report adverse events using laboratory values and clinical measurements suited to their age, and watch for effects on growth and development.

[Incomplete submissions are a common cause of delay](/kb/submission/submission-overview-v2#how-a-study-is-reviewed).

Based on: Civil Code of Québec, article 21; TCPS 2, articles 3.3, 3.9, 3.10 and 4.4; the MUHC REB's [SOPs](https://muhc.ca/cae/muhc-research-ethics-board-reb-standard-operating-procedures-sops) 402.001 (section 5.1.2) and 404.001-2 (section 5); ICH E11(R1), section 2.4.3; the Centre for Applied Ethics' [Research Ethics page](https://muhc.ca/cae/research-ethics) (the REB's panels).

## Recruit children

You should approach the parent first. A child should not be recruited directly before a parent has agreed to let the team discuss the study with them. The REB reviews how, when, where and by whom children and families are contacted, and every material you use.

When the child is a patient, first contact with the family should come from someone at the health care facility where the child receives care. A clinician-researcher may approach the families of their own patients directly, but the family should in no way feel pressured or obliged to take part. [The treating physician should not be the one who obtains consent](/kb/recruitment/consent-v2#choose-who-obtains-consent), unless the REB approves.

In schools, daycares and other community settings, children may feel they must take part when a teacher or another adult in charge is involved. You should address recruitment materials to parents, and leave the decision with the family. If a school or daycare hands out your materials, the team should not receive any information that identifies the families who don't respond.

Parents of children with a serious or chronic condition may confuse research with care. Your materials and the consent discussion should make clear what is standard care and what is research, and should not suggest a guaranteed benefit or priority access to care. When the child is a clinician-researcher's own patient, [the REB requires measures to minimize that confusion](/kb/recruitment/consent-v2#choose-who-obtains-consent).

Materials that children see, such as posters in a waiting room, should suit their age and should not use toys, prizes or other rewards to persuade them. Payment for a child's participation follows the [limits on compensation](/kb/recruitment/consent-v2#compensate-participants-within-the-limits), including what a parent or tutor may accept on the child's behalf; a sponsor's global protocol doesn't change Quebec's limits.

Based on: TCPS 2, articles 3.1 and 3.2, and [chapter 11](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter11-chapitre11.html) (the section on clinician-researchers' dual roles, and article 11.5); the MUHC REB's SOP 404.001-2, sections 5.1.2, 5.1.4 and 5.2.3; ICH E11(R1), section 2.6.2; [SOP-CR-008, section 3.1](/sops/cr-008#3-1-requirements) (the treating physician); Civil Code of Québec, article 25.

## Staff the team for children

The principal investigator, who leads the study at the MUHC, is responsible for making sure the team is qualified. Do not perform a study task until your training for it is documented and the task has been delegated on the <Tooltip tip="Lists each team member, their qualifications, and specific trial tasks authorized by the QI." headline="Task Delegation Log (TDL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tdl">Task Delegation Log</Tooltip>.

When the principal investigator delegates obtaining assent, they should delegate it to someone experienced in talking with children of that age. A coordinator who works well with adults may not be the best person to explain a study to a 7-year-old.

For a study of a drug, those conducting it should be properly trained and experienced in studying children, including in evaluating and managing adverse events in children.

Repeated invasive procedures can be painful or frightening for a child. For drug studies, the ICH says that discomfort can be minimized when investigators experienced in treating children design and conduct the study. It lists practical measures that minimize discomfort and distress:
- Staff knowledgeable and skilled in dealing with children and their age-appropriate needs, including in performing pediatric procedures.
- A setting with furniture, play equipment, activities and food suited to the child's age.
- Visits in a place the child knows, such as the hospital or clinic where they receive their care.
- Topical anesthesia before an intravenous line is placed.
- An indwelling catheter for blood sampling, to spare the child repeated needle sticks, and some protocol blood samples taken at the same time as routine ones.

For procedures that may distress a child, such as blood draws, imaging or long visits, consider involving a child life specialist, who prepares children for medical procedures and supports them during and after. No regulation requires it, but it can help the child and the quality of the data.

If your study doesn't fit these steps, ask the Research Facilitator for help.

Based on: ICH E11(R1), sections 2.6.4 and 2.6.5; [SOP-CR-002](/sops/cr-002#task-delegation) (the Task Delegation Log); [SOP-CR-003](/sops/cr-003#documenting-training) (training records); [SOP-CR-008, sections 3.1](/sops/cr-008#3-1-requirements) and [7.1](/sops/cr-008#7-1-assent) (delegating consent and assent).

## Re-consent a participant who turns 18

[A participant who turns 18 during the study signs their own consent as soon as possible](/kb/recruitment/consent-v2#keep-consent-current); the consent of the person with parental authority no longer covers them. The Centre for Applied Ethics' pediatric templates include an addendum for a participant who has become an adult. Record the change in the source documents.

A child who couldn't assent when the study began, such as an infant, must be asked for their assent to keep taking part once they can understand the research's purpose, risks and benefits. The team should plan from the start how it will notice these changes.

Based on: Civil Code of Québec, article 153; TCPS 2, article 3.3; [SOP-CR-008, section 7](/sops/cr-008#special-populations); the Centre for Applied Ethics' pediatric templates (Sept. 30, 2024).

## Follow the pediatric guidelines

Health Canada implemented ICH E11(R1), the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) guideline on clinical investigation of medicinal products in the pediatric population, on April 5, 2018, and ICH E11A, on pediatric extrapolation, on Oct. 17, 2025. E11(R1) covers, among other things, formulations suited to children, endpoints for different ages, ethics in pediatric studies and, in its 2017 addendum, extrapolation, modelling and simulation. Read both if your study tests a drug in children.

The 2025 statements on Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) and the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) each have a 2026 extension for randomized trials with children and adolescents: SPIRIT-Children and Adolescents (SPIRIT-C), for trial protocols, and CONSORT-Children and Adolescents (CONSORT-C), for reports of results. Use each extension alongside its parent statement. The Maternal Infant Child and Youth Research Network (MICYRN), whose members include the RI-MUHC, [encourages researchers to apply the reporting criteria early](https://www.micyrn.ca/posts/spirit-c-2026-consort-c-2026-new-guidelines-for-pediatric-clinical-trials).

Based on: Health Canada's [ICH guidelines page](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/applications-submissions/guidance-documents/international-council-harmonisation/guidelines.html); ICH E11(R1), sections 2.2, 2.4.2 and 2.6, and its addendum, section 5.1; the [SPIRIT-C 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42283642/) and [CONSORT-C 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42283643/) statements.

For a multi-site pediatric study, these Canadian networks may help:

| Network | What it offers |
|---|---|
| [MICYRN](https://www.micyrn.ca/) | A national network of maternal and child health research organizations. It runs KidsCAN Trials. |

Research with First Nations, Inuit and Métis participants or communities follows chapter 9 of the federal research agencies' [*Tri-Council Policy Statement*](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter9-chapitre9.html) (<Tooltip tip="The federal research agencies' policy on the ethical conduct of research involving humans, 2022 edition." headline="TCPS 2" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tcps2">TCPS 2</Tooltip>). The agencies present it as a framework that isn't intended to override or replace the ethical guidance of Indigenous Peoples themselves, and many Nations and communities have their own research codes and review processes.

At the MUHC, research under the auspices of the Cree Board of Health and Social Services of James Bay (CBHSSJB), such as research that uses Cree beneficiary status or identification as a variable, also follows a joint procedure of the CBHSSJB and the MUHC <Tooltip tip="The ethics committee that reviews and approves research involving human participants. Also called CER at French-language institutions." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#reb">Research Ethics Board (REB)</Tooltip>.

## Engage the community when your research may affect it

When your research is likely to affect the welfare of an Indigenous community to which participants belong, you must seek the community's engagement. Engagement is required in these situations, among others:
- Research on First Nations, Inuit or Métis lands.
- Recruitment criteria that include Indigenous identity, for the whole study or a subgroup.
- Research that asks participants about a community's cultural heritage, artefacts, Traditional Knowledge or unique characteristics.
- Analysis that uses Indigenous identity, or membership in an Indigenous community, as a variable.
- Interpretation of results that will refer to Indigenous communities, Peoples, languages, history or culture.

You and the community decide together what form the engagement takes, to suit the community and the research. Learn the customs and codes of research practice of each community your research affects, and respect them, even where none is written down. Where they differ from TCPS 2, address the difference before the research starts, or as it arises.

Building relationships, clarifying a project's goals and negotiating agreements take substantial time and resources, for the community and for you. Build the costs into your funding proposal as far as the funder's rules allow.

On lands under a First Nations, Inuit or Métis authority, seek the engagement of the community's leaders. If you propose an alternative to the formal leaders' agreement, you should engage community processes and document the steps you take, so that the REB can review them. In engaging the community, you should also take into account, as far as possible, the views of all its relevant sectors, including people and groups who may not have a voice in its formal leadership.

The REB and any community body the authority recognizes must both review the research before you recruit anyone, and a community's review doesn't replace the REB's. Neither does a community's agreement replace [the consent of each participant](/kb/recruitment/consent).

In your REB submission, explain how you have engaged, or will engage, the community, with evidence such as a research agreement, a record of the community's decision or a summary of advice from a culturally informed advisory group. If you propose no engagement, ask the REB to approve an exception, and give it your reasons.

Engagement isn't required when Indigenous participation is incidental, for example in a study of treatments for high blood pressure among hospital outpatients that isn't designed to collect Indigenous-specific data. If a participant tells you they are Indigenous while you collect data, ask whether they would like culturally appropriate help to understand the research or take part in it.

Research that uses only publicly available information doesn't need engagement, but you should seek culturally informed advice before you use it, to judge whether harms may result. Anonymous data that can't be linked to a specific community don't call for engagement either. Research that critically examines public institutions, or First Nations, Inuit or Métis governments and organizations, may proceed without the usual engagement of community leaders.

Based on: [TCPS 2, chapter 9](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter9-chapitre9.html), articles 9.1 to 9.3, 9.5 to 9.11, 9.21 and 9.22.

## Set out the research in an agreement with the community

Where a community has formally engaged with you through a designated representative, you should set out both sides' terms and commitments in a research agreement. Where an agreement applies, it must come before you recruit anyone or collect or access data. Consider a collaborative and participatory approach, suited to the research and to the level of engagement the community wants.

An agreement typically sets out the purpose of the research and each side's responsibilities, for example for data collection and management, interpretation, co-authorship and dissemination; the federal research agencies list its other usual contents in [chapter 9 of TCPS 2](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter9-chapitre9.html#11).

The agreement must address any rights of people and communities in biological materials and associated data, and whether personal information will be shared with community partners. Do not share a participant's personal information with community partners without their consent.

Tell prospective participants how far the research is a collaboration with a community organization, including how data will be shared, when they first consent and as the research goes on.

Each participant consents before you collect or access their data or biological materials. If signed written consent isn't culturally appropriate, tell the REB how you will seek and record consent instead. In Quebec, [consent to research that could interfere with a person's integrity is given in writing, unless the REB finds that the circumstances justify another way](/kb/recruitment/consent#obtain-consent-before-any-study-procedure).

If a participant's first language is an Indigenous language, you should offer translation by a knowledgeable person during the consent process, and during the research as the participant wishes. Inuit languages have official status in Inuit regions.

Plan for these as well:
- Benefits to the community, such as training, local hiring, recognition of contributors and the return of results, where the engagement and the research allow.
- A chance for the community's representatives to take part in interpreting the data and reviewing the findings before the final report and publications. If you can't agree on an interpretation, give the community a chance to make its views known, or report the disagreement.
- The final reports, which must be made available to the community.
- Intellectual property, discussed with the community and the institution and specified in the agreement, as appropriate, before the research begins.

Taking part in interpretation doesn't give a community the right to block publication; it gives the community the opportunity to put the findings in context. A community may ask more, as the CBHSSJB does.

If you plan to reuse data or biological materials identifiable as coming from an Indigenous community, the REB must review the project. Unless the data are publicly available or legally accessible, you must also engage the community before you start where there's no research agreement, or where neither the agreement nor the participants' original consent covers the reuse.

Each participant's consent to the reuse of identifiable information is required unless the REB agrees that an exception applies. Linking anonymous data sets also needs REB review when there's a reasonable prospect that it could produce information identifiable as coming from a specific Indigenous community or a segment of the Indigenous community at large.

Based on: TCPS 2, articles 9.4, 9.11 to 9.13 and 9.16 to 9.22; [Civil Code of Québec](https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/CCQ-1991), article 24.

## Follow the joint review with the CBHSSJB

The MUHC REB and the CBHSSJB's Research Office wrote a joint procedure for this research, the REB's standard operating procedure (SOP) 410.001, *Research with Eeyou/Eenou (Cree) Communities*, in effect since Jan. 23, 2025.

The procedure applies to research under the CBHSSJB's auspices, including any project that plans to use, as a variable at any stage of the research, Cree beneficiary status under the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement, Cree of Northern Québec identification or a catchment area that includes Quebec's health and social services region 18.

At the CBHSSJB's request, the MUHC REB acts for the time being as the CBHSSJB's REB of record for multicentre research under the CBHSSJB's auspices (as of November 2026).

You must contact the CBHSSJB's Research Office before you develop a project in any of these cases:
- You plan to recruit Eeyou/Eenou participants, wherever they live, in Eeyou Istchee or elsewhere.
- You want to use the CBHSSJB's facilities, resources or staff.
- You want to use its databases, medical records or other confidential patient information.
- You work for, or are affiliated with, the CBHSSJB.

The Research Office also answers questions about whether a project falls within the procedure. The CBHSSJB approves only participatory, community-based research projects.

The REB and the CBHSSJB set these steps, in order, before you submit to the REB:
1. Engage with the CBHSSJB before you take any step to create the project; the Research Office and you must agree on an initial outline. The project must align with the CBHSSJB's research priorities or be developed with it.
2. Send a preliminary proposal on the Research Office's briefing sheet before you write the full protocol, and present it when the CBHSSJB invites you.
3. If the proposal is found adequate, the Research Office may give you a Letter of Support for funding applications. It isn't an authorization, and you should not apply for funding before you have it.
4. Develop the full proposal with the input of the people the CBHSSJB identifies, send the Research Office the documents on its checklist (final protocol, consent forms, data collection forms, a collaboration plan with the CBHSSJB and the community, a knowledge translation plan and a signed Researcher Status Form), and present the full proposal when the CBHSSJB invites you.
5. The CBHSSJB reviews the project's cultural safety and its consistency with the Miyupimaatisiiun Research Principles, and its suitability. If both are satisfactory, the Research Office issues a Letter of Satisfactory Review.

Then follow these steps through REB review to the start of the research:
1. Submit to the REB in Nagano with the Letter of Satisfactory Review, the CBHSSJB's reviews and your responses, and the knowledge translation plan the CBHSSJB reviewed.
2. The REB reviews the project at a full-board meeting, possibly after a pre-review. A project the REB finds to be minimal risk is eligible for delegated review, with input whenever possible from the CBHSSJB or an Eeyou/Eenou community member it designates.
3. Sign the research agreement with the CBHSSJB, with the REB-approved protocol and approval letter attached. You must conclude it before the project begins, as a condition of the CBHSSJB's authorization.
4. Do not start any research activity, including recruitment, data collection or analysis, until you have both REB approval and MUHC Authorization. At a CBHSSJB site, also wait for the CBHSSJB's own authorization before any recruitment, data collection, access to data or collection of biological materials.

To approve the research, the REB also assesses whether:
- Data are collected, used, kept and managed according to the First Nations principles of ownership, control, access and possession (OCAP®) and the CBHSSJB's research agreement, including the return of results. OCAP® is a registered trademark of the [First Nations Information Governance Centre (FNIGC)](https://fnigc.ca/ocap-training/).
- The methods follow participatory, collaborative research practice and integrate Indigenous ways of knowing wherever appropriate. Where they can't, you must justify it.
- Participant-facing documents and recruitment materials, social media included, are in plain English, with Cree translations where appropriate, arranged with the CBHSSJB's guidance.
- Compensation suits local conditions, such as the cost of living, and can be used where participants live. Gift cards, for example, should be for businesses they can reach. [The limits on compensation](/kb/recruitment/consent#compensate-participants-within-the-limits) still apply.

Ask the REB whether your study also needs French versions of its documents.

Everyone on the research team, students and research assistants included, must give a written statement that they will respect the CBHSSJB's [Miyupimaatisiiun Research Principles](https://www.creehealth.org/about-us/departments/research-office/research-principles). The CBHSSJB asks that research be governed by the Eeyou/Eenou values of truth, respect and reciprocity. The principles include the following:
- Results must be interpreted with Eeyou/Eenou input through the CBHSSJB before publication, and its publication review processes must be followed.
- Any interpretation of data and any creation, including intellectual property, developed in the project is jointly owned by the CBHSSJB and the researcher.
- Consent forms should be available in the Eeyou language, îyiyû ayimuwin.
- The CBHSSJB may withdraw from a project at any time, after which the research, and the use of Eeyou/Eenou data and of any results created, must stop unless the CBHSSJB decides otherwise.

Review times vary; start early. For a student's project, the CBHSSJB's approval steps apply, with the necessary adaptations, to the supervisor. When the REB renews its approval, the CBHSSJB's authorization is renewed too, unless the CBHSSJB gives written notice.

Based on: the MUHC REB's [SOP 410.001](https://muhc.ca/cae/muhc-research-ethics-board-reb-standard-operating-procedures-sops), sections 2 to 6; the CBHSSJB's [Information for Researchers](https://www.creehealth.org/about-us/departments/research-office/info-researchers) (updated March 18, 2026) and its Miyupimaatisiiun Research Principles.

## Respect the data governance of each People

Data governance differs between First Nations, Inuit and Métis Peoples.

For research with First Nations, OCAP® asserts that First Nations alone have control over data collection processes in their communities, and that they own and control how this information can be stored, interpreted, used or shared:
- **Ownership.** A community or group owns its information collectively, as a person owns their personal information.
- **Control.** First Nations, their communities and representative bodies are within their rights to seek control over every part of research and information management that affects them.
- **Access.** First Nations must have access to information and data about themselves and their communities, wherever it is held, and First Nations communities and organizations have the right to manage and make decisions about access to their collective information.
- **Possession.** Physical control of the data is the mechanism by which ownership can be asserted and protected.

Each First Nations community or region may interpret OCAP® in its own way. FNIGC asks anyone planning research with a First Nation to acquaint themselves with OCAP® before they begin, and the RI-MUHC's SOPs ask for a basic understanding of it. FNIGC's online course, [The Fundamentals of OCAP®](https://fnigc.ca/ocap-training/), is a place to start.

<Warning>
OCAP® is a set of specifically First Nations principles. Do not apply it to Inuit or Métis data, or to Indigenous data in general; follow the governance each community sets.
</Warning>

For research with Inuit, Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami (ITK), the national representative organization for Inuit, launched the [National Inuit Strategy on Research](https://itk.ca/newsreleasenisr/) (NISR) in 2018. ITK asks governments and researchers to respect Inuit self-determination in research, and one of the NISR's five priority areas is to “Ensure Inuit access, ownership, and control over data and information”.

For research with Métis participants or communities, the NISR doesn't apply either, and Métis governance is regional.

Based on: FNIGC's [OCAP® page](https://fnigc.ca/ocap-training/) and its answers to frequent questions; [SOP-CR-006, section 5.6](/sops/cr-006#5-6-cultural-considerations); TCPS 2, article 9.8; ITK's [news release on the NISR](https://itk.ca/newsreleasenisr/) (March 22, 2018); the Métis National Council's [governance page](https://www.metisnation.ca/about/governance) (all read Nov. 10 and 11, 2026).

## Find the right contact in Quebec

Before you contact a community or the REB, write first to the RI-MUHC's equity, diversity and inclusion (EDI) team, at [edi.ri@muhc.mcgill.ca](mailto:edi.ri@muhc.mcgill.ca): it helps you find the right contact and Indigenous-led resources. Each community normally decides for itself whether to take part. Regional and national organizations may help, but they don't take that decision away from the community.

For research with First Nations in Quebec, the Assembly of First Nations Quebec-Labrador (AFNQL) has published the [*First Nations in Quebec and Labrador's Research Protocol*](https://apnql.com/en/research-protocol/) (2014), with a companion guide for researchers (2019).

The AFNQL asks that first contact take the form of a letter of intent to the community's authorities, asking for a meeting to discuss the research. Some First Nations issue their own research ethics certificates, Kahnawà:ke among them. Where a First Nation or a regional First Nations organization issues one, the AFNQL asks that it come before your institution's. Ask the community which rules apply.

These organizations and offices can help in Quebec:
- The First Nations of Quebec and Labrador Health and Social Services Commission ([FNQLHSSC](https://cssspnql.com/en/introduction-and-background/)), created by the AFNQL's Chiefs in 1994, provides technical support for research at the request of First Nations communities. It offers, on request, a workshop on the AFNQL's research protocol and OCAP®. Engaging it doesn't replace engaging each community.
- [Tahatikonhsontóntie'](https://errsaqc-qcneihr.ca/en/), the Quebec Network Environment for Indigenous Health Research (QcNEIHR), is a community-based health research network for Indigenous communities and organizations in Quebec. The Kahnawà:ke Schools Diabetes Prevention Program hosts it, in Kanien'kehá:ka (Mohawk) territory of Kahnawà:ke. It offers resources on research ethics and on managing research data in Indigenous contexts. It is one of the centres of a program of the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR), the [Network Environments for Indigenous Health Research](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/54200.html) (NEIHR).
- The RI-MUHC's Equity, Diversity and Inclusion (EDI) Specialists give guidance on research with Indigenous communities at the RI-MUHC. Ask them which Métis body, if any, to engage for your study.

In Nunavik, the first step for a health and wellness research project is to submit it to the Nunavik Regional Board of Health and Social Services (NRBHSS) for evaluation. The Nunavik Nutrition and Health Committee, an advisory committee to the board's Director of Public Health, serves as a liaison between researchers and Nunavik communities and is the regional ethics committee for research on health, nutrition and contaminants.

Nunavik is the only region of Inuit Nunangat without a centralized process to review, approve and monitor research. Atanniuvik, a research management organization, is being established by regional organizations including Makivvik, the Kativik Regional Government and the NRBHSS; until it operates, follow the existing regional and community processes (as of November 2026).

For research with Eeyou/Eenou participants, start with the [joint review with the CBHSSJB](#follow-the-joint-review-with-the-cbhssjb).

If your study doesn't fit these steps, ask the EDI Specialists or the REB.

Research involving Indigenous peoples in Canada is governed by specific ethical requirements that go beyond standard research ethics review. [TCPS2 (2022) Chapter 9](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter9-chapitre9.html) establishes the framework; community-specific principles and agreements add further obligations. This page covers the requirements that apply at the RI-MUHC.

## TCPS2 Chapter 9

Chapter 9 of the Tri-Council Policy Statement (2022) applies to all research funded by CIHR, NSERC, or SSHRC — and by extension to all research conducted at institutions that receive Tri-Council funding, including the RI-MUHC. It applies when:

- The research involves **Indigenous peoples, communities, or organizations** as participants
- The research involves **Indigenous identity, language, culture, or knowledge** as subject matter
- The research uses **data or biological materials** identifiable as originating from Indigenous peoples

### Key principles

- **Community engagement** is required before, during, and after research. Researchers must engage with relevant community leaders or organizations before submitting for ethics review.
- **Research agreements** between researchers and communities should address data ownership, community review of findings, dissemination plans, intellectual property, and community benefit.
- **Respect for community customs and codes of research practice** — communities may have their own research review processes that must be completed in addition to institutional REB review.
- **Benefit sharing** — research should produce outcomes that benefit the participating community, not only the researcher or institution.

## OCAP® principles

OCAP® stands for **Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession**. It is a registered trademark of the [**First Nations Information Governance Centre (FNIGC)**](https://fnigc.ca) and applies specifically to research involving First Nations peoples and communities.

| Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Ownership** | A First Nations community collectively owns its cultural knowledge, data, and information |
| **Control** | First Nations communities have the right to control all aspects of research and information management affecting them |
| **Access** | First Nations communities must have access to data and information about themselves, and be involved in decisions about who else may access it |
| **Possession** | Physical control of data should be with the First Nation or an Indigenous-controlled custodian |

<Warning>
**OCAP® is First Nations-specific.** It does not apply to Inuit or Métis peoples, who have developed their own data governance frameworks. Applying OCAP® broadly to all Indigenous peoples is a common error. Use the framework appropriate to the community you are working with.
</Warning>

FNIGC offers an [**OCAP® Fundamentals course**](https://fnigc.ca/ocap-training/) that researchers should complete before undertaking research involving First Nations data or communities.

## Inuit-specific considerations

Research involving Inuit communities in Quebec (Nunavik) follows the [**National Inuit Strategy on Research (NISR)**](https://www.itk.ca/national-inuit-strategy-on-research/), published by Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami (ITK). The NISR emphasizes Inuit self-determination in research, Inuit access and ownership of data, and alignment of research with Inuit priorities.

For research involving Nunavik communities specifically, researchers should consult with:

- The **Nunavik Research Centre** (operated by Makivik Corporation)
- The **Kativik Regional Government**
- The **Nunavik Nutrition and Health Committee** (for health-related research)

Unlike Nunavut (which has a formal research licensing process through the Nunavut Research Institute), Nunavik does not have a centralized research licensing body. Community-level engagement and consent are the primary mechanisms.

## Research involving the Cree Community

The RI-MUHC has established specific procedures for research involving the Cree community. The MUHC REB has a specific SOP — [410.001 Research with Eeyou/Eenou (Cree) Communities](https://muhc.ca/sites/default/files/users/user237/SOP_EN_Research%20CBHSSJB-MUHC_2024-11-06_FINAL.pdf) — governing the review of projects that involve the Cree Community, developed in partnership with the [Cree Board of Health and Social Services of James Bay (CBHSSJB)](https://www.creehealth.org).

Under these procedures, research involving the Cree community requires review and approval by the CBHSSJB in addition to MUHC REB approval. The CBHSSJB's research review process is guided by the **Miyupimaatisiiun Research Principles**, which emphasize reciprocal relationships between researchers and community members.

<Note>
Contact the MUHC REB early if you are planning research that involves any Indigenous community. The community engagement and review processes take time, and they must be initiated before — not after — the REB submission.
</Note>

## Practical requirements for researchers

1. **Engage the community first.** Before developing a protocol, establish contact with relevant community organizations. Community engagement is a prerequisite for REB submission, not a step that happens after approval.
2. **Develop a research agreement.** A written agreement between the researcher and the community covering data governance, intellectual property, benefit sharing, and dissemination should be in place before the study begins.
3. **Plan for community review of results.** Communities should have the opportunity to review findings before publication — both to verify accuracy and to assess potential community impact.
4. **Budget for engagement.** Community engagement, travel, translation, and benefit-sharing activities should be included in the study budget.
5. **Complete relevant training.** FNIGC's OCAP® Fundamentals course (for First Nations research) and the [TCPS2 Chapter 9 tutorial](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter9-chapitre9.html) are recommended before beginning any research involving Indigenous communities.

## Quebec First Nations pathways — FNQLHSSC

For research involving First Nations communities in Quebec (outside the Cree community, which has its own structure described above), researchers should engage the [**First Nations of Quebec and Labrador Health and Social Services Commission (FNQLHSSC / CSSSPNQL)**](https://cssspnql.com/en/).

The FNQLHSSC represents the health and social services interests of First Nations in Quebec and Labrador and advises communities on research partnerships. It can help identify the appropriate community research contact, advise on community-specific research protocols, and support data governance planning for research that crosses multiple First Nations communities in the province.

<Note>
Community governance structures vary significantly across First Nations in Quebec. The FNQLHSSC does not provide a centralized ethics review but can guide researchers toward the appropriate community review process. Engagement with FNQLHSSC does not substitute for direct community engagement.
</Note>

## Métis-specific considerations

Métis peoples have distinct cultural identities, governance structures, and research ethics perspectives that are separate from First Nations and Inuit frameworks. OCAP® is First Nations-specific and does not apply to Métis communities. Inuit frameworks (NISR) do not apply either.

For research involving Métis communities in Quebec or eastern Canada, researchers should:

- Contact the **Métis Nation of Ontario** or the relevant regional Métis authority for consultation on community-specific research protocols — there is no single national Métis research ethics body, and governance is regional
- Recognize that Métis data sovereignty principles are actively evolving; research agreements should be developed in consultation with the specific community rather than by analogy to First Nations frameworks
- Review the **CIHR Institute of Aboriginal Peoples' Health (IAPH)** guidance on Métis-specific health research, which provides context on historical and contemporary data access issues

## NEIHR — national research network

The [**Network Environments for Indigenous Health Research (NEIHR)**](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/51161.html) is a CIHR-funded national network of regional centres that support community-led Indigenous health research. NEIHR centres are embedded within Indigenous communities and partner institutions across Canada, and they can:

- Provide co-investigator capacity and community navigation for researchers planning Indigenous-focused studies
- Support TCPS2 Chapter 9 compliance, particularly for the community engagement and research agreement requirements
- Connect researchers with community-owned data infrastructure and Indigenous data governance expertise

Contact the [Research Facilitator](mailto:Research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca) for the NEIHR centre most relevant to a given research question — the relevant centre depends on the specific community and region involved.

## IRLET and PORLET engagement tools

The [**SCPOR**](https://www.scpor.ca/porlet20) (Saskatchewan Centre for Patient-Oriented Research) has developed the Indigenous Research Level of Engagement Tool (**IRLET**), the Patient-Oriented Research Level of Engagement Tool (**PORLET**), and the Partner as Indigenous Research Level of Engagement Tool (**PaIRLET**). These tools help research teams:

- Define and document the intended level of engagement with Indigenous communities at each stage of a research project
- Assess whether the planned engagement aligns with TCPS2 Chapter 9 expectations and community expectations
- Create a shared understanding between researchers and community partners about roles, responsibilities, and decision-making authority

IRLET distinguishes between consultation, collaboration, and community leadership models — a useful framework when designing the research agreement described in the practical requirements section above. These tools are freely available through SCPOR's website.

## Decolonizing approaches in research design

TCPS2 Chapter 9 and CIHR's Indigenous health research strategy increasingly recognize that conventional research methods — developed primarily in Western academic contexts — may embed assumptions that do not align with Indigenous worldviews or research priorities. "Decolonizing" research refers to approaches that actively work against these embedded assumptions.

In practice, this involves:

- **Community-driven research questions** — the question originates from a community need, not a researcher's grant agenda. Community members participate in framing the question and the outcome measures.
- **Indigenous methodologies** — oral traditions, talking circles, land-based methods, and narrative inquiry may be more appropriate than surveys and structured interviews for certain research questions. The choice of method should be culturally appropriate, not simply convenient.
- **Indigenous data sovereignty** — data collected from or about Indigenous communities are governed by the community, not by the institution. Research agreements should specify who controls the data, who can access it, and what happens to it after the study ends.
- **Reciprocity** — research should return value to the community in forms the community defines as meaningful: reports in community languages, community presentations, policy advocacy, training opportunities, or authorship of community members.

These are not add-ons to a conventional protocol. They are design elements that should be addressed before the study is conceived, not after REB submission. Researchers at early stages of engagement with Indigenous health research are encouraged to consult with a NEIHR centre and to review the CIHR resources on [community-based participatory research](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/29418.html).

Research with First Nations, Inuit and Métis participants or communities follows chapter 9 of the federal research agencies' [*Tri-Council Policy Statement*](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter9-chapitre9.html) (<Tooltip tip="The federal research agencies' policy on the ethical conduct of research involving humans, 2022 edition." headline="TCPS 2" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tcps2">TCPS 2</Tooltip>). The chapter also covers the reuse of data and biological materials identifiable as coming from an Indigenous community or People. The agencies present the chapter as a framework that isn't intended to override or replace the ethical guidance of Indigenous Peoples themselves. Many Nations and communities have their own research codes and review processes.

These bodies set the rules for this research:

## Engage the community when your research may affect it

When your research is likely to affect the welfare of an Indigenous community to which participants belong, you [must seek the community's engagement](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter9-chapitre9.html#1). Engagement is required in these situations, among others:
- Research on First Nations, Inuit or Métis lands.
- Recruitment criteria that include Indigenous identity, for the whole study or a subgroup.
- Research that asks participants about a community's cultural heritage, artefacts, Traditional Knowledge or unique characteristics.
- Analysis that uses Indigenous identity, or membership in an Indigenous community, as a variable.
- Interpretation of results that will refer to Indigenous communities, Peoples, languages, history or culture.

Some research doesn't call for community engagement:
- Research in which Indigenous participation is incidental, for example a study of treatments for high blood pressure among hospital outpatients that isn't designed to collect Indigenous-specific data.
- Research that uses only publicly available information.
- Research that uses only anonymous data that can't be linked to a specific community.

In research where Indigenous participation is incidental, if a participant tells you they are Indigenous while you collect data, you should ask whether they would like culturally appropriate help to understand the research or take part in it. Before using publicly available information, researchers should still seek culturally informed advice, to judge whether harms may result.

Research may proceed without the usual engagement of community leaders when it critically examines the conduct of public institutions, First Nations, Inuit or Métis governments, institutions or organizations, or persons exercising authority over First Nations, Inuit or Métis individuals.

For community engagement and collaboration, researchers and the REB must recognize Indigenous organizations and communities of interest as communities. The organizations include First Nations, Inuit and Métis representative bodies and service organizations.

Decide together with the community what form the engagement takes, to suit the community and the research. Learn the customs and codes of research practice of each community your research affects, and respect them, even where none is written down. Where a community's customs or codes differ from TCPS 2, the differences should be identified and addressed before the research starts, or as they arise.

Building relationships, clarifying a project's goals and negotiating agreements take substantial time and resources, for the community and for you. The community's costs and your own should be built into your funding proposal, as far as the funder's rules allow.

On lands under a First Nations, Inuit or Métis authority, seek the engagement of the community's leaders. For research on First Nations, Inuit or Métis lands or in an organizational community, if you propose an alternative to the formal leaders' agreement, you should engage community processes and document the steps you take, so that the REB can review them.

The REB and any community body the authority recognizes must both review the research before you recruit anyone. A community's review doesn't replace the REB's. A community's agreement doesn't replace [the consent of each participant](/kb/recruitment/consent).

In engaging a territorial or organizational community, researchers should take into account the views of all its relevant sectors as far as possible, including people and groups who may not have a voice in its formal leadership.

In your REB submission, explain how you have engaged or will engage the community. Give evidence in one or more of these forms: a research agreement, a record of the community's decision or a written summary of advice from a culturally informed advisory group.

If you propose no engagement, seek the REB's approval for an exception, and give the REB your reasons. If a community signals in preliminary discussions before REB review that the research may proceed without further engagement, document the steps you took to invite and facilitate its engagement and present them to the REB.

## Set out the research in an agreement with the community

Where a community has formally engaged with you through a designated representative, both sides' terms and commitments should be set out in a research agreement. Where an agreement applies, it must come before you recruit anyone or collect or access data. Researchers and communities should consider a collaborative and participatory approach, suited to the research and to the level of engagement the community wants.

[An agreement typically sets out](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter9-chapitre9.html#11) the purpose of the research and each side's responsibilities, for example for data collection and management, interpretation, co-authorship and dissemination.

With your community partners, address privacy and confidentiality for communities and individuals early in the community engagement process. The agreement must address whether personal information will be shared with community partners. Do not share a participant's personal information with community partners without the participant's consent. Where the agreement gives community partners limited or full access to identifiable personal data, participants' consent to that disclosure must form part of the consent process.

The agreement must also address any rights of people and communities in biological materials and associated data.

As part of the initial and ongoing consent process, tell prospective participants how far the research is a collaboration with an organizational community or a community of interest, including how data will be shared.

Each participant's consent must come before you collect or access their data or biological materials. Where a research agreement exists, consent must be given in accordance with it.

In Quebec, [consent to research that could interfere with a person's integrity is given in writing, unless the REB finds that the circumstances justify another way](/kb/recruitment/consent#obtain-consent-before-any-study-procedure). If signed written consent isn't culturally appropriate, tell the REB how you will seek and record consent instead.

If a participant's first language is an Indigenous language, you should offer translation by a knowledgeable person during the consent process, and during the research as the participant wishes. Researchers should be aware of the official status of Inuit languages in Inuit regions.

Researchers should also:
- Make the research relevant to the community's needs and priorities, where the form of community engagement and the nature of the research make it possible.
- Benefit the community, for example through training, local hiring, recognition of contributors and the return of results.
- Support capacity building by enhancing the skills of community personnel in research methods, project management, and ethical review and oversight.
- Engage the community in identifying Elders or other recognized knowledge holders to take part in designing and conducting the research, and in interpreting the findings in the context of cultural norms and Traditional Knowledge, and seek the community's advice on appropriate recognition for the unique advisory role of these persons.
- In collaborative research, give community representatives an opportunity to take part in interpreting the data and reviewing the findings before the final report is completed and publications are finalized.
- If a disagreement about the interpretation can't be resolved, either give the community an opportunity to make its views known, or accurately report the disagreement in the reports or publications.
- In collaborative research, discuss intellectual property rights with the community and the institution, and specify them in the research agreement, as appropriate, before the research is conducted.

Taking part in interpretation doesn't give a community the right to block publication. It gives the community the opportunity to put the findings in context. A community may require more, as [the CBHSSJB does](#follow-the-joint-review-with-the-cbhssjb).

Make the final reports available to the participating community.

If you plan to reuse data or biological materials identifiable as coming from an Indigenous community, [the REB must review the project](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter9-chapitre9.html#20). For the reuse of data, address any risk of identifying a community inadvertently or of misusing Traditional Knowledge, through community engagement as appropriate. Unless the data are publicly available or legally accessible, you must also engage the community before you start any reuse of the data or biological materials in either of these cases:
- There is no research agreement.
- The agreement doesn't address the reuse, and the participants' original consent doesn't authorize it.

Each participant's consent to the reuse of identifiable information is required unless the REB agrees that an exception applies. Linking anonymous data sets also needs REB review when there's a reasonable prospect that it could produce information identifiable as coming from a specific Indigenous community or a segment of the Indigenous community at large.

## Respect the data governance of each People

Data governance differs between First Nations, Inuit and Métis Peoples.

For research they fund, the [federal research agencies](https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/interagency-research-funding/policies-and-guidelines/research-data-management/tri-agency-research-data-management-policy) require that a data management plan for research conducted by and with First Nations, Métis and Inuit communities, collectives and organizations be co-developed with those communities, collectives and organizations, in accordance with research data management principles or plan formats that they accept. For research with First Nations, [the First Nations principles of ownership, control, access and possession (OCAP®)](https://fnigc.ca/ocap-training/) assert that First Nations alone have control over data collection processes in their communities, and that they own and control how this information can be stored, interpreted, used or shared:
- **Ownership.** A community or group owns its information collectively, as a person owns their personal information.
- **Control.** First Nations, their communities and representative bodies are within their rights to seek control over every part of research and information management that affects them.
- **Access.** First Nations must have access to information and data about themselves and their communities, wherever it is held. First Nations communities and organizations have the right to manage and make decisions about access to their collective information.
- **Possession.** Physical control of the data is the mechanism by which ownership can be asserted and protected.

OCAP® is a registered trademark of the First Nations Information Governance Centre (FNIGC). Each First Nations community or region may interpret OCAP® in its own way. FNIGC advises that anyone interested in conducting research with a First Nation should acquaint themselves with OCAP® before they begin. At the RI-MUHC, anyone considering such research [should have a basic understanding of it](/sops/cr-006#5-6-2). FNIGC's online course, [The Fundamentals of OCAP®](https://fnigc.ca/ocap-training/), is a place to start.

<Warning>
OCAP® is a set of specifically First Nations principles. Do not apply it to Inuit or Métis data, or to Indigenous data in general. Follow the governance each community sets.
</Warning>

Each community normally decides for itself whether to take part. Regional and national organizations may help, but they don't take that decision away from the community.

For research with First Nations in Quebec, the Assembly of First Nations Quebec-Labrador (AFNQL) has published the [*First Nations in Quebec and Labrador's Research Protocol*](https://apnql.com/en/research-protocol/) (2014), with a companion guide for researchers (2019). First contact should take the form of a letter of intent asking for a meeting with the local First Nation authorities to discuss the research's objectives. Send the letter to the appropriate First Nations authorities. Some First Nations issue their own research ethics certificates, Kahnawà:ke among them. Where a First Nation or a regional First Nations organization issues one, it should come before your institution's. Ask the community which rules apply.

These organizations and offices can help in Quebec:
- The First Nations of Quebec and Labrador Health and Social Services Commission ([FNQLHSSC](https://cssspnql.com/en/introduction-and-background/)), created by the AFNQL's Chiefs in 1994, provides technical support for research at the request of First Nations communities. On request, it offers [a workshop on the AFNQL's research protocol and OCAP®](https://cssspnql.com/en/evenement/getting-familiar-with-the-first-nations-in-quebec-and-labradors-research-protocol-2/). Engaging it doesn't replace engaging each community.
- [Tahatikonhsontóntie'](https://errsaqc-qcneihr.ca/en/), the Quebec Network Environment for Indigenous Health Research (QcNEIHR), is a community-based health research network for Indigenous communities and organizations in Quebec. The Kahnawà:ke Schools Diabetes Prevention Program hosts it, in Kanien'kehá:ka (Mohawk) territory of Kahnawà:ke. The network offers resources on research ethics and on managing research data in Indigenous contexts. It is one of the centres of a program of the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR), the [Network Environments for Indigenous Health Research](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/54200.html) (NEIHR).

In Nunavik, the first step for a health and wellness research project is to [submit it to the Nunavik Regional Board of Health and Social Services](https://nrbhss.ca/en/nrbhss/public-health/nunavik-health-surveys/conducting-health-and-wellness-research-project-nunavik) (NRBHSS) for evaluation. The [Nunavik Nutrition and Health Committee](https://nrbhss.ca/en/departments/public-health) is an advisory committee to the board's Director of Public Health. The committee serves as a liaison between researchers and Nunavik communities and is the regional ethics committee for research on health, nutrition and contaminants.

Nunavik is the only region of Inuit Nunangat without a centralized process to review, approve and monitor research. [Atanniuvik](https://atanniuvik.ca/background/), a research management organization, is being established by regional organizations including Makivvik, the Kativik Regional Government and the NRBHSS. ## Follow the joint review with the CBHSSJB

At the MUHC, research under the CBHSSJB's auspices also follows a joint procedure of the CBHSSJB and the MUHC REB, [the REB's standard operating procedure (SOP) 410.001](https://muhc.ca/cae/muhc-research-ethics-board-reb-standard-operating-procedures-sops), *Research with Eeyou/Eenou (Cree) Communities*. At the CBHSSJB's request, the MUHC REB acts for the time being as the CBHSSJB's REB of record for multicentre research under the CBHSSJB's auspices.

### Check whether the procedure applies

Research under the CBHSSJB's auspices includes any project that plans to use any of the following as a variable at any stage of the research, from eligibility screening and recruitment to data analysis and publication:
- Cree beneficiary status under the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement.
- Cree of Northern Québec identification.
- A catchment area, such as postal codes, that includes Eeyou Istchee, Quebec's health and social services region 18.

If you aren't sure whether your project is covered, ask the CBHSSJB's [Research Office](mailto:18tcr.research.committee@ssss.gouv.qc.ca).

A quality assurance or quality improvement (QA/QI) project involving Eeyou/Eenou (Cree) populations must also be declared to the CBHSSJB. Start the declaration yourself by writing to its [Research Office](mailto:18tcr.research.committee@ssss.gouv.qc.ca).{/* Sources: the Centre for Applied Ethics' Research versus QA/QI Screening Tool (2025-05-16), question 13 c): "QA/QI projects involving Eeyou/Eenou (Cree) populations must be declared to the Cree Board of Health and Social Services of James Bay (CBHSSJB)." and "You must initiate this process" directly. Moved here from Is It Research? (b146; MF, ledger row 209: Indigenous content kept on this page). Awaits the EDI team's Indigenous partners' review (STYLE-HUB G1). */}

### Contact the CBHSSJB Research Office before you develop a project

Contact the CBHSSJB's [Research Office](mailto:18tcr.research.committee@ssss.gouv.qc.ca) [before you develop a project](https://www.creehealth.org/about-us/departments/research-office/info-researchers) in any of these cases:
- You plan to recruit Eeyou/Eenou participants, whether or not they live in Eeyou Istchee.
- You want to use the CBHSSJB's facilities, resources or staff.
- You want to use its databases, medical records or other confidential patient information.
- You work for, or are affiliated with, the CBHSSJB.

The CBHSSJB approves only participatory, community-based research projects.

[The joint procedure](https://muhc.ca/cae/muhc-research-ethics-board-reb-standard-operating-procedures-sops) sets the steps after the first contact with the Research Office, from a preliminary proposal to the start of the research and the renewal of its approval. The CBHSSJB's cultural safety and suitability reviews end with the Research Office's Letter of Satisfactory Review, which the submission to the REB needs.

The CBHSSJB asks researchers to be aware of the time and effort each step takes. For a student's project, the CBHSSJB's approval steps apply to the supervisor, with the necessary adaptations.

Wait for the Research Office's Letter of Support before you develop the full research proposal. The Letter of Support is not a formal authorization and gives no right to proceed with the research. Researchers should not apply for funding until their project has received the letter.

### Meet the requirements of the joint procedure before the research starts

To approve research under the CBHSSJB's auspices, the REB assesses these aspects as well as its usual criteria:
- The collection, use, retention and stewardship of data respect the OCAP® principles and are consistent with the relevant standard provisions of the CBHSSJB's research agreement, including the return of results.
- The research methods follow participatory, collaborative research practice and integrate Indigenous ways of knowing wherever appropriate. Where that isn't possible, you must provide sufficient justification.
- Participant-facing documents and recruitment materials, social media included, are in plain English, with Cree translations where appropriate, arranged with the CBHSSJB's guidance.
- Compensation takes into account local suitability, such as the cost of living, and usability, such as gift cards for businesses that participants can reach. [The limits on compensation](/kb/recruitment/consent#compensate-participants-within-the-limits) still apply.

Ask the [REB](mailto:reb@muhc.mcgill.ca) whether your study also needs French versions of its documents.{/* TO CONFIRM (the REB): for a project under the CBHSSJB's auspices, is a French version of the participant documents needed? After the Research Office issues its Letter of Satisfactory Review, these requirements apply:
- Submit the project to the REB in Nagano. Besides the REB's usual documents, the submission must include the Letter of Satisfactory Review, the CBHSSJB's cultural safety and suitability reviews with any responses from your team, and the knowledge translation plan the CBHSSJB reviewed.
- The researcher and the CBHSSJB must conclude a research agreement before the project starts, with the REB-approved protocol and the REB approval letter attached. No research activity can take place until all parties have signed it.
- Research under the CBHSSJB's auspices requires the formal authorization of the CBHSSJB's mandated person. When researchers are affiliated with sites other than the CBHSSJB, each participating site's authorization must be obtained too. No recruitment, data collection, access to data or collection of biological materials can start at a site until that site's authorization has been obtained.

Do not start any research activity, including recruitment, data collection or analysis, until you have both REB approval and MUHC Authorization.

After REB approval, follow any CBHSSJB procedures that apply to continuing review and renewal.

### Commit to the Miyupimaatisiiun Research Principles

Everyone on the research team, students and research assistants included, must give a written statement that they will respect the CBHSSJB's [Miyupimaatisiiun Research Principles](https://www.creehealth.org/about-us/departments/research-office/research-principles).{/* TO CONFIRM (the EDI team, with its Indigenous partners): the names and spellings on this page, each as the Nation or organization writes it: Eeyou/Eenou (the REB's SOP 410.001 and the CBHSSJB's Miyupimaatisiiun Research Principles page) or Eeyou-Eenou (the CBHSSJB's Research Office and Information for Researchers pages), Eeyou Istchee, Eeyouch; Miyupimaatisiiun (the CBHSSJB's page title and suggested citation) or Miyupimâtisîun (its text and SOP 410.001); Makivvik (its own site; Atanniuvik's site writes "Makivik Corporation") (14-open-questions.md, batch 76). */} The principles include the following:

- Results must be interpreted with Eeyou/Eenou input through the CBHSSJB before publication, and its publication review processes must be followed.
- Any interpretation of data and any creation, including intellectual property, developed in the project is jointly owned by the CBHSSJB and the researcher.
- Consent forms should be available in the Eeyou language, îyiyû ayimuwin.

Guided by the CBHSSJB, researchers must show a commitment to promoting cultural safety throughout the life cycle of the project.

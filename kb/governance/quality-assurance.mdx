## QA's role in the review process

QA sits within the institutional layer of the [regulatory framework](/kb/governance/regulatory-framework) as the party responsible for confirming that training levels are appropriate, that investigator credentials are current, and that the study is feasible from a compliance standpoint before submission proceeds to the Personne mandatée.

QA feasibility approval is a required step in the triple review. Without it, the Personne mandatée cannot issue the MUHC Authorization letter, and the study cannot begin.

Beyond the feasibility check, QA plays a continuing role throughout the study lifecycle:

- **SOP development and maintenance** — QA develops and maintains the RI-MUHC Clinical Research SOPs, ensuring alignment with international guidelines, provincial and federal regulatory requirements, and institutional procedures.
- **Compliance training** — QA manages all compliance training for human participant research, including the SOP Reader and Competency Assessment (TalentLMS), Good Clinical Practice (GCP), Health Canada Division 5 (drug studies), and ISO 14155 (medical device studies).
- **Vendor qualification** — ensuring that vendors involved in a study meet applicable regulations is the responsibility of the Investigator or Sponsor-Investigator. You can submit a request to QA to assist in assessing and qualifying a vendor.
- **Continuous process improvement** — through internal process audits and collaboration with subject matter experts, QA identifies risks and implements improvements to enhance compliance and streamline research processes across the Institute.
- **For-cause investigations** — QA may conduct for-cause investigations triggered by reports of non-compliance from a study team, the REB, other departments, CAPA forms, or the Intake Committee.

## Quality by design: ICH E6(R3)

ICH E6(R3), effective April 1, 2026 in Canada, introduces a **quality-by-design (QbD)** approach to clinical trial management. Rather than relying on retrospective detection of problems, QbD means building quality into trial design from the outset — identifying the critical processes and data that matter most to participant safety and data integrity, and focusing oversight resources accordingly.

In practice, this means:

- **Critical to Quality (CtQ) factors** are identified during protocol development — the processes and data points whose failure or unreliability would most affect trial outcomes.
- **Risk-based monitoring** replaces the traditional model of comprehensive on-site source data verification. The Monitoring Plan targets resources toward the risks and sites that need the most attention.
- **Proportionate oversight** — routine, lower-risk processes receive less intensive oversight, freeing resources for areas with genuine risk.

The RI-MUHC SOP framework and QA's feasibility and audit activities are aligned with E6(R3)'s quality-by-design principles. When QA reviews your study at feasibility, they are assessing whether the quality management approach is appropriate for the study's risk profile, not simply whether required documents are present.

## What happens during a QA feasibility review

When you submit a new study in Nagano, QA performs an institutional feasibility check before the study can proceed to MUHC Authorization. The check confirms three things:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Investigator credentials">
    The QI/PI or Sponsor-Investigator holds valid Human Research Privileges or Researcher Status, as administered by the Performance Office and renewed every three years.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Trial Master File readiness (Sponsor-Investigator studies)">
    The Sponsor-Investigator has a Trial Master File (TMF) appropriate for study start.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Required training completed">
    All required institutional training based on study type has been completed by the QI/PI or Sponsor-Investigator. Training requirements differ based on whether the study involves investigational drugs (Division 5), medical devices (ISO 14155), or neither.
  </Step>
</Steps>

If any of these three conditions are not met, QA will flag the study and contact the research team. Questions about feasibility status or training requirements can be directed to [QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca](mailto:QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca).

## CAPA management

A Corrective and Preventive Action (CAPA) is the formal mechanism for addressing non-compliance identified during audits, inspections, monitoring visits, or for-cause investigations. QA is the institutional point of contact for CAPA management — CAPA forms are sent to [QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca](mailto:QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca) and filed in the ISF.

A well-constructed CAPA has three parts:

1. **Corrective action** — what was done immediately to address the specific finding and its impact
2. **Root cause analysis** — why the non-compliance occurred (training gap, process failure, unclear protocol requirement, system limitation)
3. **Preventive action** — what was changed so the same issue cannot recur

<Warning>
A CAPA that simply restates the finding and says "staff were retrained" without identifying the root cause is unlikely to satisfy an auditor or inspector. The root cause must be genuinely analyzed and the preventive action must address it.
</Warning>

For major and critical protocol deviations, CAPA is required and must be reported to the REB within 7 or 15 days depending on classification. See [Reportable Events and Protocol Deviations](/kb/conduct/reportable-events) for the full deviation classification framework.

## Internal audits

QA conducts internal audits of research processes across MUHC and RI-MUHC sites. These take two forms:

**Process audits** review how a specific process — ISF maintenance, deviation reporting, consent procedures — is being carried out across multiple studies or sites. They are not punitive; their purpose is continuous improvement of SOP content and practices. Meetings of the Quality Task Force (QTF) are typically convened after process audits to discuss and implement improvements.

**Study-specific audits** may be triggered when there are specific concerns about a study — unusual deviation rates, safety reporting issues, or findings from a monitoring visit. A for-cause audit triggered by a specific concern tends to be more intensive than a routine process audit.

Sponsor auditors may also conduct their own audits of your site. For-cause sponsor audits can be triggered by unusual adverse event rates, data quality issues, unexpectedly high enrolment, or suspected protocol non-compliance.

## Regulatory inspections

If you receive a notice from Health Canada or another regulatory body (for example, the FDA) confirming an inspection at your site:

<Warning>
Contact QA within 24 hours of receiving an inspection notice at [QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca](mailto:QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca). QA coordinates site readiness, facilitates communication with the regulatory body, and ensures your team is prepared. Do not wait — four weeks' notice is not as much time as it seems.
</Warning>

QA will assist with documentation review, organization of the ISF, and preparation of a CAPA response to any inspection findings. For a full description of the Health Canada inspection process, including finding classifications and the preparation checklist, see [Monitoring, Audits, and Inspections](/kb/conduct/monitoring-audits).

## Quality Task Force

The **Quality Task Force (QTF)** is a group of subject matter experts from across the Institute who work with QA to improve clinical research processes. Meetings are usually held after QA process audits to discuss improvements to a specific process, but can also be triggered by suggestions from researchers.

To share a process improvement idea, flag a gap in an existing SOP, or volunteer as a QTF member, email [QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca](mailto:QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca).

Research integrity is not a separate compliance layer — it is the condition under which everything else in clinical research is valid. Fabricated data, falsified records, or suppressed results do not just violate policy; they invalidate the science and potentially harm people who received or were denied a treatment based on it.

## What research integrity means

Research integrity means conducting research honestly, rigorously, and transparently — in a way that can withstand scrutiny and that others can rely on. In clinical research, it carries additional weight because the people making decisions based on the data are not the researchers but patients, clinicians, regulators, and funding bodies.

The concept encompasses how data are collected, recorded, and reported; how authorship is attributed; how conflicts of interest are disclosed; how research subjects are treated; and how results — including negative ones — are published. It is the obligation of every member of a research team, not only the <Tooltip tip="US equivalent of the Canadian Qualified Investigator (QI)." headline="Principal Investigator (PI)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#pi">principal investigator</Tooltip>.

The 2024 revision of the Declaration of Helsinki formally incorporated scientific integrity as an ethical requirement, stating that individuals, teams, and organizations involved in medical research must never engage in research misconduct.

<Note>
  Research integrity standards apply to research, as defined by TCPS2 — *"an undertaking intended to extend knowledge through a disciplined inquiry and/or systematic investigation."* Quality assurance and quality improvement projects are governed by TCPS2 Article 2.5 and follow a different framework (still grounded in respect for persons, welfare, and justice). If you are not sure which category your project falls into, use the [Is It Research?](/is-it-research) screening tool.
</Note>

## Responsible conduct of research obligations

At the RI-MUHC, responsible conduct of research (RCR) obligations come from three overlapping sources:

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Tri-Council (federal)">
    The [TCPS2](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/policy-politique_tcps2-eptc2_2022.html) and the [Tri-Agency Framework on Responsible Conduct of Research](https://rcr.ethics.gc.ca/eng/framework-cadre.html) establish national standards for all institutions receiving CIHR, NSERC, or SSHRC funding. They define misconduct, set out institutional obligations, and require that researchers and their teams conduct research with honesty, rigour, transparency, openness, fairness, and accountability.

    **Research Data Management (RDM).** The [Tri-Agency Research Data Management Policy](https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/interagency-research-funding/policies-and-guidelines/research-data-management/tri-agency-research-data-management-policy) (effective March 2021, mandatory for grant applications phased in by agency) requires that: institutions have an RDM policy; researchers submit a Data Management Plan (DMP) with eligible grant applications; and research data underlying publications be deposited in an accessible repository. CIHR now requires a DMP for most Project and Team Grant applications. A DMP describes how data will be collected, stored, shared, and preserved. Contact [CORD](/kb/design-support/cord) for DMP support and REDCap infrastructure letters.

    **Sex- and Gender-Based Analysis Plus (SGBA+ / GBA+).** CIHR requires applicants to integrate sex and gender considerations into research design as a condition of most competitions. SGBA+ asks how biological sex (and its intersections with age, ethnicity, disability, etc.) and socially constructed gender affect the research question, the study population, the intervention, and the outcomes. The [CIHR SGBA+ training](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/50836.html) module is free and expected for all team members on CIHR-funded studies. Contact the [EDI team](/kb/team/edi) for GBA+ consultation.

    **EDI Champion on CIHR Team Grants.** CIHR Team Grants (including SPOR, Strategy Grants, and some Priority Announcements) include a named **EDI Champion** role on the research team. The EDI Champion is accountable for embedding equity, diversity, and inclusion practices into the project — not as an add-on but as part of the scientific design, team composition, and dissemination plan. The role must be described in the application and is assessed by reviewers. If your team grant requires an EDI Champion, designate the role before submission and document their planned activities.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Provincial (Quebec)">
    The [MSSS Cadre de référence](https://publications.msss.gouv.qc.ca/msss/document-002696/) (2020), Norm 9, requires every <Tooltip tip="Quebec's public health and social services network (Réseau de la santé et des services sociaux), including the RI-MUHC." headline="RSSS" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#rsss">RSSS</Tooltip> institution to promote responsible conduct of research and to establish a procedure for handling allegations of misconduct. The institution must designate a person responsible for applying this norm and receiving allegations. This is a provincial governance obligation independent of federal funding.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Institutional">
    Because RI-MUHC investigators hold McGill Faculty of Medicine appointments, the [McGill Regulations Concerning the Investigation of Research Misconduct](https://www.mcgill.ca/research-integrity/policies) and the Regulation on the Conduct of Research apply to their work. The McGill Research Integrity Office is the institutional point of contact for misconduct allegations involving McGill-affiliated researchers.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## What constitutes research misconduct

The core definition of research misconduct is consistent across TCPS2, the Tri-Agency Framework, and McGill policy: **fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism** in the design, execution, reporting, or review of research.

| Category | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Fabrication | Making up data or results | Recording or reporting data, observations, or results that were never actually obtained; inventing participant data, test results, or consent records |
| Falsification | Manipulating existing data or results | Altering data, images, or results to misrepresent findings; selectively omitting data points to change the apparent outcome of an analysis |
| Plagiarism | Appropriating others' work without attribution | Presenting another person's ideas, methods, results, or writing as one's own — in publications, grant applications, protocols, or data |

Beyond the core three, TCPS2 and McGill policy recognize other serious departures from accepted standards that may constitute misconduct depending on severity: failure to meet obligations to research participants; improper authorship practices (gift authorship, ghost authorship, denial of authorship); inadequate supervision; misrepresentation in grant applications; breach of confidentiality in peer review; and failure to report or address misconduct by others.

<Note>
**Deviation vs. misconduct:** Not every protocol deviation, data error, or documentation lapse constitutes research misconduct. Mistakes, poor judgment, and disagreements about data interpretation are not misconduct under TCPS2 or McGill policy. The test is whether there was an intentional departure from accepted standards or a reckless disregard for them — not simply whether an error occurred.
</Note>

## The institutional framework at the RI-MUHC

There is no standalone RI-MUHC Research Integrity Officer. The institutional framework for research integrity at the MUHC is built through the Management Agreement between the MUHC and the RI-MUHC, which obliges the Research Institute to comply with McGill University Faculty of Medicine policies on research ethics and scientific integrity. In practice, misconduct investigations involving RI-MUHC researchers are handled through McGill's structures.

At the same time, the MSSS Cadre de référence (Norm 9) requires the MUHC, as an <Tooltip tip="Quebec's public health and social services network (Réseau de la santé et des services sociaux), including the RI-MUHC." headline="RSSS" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#rsss">RSSS</Tooltip> institution, to designate a person responsible for applying the norm on responsible conduct and receiving allegations. How the MUHC fulfils this obligation through its McGill relationship is not publicly documented in detail — if you need to raise a concern, the paths below are the operative ones.

The RI-MUHC <Tooltip tip="All planned and systematic actions to ensure trial data are generated, documented, and reported in compliance with GCP and SOPs." headline="Quality Assurance (QA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#qa">QA</Tooltip> Department plays a related but distinct role: it identifies protocol deviations, GCP non-compliance, and data integrity issues during audits and inspection preparation. QA findings that suggest potential misconduct would be escalated through the appropriate channels, but QA is not the body that investigates misconduct allegations.

## Raising a concern or allegation

Research misconduct can be raised through several channels depending on the nature and severity of the concern. These are not mutually exclusive.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Supervisory or departmental level">
    For concerns about conduct within a study team or department, the first step is typically the study's principal investigator, a department head, or the RI-MUHC Director of Clinical Research. This is appropriate for concerns that may be addressable through correction and oversight rather than formal investigation.
  </Step>
  <Step title="McGill Research Integrity Office">
    For formal allegations of misconduct by a McGill-affiliated researcher, the [McGill Research Integrity Office](https://www.mcgill.ca/research-integrity/) is the institutional point of contact. McGill's process includes initial assessment, inquiry if warranted, and formal investigation if the inquiry finds substance. Confidentiality is protected throughout, and reprisal against anyone raising a concern in good faith is prohibited.
  </Step>
  <Step title="MUHC REB — for ethics-related concerns">
    If the concern relates to a specific ongoing study — non-compliance with the approved protocol, improper consent, or risk to participants — it can be reported to the MUHC REB via Nagano. The REB has authority to suspend or terminate approval for a study and to require corrective action.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Funding agency — if federal funds are involved">
    For research funded by CIHR, NSERC, or SSHRC, the Secretariat on Responsible Conduct of Research (SRCR) has oversight authority. Allegations can also be submitted directly to the [SRCR](https://rcr.ethics.gc.ca/eng/contact.html) if institutional processes have been exhausted or are not appropriate.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Note>
Good faith reporting is protected. McGill policy and TCPS2 both explicitly prohibit reprisal against anyone who raises a concern about research misconduct in good faith. A report made honestly, even if the allegation is ultimately not substantiated, does not expose the person raising it to disciplinary action.
</Note>

## RCR training

A Responsible Conduct of Research (RCR) course is available through CITI and is recommended for all research personnel. For projects funded by CIHR, NSERC, or SSHRC, completing RCR training is a condition of funding.

The RI-MUHC institutional compliance training incorporates TCPS2 principles and is accepted by tri-council agencies as fulfilling the TCPS2 ethics component of the RCR training requirement. For a standalone RCR certificate, the CITI RCR course is available through the RI-MUHC portal, and the [TCPS2 Core tutorial](https://tcps2core.ca) is available free of charge.

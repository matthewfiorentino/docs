Research integrity is not a separate compliance layer — it is the condition under which everything else in clinical research is valid. Fabricated data, falsified records, or suppressed results do not just violate policy; they invalidate the science and potentially harm people who received or were denied a treatment based on it.

## What research integrity means

Research integrity means conducting research honestly, rigorously, and transparently — in a way that can withstand scrutiny and that others can rely on. In clinical research, it carries additional weight because the people making decisions based on the data are not the researchers but patients, clinicians, regulators, and funding bodies.

The concept encompasses how data are collected, recorded, and reported; how authorship is attributed; how conflicts of interest are disclosed; how research subjects are treated; and how results — including negative ones — are published. It is the obligation of every member of a research team, not only the principal investigator.

The 2024 revision of the Declaration of Helsinki formally incorporated scientific integrity as an ethical requirement, stating that individuals, teams, and organizations involved in medical research must never engage in research misconduct.

## Responsible conduct of research obligations

At the RI-MUHC, responsible conduct of research (RCR) obligations come from three overlapping sources:

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Tri-Council (federal)">
    The [TCPS2](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/policy-politique_tcps2-eptc2_2022.html) and the [Tri-Agency Framework on Responsible Conduct of Research](https://rcr.ethics.gc.ca/eng/framework-cadre.html) establish national standards for all institutions receiving CIHR, NSERC, or SSHRC funding. They define misconduct, set out institutional obligations, and require that researchers and their teams conduct research with honesty, rigour, transparency, openness, fairness, and accountability.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Provincial (Quebec)">
    The [MSSS Cadre de référence](https://publications.msss.gouv.qc.ca/msss/document-002696/) (2020), Norm 9, requires every RSSS institution to promote responsible conduct of research and to establish a procedure for handling allegations of misconduct. The institution must designate a person responsible for applying this norm and receiving allegations. This is a provincial governance obligation independent of federal funding.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Institutional">
    Because RI-MUHC investigators hold McGill Faculty of Medicine appointments, the [McGill Regulations Concerning the Investigation of Research Misconduct](https://www.mcgill.ca/research-integrity/policies) and the Regulation on the Conduct of Research apply to their work. The McGill Research Integrity Office is the institutional point of contact for misconduct allegations involving McGill-affiliated researchers.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## What constitutes research misconduct

The core definition of research misconduct is consistent across TCPS2, the Tri-Agency Framework, and McGill policy: **fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism** in the design, execution, reporting, or review of research.

| Category | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Fabrication | Making up data or results | Recording or reporting data, observations, or results that were never actually obtained; inventing participant data, test results, or consent records |
| Falsification | Manipulating existing data or results | Altering data, images, or results to misrepresent findings; selectively omitting data points to change the apparent outcome of an analysis |
| Plagiarism | Appropriating others' work without attribution | Presenting another person's ideas, methods, results, or writing as one's own — in publications, grant applications, protocols, or data |

Beyond the core three, TCPS2 and McGill policy recognize other serious departures from accepted standards that may constitute misconduct depending on severity: failure to meet obligations to research participants; improper authorship practices (gift authorship, ghost authorship, denial of authorship); inadequate supervision; misrepresentation in grant applications; breach of confidentiality in peer review; and failure to report or address misconduct by others.

<Note>
**Deviation vs. misconduct:** Not every protocol deviation, data error, or documentation lapse constitutes research misconduct. Mistakes, poor judgment, and disagreements about data interpretation are not misconduct under TCPS2 or McGill policy. The test is whether there was an intentional departure from accepted standards or a reckless disregard for them — not simply whether an error occurred.
</Note>

## Authorship responsibilities

Authorship of a publication carries credit, academic weight, and accountability. Adding someone as an author who did not meaningfully contribute — or omitting someone who did — are both integrity issues with real consequences.

The [ICMJE criteria](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html) are the standard applied by virtually all biomedical journals. To qualify as an author, an individual must meet **all four** of the following:

1. Substantial contributions to the **conception or design** of the work, or to the **acquisition, analysis, or interpretation of data**
2. **Reviewing the work critically** for important intellectual content (with the opportunity to make changes if warranted)
3. **Final approval** of the version to be published
4. **Agreement to be accountable** for all aspects of the work, including ensuring that questions about accuracy or integrity are appropriately investigated and resolved

Meeting only some of these criteria does not qualify a person for authorship — they should be acknowledged by name in the acknowledgements section with their contribution specified.

Activities that **do not** qualify for authorship on their own include obtaining funding, general supervision of the research group, administrative support, data collection without analytical involvement, and writing assistance or proofreading.

<Warning>
Ghost authorship (omitting someone who meets the criteria) and gift authorship (including someone who does not) both violate ICMJE guidelines and the Tri-Agency Responsible Conduct of Research framework. Either can result in correction or retraction of the published paper.
</Warning>

Discuss authorship expectations within the team early — ideally before data collection begins, not after the manuscript is written.

## Conflict of interest disclosure

A conflict of interest in research exists when a researcher's personal, financial, or professional interests could — or could be perceived to — influence how they design, conduct, report, or oversee a study. The obligation is to disclose. Disclosure is not an admission of wrongdoing; it is the mechanism that allows the institution, the REB, and ultimately research participants to make informed judgements about the research.

A conflict does not have to be financial — a personal relationship, a professional rivalry, or a reputational stake can all constitute a conflict. The conflict does not have to be actual — the appearance of a conflict, as perceived by a reasonable outside observer, triggers the same obligations.

### Two disclosure tracks

COI disclosure at the RI-MUHC operates through two independent channels that must each be satisfied separately.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Track 1 — McGill institutional">
    **What:** Ongoing financial and non-financial interests that could affect any aspect of your university responsibilities

    **When:** Annually, and whenever a new reportable interest arises

    **To whom:** Reporting Officer (typically your department chair; dean for chairs)

    **Form:** McGill Statement of Conflict of Interest (Short Form for academic staff; Long Form B for research-related disclosures)

    **Governed by:** [McGill Regulation on Conflict of Interest](https://www.mcgill.ca/secretariat/policies-and-regulations)

    Financial conflicts include equity holdings, consulting fees, honoraria, royalties, paid advisory roles, and sponsored travel. Non-financial conflicts include personal relationships, supervisory relationships, academic rivalries, and reputational interests. Both categories are subject to disclosure.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Track 2 — REB study-level">
    **What:** Any conflict specific to a particular research study — financial interest in the sponsor, equity in a company whose product is being tested, finder's fees, or any personal stake in the outcome

    **When:** At initial REB submission and whenever a new conflict arises during the study lifecycle

    **To whom:** MUHC REB (via Nagano) and to research participants (in the informed consent form)

    **Form:** Disclosed in the Nagano F11 application and in the ICF

    **Governed by:** [MUHC REB SOP on Conflicts of Interest](https://muhc.ca/cae/muhc-research-ethics-board-reb-standard-operating-procedures-sops) · [TCPS2 Ch. 7](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter7-chapitre7.html)

    The MUHC REB SOP requires that QI/PIs disclose all details of their research activities including budget agreements, commercial interests, relations with consultants, and any other information relevant to a potential conflict. TCPS2 and the MUHC REB SOP also require that investigators disclose potential conflicts to research participants in the informed consent form.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

<Warning>
The two tracks are additive, not alternative. Disclosing to your department chair does not satisfy the REB disclosure obligation — and vice versa. Each track must be satisfied independently.
</Warning>

### Common conflict of interest situations

| Situation | Why it may be a conflict | What is required |
|---|---|---|
| Financial interest in sponsor company | Equity, stock options, or consulting arrangements with the company whose product is being tested | Disclosure to McGill (annual, Long Form B) and to MUHC REB at submission; may require disclosure in ICF |
| Finder's fee or recruitment bonus | Per-participant financial incentive creates pressure to enrol participants who may not meet criteria | Must be disclosed to REB; per-participant fees above actual costs are not permitted |
| Investigator as Sponsor-Investigator | Holding both sponsor and investigator roles means no independent oversight of your own study | Disclosure at submission; additional governance requirements under the 100-series SOPs |
| PI recruiting their own patients | Therapeutic relationship creates power imbalance | Addressed in the consent process — where possible, a person other than the treating clinician should obtain consent |
| Academic stake in outcomes | Career advancement or publication record tied to the study's results | Non-financial conflict — disclosure to McGill Reporting Officer; REB may ask about blinding or independent data review |

### What happens after disclosure

Disclosure triggers a management process, not automatic disqualification. The Reporting Officer (McGill track) or REB (study-level track) reviews the conflict and determines whether management measures are needed — these typically range from enhanced oversight to specific ICF language, and rarely result in prohibition. A conflict that cannot be adequately managed may result in removal from the specific activity.

<Warning>
Undisclosed conflicts carry greater risk than disclosed ones. A conflict that is disclosed and managed does not by itself constitute misconduct. A conflict that is not disclosed — particularly one that subsequently influences study conduct or reporting — is a serious integrity breach that may constitute research misconduct under both McGill policy and the Tri-Agency Framework.
</Warning>

## Raising a concern or allegation

Research misconduct can be raised through several channels depending on the nature and severity of the concern. These are not mutually exclusive.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Supervisory or departmental level">
    For concerns about conduct within a study team or department, the first step is typically the study's principal investigator, a department head, or the RI-MUHC Director of Clinical Research. This is appropriate for concerns that may be addressable through correction and oversight rather than formal investigation.
  </Step>
  <Step title="McGill Research Integrity Office">
    For formal allegations of misconduct by a McGill-affiliated researcher, the [McGill Research Integrity Office](https://www.mcgill.ca/research-integrity/) is the institutional point of contact. McGill's process includes initial assessment, inquiry if warranted, and formal investigation if the inquiry finds substance. Confidentiality is protected throughout, and reprisal against anyone raising a concern in good faith is prohibited.
  </Step>
  <Step title="MUHC REB — for ethics-related concerns">
    If the concern relates to a specific ongoing study — non-compliance with the approved protocol, improper consent, or risk to participants — it can be reported to the MUHC REB via Nagano. The REB has authority to suspend or terminate approval for a study and to require corrective action.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Funding agency — if federal funds are involved">
    For research funded by CIHR, NSERC, or SSHRC, the Secretariat on Responsible Conduct of Research (SRCR) has oversight authority. Allegations can also be submitted directly to the [SRCR](https://rcr.ethics.gc.ca/eng/contact.html) if institutional processes have been exhausted or are not appropriate.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Note>
Good faith reporting is protected. McGill policy and TCPS2 both explicitly prohibit reprisal against anyone who raises a concern about research misconduct in good faith. A report made honestly, even if the allegation is ultimately not substantiated, does not expose the person raising it to disciplinary action.
</Note>

## RCR training

A Responsible Conduct of Research (RCR) course is available through CITI and is recommended for all research personnel. For projects funded by CIHR, NSERC, or SSHRC, completing RCR training is a condition of funding.

The RI-MUHC institutional compliance training incorporates TCPS2 principles and is accepted by tri-council agencies as fulfilling the TCPS2 ethics component of the RCR training requirement. For a standalone RCR certificate, the CITI RCR course is available through the RI-MUHC portal, and the [TCPS2 Core tutorial](https://tcps2core.ca) is available free of charge.

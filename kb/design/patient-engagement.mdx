## Know what funders expect

The [Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR)](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/41204.html) defines patient-oriented research as engaging patients, their caregivers and families as partners in the research process. ## Choose how far patients will be involved

Patient engagement runs along a spectrum, and the right level depends on the study, its question and its stage. CIHR's [SPOR Patient Engagement Framework](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/48413.html) sets out key concepts, principles and areas for engagement rather than levels. A widely used model, the International Association for Public Participation's [IAP2 Spectrum of Public Participation](https://www.iap2.org/page/pillars), has five levels:

| Level | What it can look like in a study | When it can fit |
|---|---|---|
| Inform | Patients receive information about the study. | The minimum for every study: standard communication with participants. |
| Consult | Patients are asked for input on specific questions, for example on the readability of consent forms or on outcome measures. | Early study design, and refining the protocol. |
| Involve | Patients work directly with the research team throughout, so that their concerns are understood and considered. | Studies where patients' experience shapes design decisions. |
| Collaborate | Patients take part in the study's governance: steering committees, interpreting data, writing knowledge translation materials with the team. | Studies with a direct effect on patients, and CIHR-funded patient-oriented research. |
| Empower | Patients hold the final decisions. | Community-based participatory research, and studies of priorities patients have identified. |

For most clinical trials at the RI-MUHC, engagement at the consult or collaborate level is realistic. Even at the consult level, patients' comments on consent form language, the burden of visits and the relevance of outcomes can improve a study.{/* TO CONFIRM (the Research Facilitator): no source found for the "When it can fit" column, for the examples in the "What it can look like in a study" column (b161: they are the Hub's, not IAP2's), or for "most clinical trials at the RI-MUHC" fitting consult or collaborate. ## Recruit patient partners

Patient partners are not study participants. They are people with lived experience of a condition or of the health care system who bring that perspective to the research.

Teams often find patient partners through:

- MUHC patient committees and patient advisory groups.
- Patient organizations for a disease, such as Diabetes Québec or the Canadian Cancer Society.
- The Unité de soutien SSA Québec, which supports the identification and recruitment of patient partners.
- Community organizations and advocacy groups.
- Former study participants who said they would like to be involved in research, with their consent.

When recruiting, consider:

- Seeking diversity, so that patient partners reflect the people the research affects, in age, gender, language, socioeconomic background and experience of illness.
- Being clear about the time commitment, the expectations and what the partnership involves.
- Looking first for people with lived experience of the condition under study. People with general experience of health care can also help, for example by reviewing consent forms or the burden of visits.

## Pay patient partners

Patient partners give time, energy and expertise. Offering them payment helps remove barriers to taking part in research, and makes research more equitable and diverse. CIHR's [considerations when paying patient partners in research](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/51466.html) say that patient partners should be paid for their time and expertise, in addition to having their expenses covered, such as travel to an event. Payment should reflect the time, effort, lived experience and skill a patient brings.

There is no nationally determined or prescribed rate that SPOR recommends. The research team sets the amount with the patient partners, within institutional and provincial policies, and CIHR highly recommends that rates for patient partners stay consistent within each institution.

The principle of equity should be followed when offering payment, to encourage diversity. Different payment styles and amounts may be more appropriate for members of groups less represented in research, such as First Nations, Inuit and Métis Peoples, immigrants and refugees. Patients decide on payment with the research team, and may decline payment without losing their place in the project.

Consider these principles too:

- Reimburse expenses, such as travel, parking or childcare, separately from payment for time.
- Apply the same rates to every patient partner on the same team, except where equity calls for a different payment style or amount.
- Don't make payment depend on outputs: a patient partner who takes part in discussions is paid for their time, whether or not their input changes the protocol.

## Budget for patient partners in a grant application

CIHR encourages researchers to budget for patient partnership from the beginning of the project. Paying patients is an eligible expense in CIHR-funded research when it contributes to the research's direct costs.

When preparing a CIHR or FRQS grant application, consider:

- Showing patient partners' time as its own line, under the heading the funder's budget template uses, such as knowledge translation or participant costs.
- Budgeting the hourly rate multiplied by a realistic number of hours a year, including meetings, reviewing materials and travel time.
- Adding expense reimbursement as a separate line: travel (local transit or mileage), parking, childcare and assistive technology if needed.
- Asking the [Pre-Awards Office](mailto:pre-awards.rimuhc@muhc.mcgill.ca) whether institutional overhead applies to the patient partner line.

### Payment, taxes and benefits

Payments to patient partners go through the RI-MUHC's finance system and can have tax effects. A payment for a defined contribution, such as attending meetings or reviewing materials, is often an honorarium, which the Canada Revenue Agency defines as a voluntary payment for services where fees are not legally or traditionally required.{/* TO CONFIRM (RI-MUHC Finance; new in b154's second reader, the fact check's row P30): CIHR's honorarium is "a one-time payment as a thank-you for their contribution to the research project or activity", and a set rate for recurring work is its "fixed service income", which "may be considered employment income". Patient partners may need to give their Social Insurance Number for tax forms. RI-MUHC Finance assesses how a payment is classified.

Honoraria above the Canada Revenue Agency's reporting threshold in a calendar year may require a T4A slip, so the payment is reportable income for the patient partner. It may affect income-tested benefits, such as disability support, social assistance or drug coverage. Patient partners should be told when a payment could affect their financial situation, especially those who receive income-tested benefits.

## Bring patient views into the study design

Patient engagement is most useful early, while the study is designed and before the protocol is final. Patient partners can help answer questions such as these:

- Research question and outcomes: are the outcomes meaningful to patients, and would patients choose different endpoints?
- Consent materials: is the language clear, are the risks described in a way patients understand, and is the form too long?
- Visits and burden: is the number of visits practical, and are the assessments and procedures acceptable?
- Recruitment: how would patients prefer to learn about the study, what would motivate them to take part, and what barriers exist?
- Sharing the results: how should results be communicated to patients and the public?

## Document the engagement

For funded research that requires patient engagement, consider keeping a record of:

- Who was engaged, at what level and at what stage.
- How patients' input influenced decisions on the study's design.
- Records of meetings, if formal advisory meetings were held.
- What patient partners were paid.

CIHR progress reports and final reports may ask for this record.{/* TO CONFIRM (the Pre-Awards Office; already in 14-open-questions.md, B75): do CIHR progress and final reports ask for documentation of patient engagement? The list is Hub advice from the live page. */}

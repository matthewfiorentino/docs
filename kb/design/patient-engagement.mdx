Patient engagement in research is the practice of involving patients, caregivers, and community members as active partners in the research process — not just as study participants, but as contributors to study design, conduct, and dissemination. In Canada, this is formalized through the [Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research (SPOR)](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/41204.html), and increasingly expected by major funders.

## Why this matters

[CIHR](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/51605.html) requires meaningful patient engagement for SPOR-funded competitions and strongly encourages it across all funding programs — particularly for clinical trials, where reviewers assess whether patient perspectives informed the study design. Beyond funder requirements, studies designed with patient input tend to ask more relevant questions, use more practical outcome measures, and achieve better recruitment and retention.

## The SPOR framework

Canada's [Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research (SPOR)](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/41204.html) is a federal-provincial partnership led by CIHR that aims to ensure research is informed by patient priorities and produces outcomes that matter to patients.

SPOR defines patient-oriented research as research that:
- Engages patients as partners in the research process
- Focuses on patient-identified priorities
- Aims to improve patient outcomes through evidence-informed practice

SPOR operates through provincial SUPPORT Units (in Quebec: the [Unité de soutien SSA Québec](https://ssaquebec.ca/en/) — formerly the Unité de soutien SRAP) that provide methodological and practical support for patient-oriented research.

## Levels of engagement

Patient engagement exists on a spectrum. The right level depends on the study, the research question, and the stage of the project.

The [IAP2 Spectrum of Public Participation](https://www.iap2.org/page/pillars), adapted by CIHR for the [SPOR Patient Engagement Framework](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/48413.html), defines five levels:

| Level | What it looks like | When it's appropriate |
|---|---|---|
| **Inform** | Patients receive information about the study | Minimum for all studies — standard participant communication |
| **Consult** | Patients are asked for input on specific questions (e.g., reviewing consent forms for readability, commenting on outcome measures) | Early-stage study design; protocol refinement |
| **Involve** | Patients work directly with the research team to ensure concerns are understood and considered throughout the process | Studies where patient experience shapes design decisions |
| **Collaborate** | Patients participate in study governance — serving on steering committees, contributing to data interpretation, co-developing knowledge translation materials | Studies with direct patient impact; CIHR-funded patient-oriented research |
| **Empower** | Final decision-making authority is placed in the hands of patients | Community-based participatory research; studies addressing patient-identified priorities |

For most clinical trials at the RI-MUHC, engagement at the **consult** or **collaborate** level is realistic and productive. Even at the consult level, patient input on consent form language, visit burden, and outcome relevance can improve the study.

## Recruiting patient partners

Patient partners are not study participants. They are individuals with lived experience of a condition or the health care system who contribute their perspective to the research process.

**Where to find patient partners:**
- MUHC patient committees and patient advisory groups
- Disease-specific patient organizations (e.g., Diabetes Québec, Société canadienne du cancer)
- The [Quebec SPOR SUPPORT Unit](https://ssaquebec.ca/en/) (Unité de soutien SSA Québec) maintains a network of trained patient partners
- Community organizations and advocacy groups
- Previous study participants who expressed interest in research involvement (with appropriate consent)

**Considerations when recruiting:**
- Seek diversity — patient partners should reflect the population affected by the research, including diversity in age, gender, language, socioeconomic background, and illness experience
- Be transparent about the time commitment, expectations, and what the partnership entails
- Patients with lived experience of the specific condition under study are ideal, but patients with general health care experience can also contribute (e.g., reviewing consent forms, assessing visit burden)

## Compensation

Patient partners invest time, energy, and expertise. Compensation acknowledges this contribution and removes financial barriers to participation.

### Compensation rates

CIHR and SPOR guidelines recommend that patient partners be compensated for their time and out-of-pocket expenses (travel, parking, childcare). The most commonly referenced rate benchmark is approximately **\$40/hour for patient partner time**, which appears in the [CIHR Institute of Genetics guidelines on patient partner compensation](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/51466.html). This figure is an Institute of Genetics benchmark, not a CIHR-wide mandated rate — other CIHR institutes and FRQS do not publish a single standardized rate. The appropriate rate for a specific study should reflect the local context, the level of expertise the patient partner brings, and any applicable institutional policy.

The [SPOR Evidence Alliance](https://sporevidencealliance.ca/) and the Quebec SPOR SUPPORT Unit (Unité de soutien SSA Québec) have both published compensation frameworks for patient partners in research. Consult the [Research Facilitator](mailto:research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca) for the current institutional guidance before budgeting a new application.

**Principles:**
- Compensate for time at a fair rate; reimburse expenses separately
- Apply rates consistently across all patient partners on the same team
- Do not make compensation contingent on outputs — patient partners who contribute to discussions but whose input does not lead to protocol changes are still entitled to compensation for their time

### Finance and tax treatment at the RI-MUHC

Patient partner compensation is handled through the RI-MUHC finance system and has tax implications that research teams often discover too late:

**Honoraria vs. employment income.** Patient partner payments are typically classified as honoraria when paid for a defined contribution (attending meetings, reviewing materials) rather than as employment income. The distinction depends on the degree of control, the nature of the engagement, and the regularity of the arrangement. The RI-MUHC finance team will assess the classification; do not assume.

**T4A reporting.** Honoraria payments above the CRA reporting threshold in a calendar year may require issuance of a T4A slip to the patient partner. This means the payment is reportable income for the patient partner — it may affect their income-tested benefits (disability support, social assistance, drug benefits) even though it is not employment income. Discuss this proactively with patient partners, particularly those who receive income-tested benefits and for whom the income effect may be unexpected or unwelcome.

**Budgeting for patient partner compensation.** When preparing CIHR or FRQS grant applications:

- Include patient partner time as a distinct line item under "Knowledge Translation" or "Participant Costs" depending on the funder's budget template
- Budget the hourly rate multiplied by realistic hours per year — include meeting attendance, materials review, and travel time
- Add expense reimbursement as a separate line: travel (local transit or mileage), parking, childcare, assistive technology if needed
- Account for institutional overhead if it applies to the patient partner budget line — confirm with the Pre-Awards Office whether the applicable indirect rate covers patient partner costs

<Note>
Patient partner compensation is distinct from participant compensation for study visits. They are budgeted and managed separately. For participant compensation, see [Recruitment, Screening, and Enrollment](/kb/recruitment/recruitment-enrollment).
</Note>

## Integrating patient perspectives into study design

Patient engagement is most valuable when it happens early — during study design, before the protocol is finalized.

**Areas where patient input adds value:**

- **Research question and outcomes** — are the outcomes meaningful to patients? Would patients prioritize different endpoints?
- **Consent materials** — is the language clear? Are the risks described in a way patients understand? Is the consent form too long?
- **Visit schedule and burden** — is the number of visits practical? Are the assessments acceptable? Would patients tolerate the procedures described?
- **Recruitment strategy** — how would patients prefer to learn about the study? What would motivate them to participate? What barriers exist?
- **Knowledge translation** — how should results be communicated back to patients and the public?

## Documentation

For funded research requiring patient engagement, document the process:

- Who was engaged, at what level, and at what stage
- How patient input influenced study design decisions
- Meeting records, if formal advisory meetings were held
- Compensation provided

This documentation may be required for CIHR progress reports and final reporting.

## Understand what a decentralized trial is

In a trial with decentralized elements, some or all trial activities take place outside traditional trial centres. They may take place in participants' homes, with health care providers or at treatment centres closer to participants, or through digital health technologies and virtual tools. A trial can add one element, such as remote follow-up visits, or be fully decentralized.

A trial site is one main location and every location of the decentralized elements attached to it. There can only be one qualified investigator (<Tooltip tip="The person responsible to the sponsor for the conduct of a drug or natural health product trial at a clinical trial site, who is entitled to provide health care under the laws of the province where the site is located." headline="Qualified Investigator (QI)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#qi">QI</Tooltip>) at each site, and every location where trial activities take place under that QI's supervision is part of the QI's site. Health Canada does not require separate Clinical Trial Site Information (CTSI) forms or REB approvals for these other locations, provided the QI oversees them and they follow the approved protocol. Other provincial or institutional approvals may still be needed.

Health Canada's regulatory requirements for a trial with decentralized elements are the same as for a trial in a traditional setting. Planning and operations may need to adapt.

Consider discussing the planned decentralized elements with <Tooltip tip="The RI-MUHC's Quality Assurance department, which checks the credentials and training of each investigator who submits a study, audits research and maintains the SOPs. In GCP, quality assurance is the planned, systematic actions that ensure a trial complies with GCP and the applicable regulatory requirements." headline="Quality Assurance (QA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#qa">Quality Assurance (QA)</Tooltip>, at [QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca](mailto:QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca), before the protocol is written.

## Know why decentralized elements are used

Decentralized elements can reduce barriers to taking part in a trial, such as geography and travel, language, family and work responsibilities, or health and care needs. Offering a trial closer to home may also lead to higher enrolment and retention of participants. For sponsors and investigators, they can help reach geographically and culturally diverse groups of participants, such as for rare disease trials. They also let researchers and other health care providers take part in research across Canada without being in the same place.

They can also create barriers. Participants without reliable internet, or less used to digital tools, may find it harder to take part.

The international good clinical practice guideline of the International Council for Harmonisation, ICH E6(R3), names trials with decentralized elements among the trial designs a protocol describes. Health Canada implemented it on April 1, 2026, and its Annex 2 on Nov. 4, 2026. ## Assess whether the trial suits decentralized elements

Health Canada's guidance lists what sponsors and QIs should weigh before decentralizing trial activities. Assess these during design:

- What is known about the investigational drug's safety, and how much of its safety profile is established.
- The route and complexity of giving the investigational drug, and any specialized preparation, administration or evaluation it needs.
- How products and specimens will be transported, stored, tracked and returned, including drugs shipped to participants' homes.
- Whether the decentralized locations have the time, equipment and staff to conduct the trial in line with good clinical practice (<Tooltip tip="International standard for design, conduct, monitoring, recording, and reporting of clinical studies." headline="Good Clinical Practice (GCP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#gcp">GCP</Tooltip>).
- Whether activities that cross provincial borders bring other legislation, regulatory requirements or professional practice requirements into play.
- How data will be managed and protected, and whether the technology could create barriers for some participants.
- Language, culturally appropriate communication and the effect on participating communities.

First-in-human and other early-phase studies generally need more in-person oversight than studies of a drug with an established safety profile.

Where a population may find electronic consent or virtual visits hard, [consideration should be made](/kb/recruitment/consent#obtain-consent-remotely-or-electronically) of whether the method used to obtain or document consent would impede a participant's capability to consent or assent.

## Choose a single site or several sites

When a trial will reach participants across a wide area, it can run as one MUHC site with decentralized elements, or as a trial with several sites, each with its own QI. The choice shapes the protocol and the Clinical Trial Application (<Tooltip tip="Application to Health Canada for authorization to conduct a drug trial under Division 5 of the Food and Drug Regulations." headline="Clinical Trial Application (CTA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#cta">CTA</Tooltip>).

| Point to compare | One site with decentralized elements | Several sites |
|---|---|---|
| Suited to | For example, a drug with an established safety profile, or complex first assessments done centrally with follow-ups at remote locations, including participants' homes. | For example, larger cohorts, populations that need several sites, or procedures that must be done in person at each site. |
| QI oversight | One QI oversees all trial activities, at every location of the site. | Each QI oversees the activities of their own site. |
| Delegation log | The QI keeps one <Tooltip tip="Lists each team member, their qualifications, and specific trial tasks authorized by the QI." headline="Task Delegation Log (TDL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tdl">Task Delegation Log</Tooltip> for the site, including the decentralized activities. | Each QI keeps a delegation log for their own site. |
| REB approval | One REB approval covers the site, including all its locations and decentralized elements. | REB approval is needed for each site. In Quebec's public network, one evaluating REB reviews the study for every participating institution, and each institution then authorizes it. Sites in other provinces submit to their own REBs, or may share a single review through CanReview if they take part in it. No Quebec REB takes part in CanReview, as of November 2026. |
| Health Canada | One site's information in the CTA. | Each site's information is submitted to Health Canada under the same protocol. |
| Across provinces | Activities that cross provincial borders may bring other provincial laws and requirements for the single QI. | Each site adapts to its own province's laws and its institution's capacity, which adds coordination across sites. |

[Multi-Centric Studies in Quebec](/kb/design/multi-centric) covers studies at several institutions of Quebec's network, including CATALIS's FAST TRACK Evaluation Service for industry-sponsored trials.

## Describe the decentralized elements in the submissions

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Studies that need Health Canada authorization</span></div>

For a drug trial, Health Canada's guidance sets what the CTA and the protocol carry about decentralized elements. When designing the trial, sponsors must demonstrate that:

- Participant risk is minimized and safety will be appropriately monitored.
- Participants are made aware of the potential risks and benefits of taking part.
- The decentralized elements will help achieve the study objectives.

Sponsors must attest that the trial, including all its decentralized elements, will be conducted according to good clinical practices. The informed consent forms filed with the CTA should include the potential risks and anticipated benefits of the planned decentralized elements, if known at the time of submission. Sponsors should also describe in the CTA how participant visits and trial activities will be conducted, if that is known. The CTA doesn't need to name third-party services or vendors.

Adding a decentralized element after the CTA is authorized may need an amendment or a notification to Health Canada, depending on whether it changes the protocol and how. [Study Amendments](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/study-amendments) covers both, and [Preparing Your Submission](/kb/submission/preparing) covers the submission to the REB.

## Write the decentralized elements into the protocol

Every decentralized element the REB approves is part of the site's REB approval. Changes to an approved protocol, including added decentralized elements, are not implemented until the REB's written approval and, if the sponsor issues one, its go letter or confirmation are received. A go letter is the sponsor's written permission to implement the change at the site.

A change may be made sooner only in an emergency, where it is important to deviate for participants' safety. The sponsor-investigator and the REB should be notified as soon as possible afterwards.

Consider describing in the protocol:

- Which activities take place at the main MUHC location and which are decentralized.
- Which kinds of location (home, satellite clinic, virtual) each element uses.
- All third parties who deliver care or collect data away from the main site, and how they are contracted and overseen.
- How data are transmitted and protected in transit and at each location.
- The risks of the decentralized elements and how they are managed.
- How participants can opt in or out of each element.

Health Canada's guidance suggests that sponsors may set out, in the protocol, contingencies for adding decentralized elements later, without naming specific third parties. That can avoid a protocol amendment when a vendor changes.

A site that obtains consent electronically follows [the RI-MUHC's rules for electronic consent (eConsent)](/kb/recruitment/consent#obtain-consent-remotely-or-electronically), from the validation of the software to the REB's acknowledgement before use and, in a study regulated by Health Canada, the written work instruction.

## Plan wearables and other digital health technologies

Wearable sensors, electronic patient-reported outcome platforms and other <Tooltip tip="Software or hardware used to collect clinical outcome data in decentralized or hybrid settings, including wearables, ePRO platforms, and connected devices." headline="Digital Health Technology (DHT)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#dht">digital health technologies (DHTs)</Tooltip> used to collect trial data should be fit for their intended purpose. Sponsors and QIs are responsible for ensuring that the computerized systems and digital health technologies supporting decentralized elements are fit for purpose, reliable and suited to the trial design. Sponsors should ensure that they are appropriately validated using a risk-based approach, in line with ICH E6.

Health Canada's [pre-market guidance for machine learning-enabled medical devices](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medical-devices/application-information/guidance-documents/pre-market-guidance-machine-learning-enabled-medical-devices.html), of April 1, 2026, also applies a risk-based approach to determine the evidence a device licence application needs.{/* Restored in b154 by the second reader (the fact check's "Dropped from the live page"; FACTS "Applied: batch 80" and line 1493): the live page said Health Canada "takes a risk-based approach in its pre-market guidance for machine learning-enabled medical devices (April 1, 2026), where what a licence application needs depends on the device's risk". Source: the guidance (saved, _review/2026-11/evidence/web/hc-mlmd-premarket-guidance_read-2026-11-11.html): "Date published: April 1, 2026"; "different intended uses or risk profiles may require different types or levels of evidence"; "Health Canada applies a risk-based approach to determine the evidence requirements." Its approach concerns licence evidence, not trial data collection. */} Being cleared for clinical use doesn't by itself make a device fit for collecting trial data.

Sponsors and QIs should also assess whether a digital tool used in the trial meets the definition of software as a medical device. A tool with a medical purpose, such as diagnosing, treating or monitoring a disease or condition, may be regulated as a medical device and subject to further requirements.

### The three stages of qualifying a digital tool

A common way to show that a digital health technology is fit for purpose has three stages, done in order:

| Stage | What it shows | Example questions |
|---|---|---|
| Verification | The device measures what it claims to measure under defined conditions: its hardware and software work as intended. | Does the accelerometer's output match reference signals? Does the platform record and time-stamp responses accurately? |
| Analytical validation | The measurement represents the biological or clinical phenomenon of interest reliably and accurately, in the study population. | Does heart-rate variability from this wearable agree with ECG-derived heart-rate variability in participants with the target condition? |
| Clinical validation | The measurement is meaningful in the clinical context: it relates to a participant's health or predicts an outcome the protocol cares about. | Does the wearable's signal predict disease progression or response to treatment in the target population and time frame? |

How much validation evidence a tool needs depends on how central its data are to the trial's main endpoints. A wearable that captures exploratory data needs less formal validation than one whose output sets dose changes or measures the main efficacy outcome.

### Before a digital tool goes live

- The investigator defines what counts as a source record, the methods of data capture and their location, before the trial starts.
- For a system the sponsor deploys, the sponsor should ensure that the requirements for computerized systems, such as validation, audit trails, user management, backup, disaster recovery and IT security, are addressed and implemented, and that documented procedures and adequate training are in place.
- For a system the investigator or institution deploys specifically for the trial, the investigator or institution [should ensure that the requirements for computerized systems are addressed](/kb/foundations/gcp-quality-by-design#check-the-computerized-systems-your-study-uses) in proportion to the risks to participants and the importance of the data.
- The sponsor ensures the software is validated and, under the RI-MUHC's SOPs, compliant with 21 CFR Part 11. Ask the vendor or sponsor for the validation documentation, and file a copy in the <Tooltip tip="The QI's collection of documentation demonstrating compliance, study integrity, and participant safety." headline="Investigator Site File (ISF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#isf">Investigator Site File (ISF)</Tooltip>.
- A written agreement covers each third-party provider of a digital tool, defining its roles, activities and responsibilities.
- The protocol and the consent form say how participants will learn to use the device and what happens if it malfunctions or is lost.

The platform should produce a complete, time-stamped audit trail from the day it is activated, and audit trails switched on after data collection began are not acceptable.

Digital tools that generate a trial's main endpoints get the same scrutiny as any other measurement instrument. The sponsor bears the main responsibility for qualifying a tool, and the QI confirms that the qualification documentation is in the site file before data collection begins. Tools for exploratory or secondary endpoints go through a lighter but still documented validation. A device regulated as a Class II, III or IV medical device has its own licensing requirements, which run in parallel and don't replace the tool's qualification for the trial.

<Note>
If the protocol includes a wearable, an electronic patient-reported outcome platform or another digital tool for data collection, involve the [Research Facilitator](mailto:research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca) at the design stage, before the platform is chosen. When you prepare for the sponsor's or sponsor-investigator's site feasibility visit, you should ask RI-MUHC information technology (IT) to evaluate the platform and any e-connectivity issues. The request goes through IT Rescue on the RI-MUHC Portal.

Some platforms commonly used in US trials keep data outside Quebec, which may require a privacy impact assessment (EFVP) before Quebec participants' information can go there.
</Note>

A study's design decides what its results can show. Randomization, blinding and the choice of comparator, taken together, decide which claims a trial can support. The Research Ethics Board (REB) weighs the same choices when it reviews the ethics of a study, and Health Canada when it reviews a trial it must authorize.

## Find what kind of study you have

What a study tests decides the training its team needs and whether Health Canada must authorize it. [Types of Study](/paths) gives the training levels, the cases that need Health Canada's authorization and how a study's design relates to its level.

A drug trial that needs Health Canada's authorization requires a <Tooltip tip="Application to Health Canada for authorization to conduct a drug trial under Division 5 of the Food and Drug Regulations." headline="Clinical Trial Application (CTA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#cta">Clinical Trial Application (CTA)</Tooltip>. A study of a Class II, III or IV medical device may need an <Tooltip tip="Health Canada's authorization for a manufacturer or importer to sell a Class II, III or IV medical device to qualified investigators for testing." headline="Investigational Testing Authorization (ITA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#ita">investigational testing authorization (ITA)</Tooltip>, [depending on the device's licence and the study](/kb/governance/laws-regulations#check-whether-a-device-study-needs-an-investigational-testing-authorization). A trial of a marketed drug within the conditions of its notice of compliance or its drug identification number needs no CTA. Its sponsor must still conduct it in accordance with Division 5 of the Food and Drug Regulations, including good clinical practices, labelling and REB approval.

The RI-MUHC's standard operating procedures (SOPs) require good clinical practice (GCP) training for each kind of study that Health Canada regulates. Teams on drug and natural health product studies train on guideline E6 of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH). Drug studies also require Health Canada Division 5 training.

Teams on medical device studies train on the International Organization for Standardization's (ISO) standard ISO 14155, or on ICH E6 if the sponsor cannot provide ISO 14155 training. [Training and Compliance Requirements](/training/compliance-requirements#training-requirements-by-study-level) gives the details.

A chart review needs neither a CTA nor an ITA. It still needs REB review, and a privacy impact assessment (<Tooltip tip="The privacy impact assessment that Quebec's Act respecting health and social services information requires before a study accesses identifiable health information without consent, including for pre-screening." headline="EFVP (privacy impact assessment)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#efvp">EFVP</Tooltip>) when it uses identifiable health information without consent.

The *Tri-Council Policy Statement* (TCPS 2), ICH E6(R3) and Division 5 of the Food and Drug Regulations can all apply to one drug trial. Where they differ, the most stringent requirement applies.

## Tell interventional and observational studies apart

An interventional study assigns participants to a health-related intervention, such as a drug, a device, a procedure or a change in behaviour, and measures its effect. The investigator changes what happens to participants beyond their standard care. Clinical trials are perhaps the most regulated kind of research. Before any human testing, the sponsor must show that the risk of the intervention is justified.

Because the study adds something to participants' lives, the consent must cover the specific risks of the intervention. Participants must be able to withdraw without consequence to their care, and safety monitoring must be in place throughout.

An observational study doesn't change participants' care. The investigators collect and analyze data, from existing records, biological samples, questionnaires or direct observation, without assigning any treatment. Cohort, case-control and cross-sectional studies are common forms. An observational study needs no CTA or ITA, but it still needs the REB's approval. In Quebec, a study that uses identifiable health information before or without consent also needs an EFVP.

## Know what blinding hides, and from whom

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Medical device and surgery studies (Level II)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Prospective studies not regulated by Health Canada (Level IV)</span></div>

People who know which treatment they receive, or give, can change the outcome without meaning to. A participant who knows they received the active drug may report feeling better whatever the drug's effect. An investigator who knows a participant's group may assess the outcome differently. Blinding hides the treatment assignment from some or all of the people involved.

Random and blinded assignment does not change the consent requirements, as long as participants are told the probability of their assignment to each group of the trial. Blinding hides which treatment a participant receives. It doesn't hide that the trial has more than one treatment.

| Design | Participant | Investigator | Data analyst | When it is used |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Open-label | Knows | Knows | Knows | When blinding is impractical, such as surgery, device implantation or a change in lifestyle. |
| Single-blind | Blinded | Knows | Knows | Usually the participant alone doesn't know the assignment. |
| Double-blind | Blinded | Blinded | Knows | Neither the participant nor the investigator knows. The research pharmacy keeps the allocation code. Most Phase II and III drug trials use this design. |
| Triple-blind | Blinded | Blinded | Blinded | All three are blinded. The allocation code is held independently, often by the <Tooltip tip="Independent committee that reviews interim safety and efficacy data and recommends whether to continue, modify, or stop the study." headline="Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#dsmb">Data Safety Monitoring Board</Tooltip>, until the protocol's criteria for unblinding are met. |

### Unblind a participant

Emergency unblinding happens when a participant has a serious <Tooltip tip="Any untoward medical occurrence in a participant, whether or not related to the investigational product." headline="Adverse Event (AE)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#ae">adverse event</Tooltip> and the treating physician needs to know what they received to treat them safely. Planned unblinding happens at an interim analysis set in advance or at the end of the study. The protocol must define both in advance, and the team follows the protocol's requirements for unblinding.

In an investigator-blinded trial, the investigator should ensure that the randomization code is broken only as the protocol allows. In an emergency, to protect a participant's safety, the investigator should be prepared and capable from the start of the trial to unblind without undue delay and hindrance.

[Promptly document and explain to the sponsor any premature unblinding](/sops/cr-010#5-8-3), whether accidental or because of a serious adverse event, and inform the REB as its procedures require. File the unblinding documentation with the study's essential documents.

## Randomize to make the groups comparable

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Medical device and surgery studies (Level II)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Prospective studies not regulated by Health Canada (Level IV)</span></div>

Randomization assigns participants to groups by chance, not by anyone's choice. It spreads known and unknown factors evenly across the groups, so that the groups are comparable at the start. Without it, a difference between groups at the end may come from differences that existed before the trial, not from the treatment.

| Assignment | How it works | What to weigh |
|---|---|---|
| Randomized | A random process assigns participants: a computer-generated sequence, sealed envelopes or central randomization through an interactive response technology (IRT) system. No one involved in care or in assessing outcomes chooses who receives which treatment. | In a study of interventions meant to benefit participants, the relevant expert community must be genuinely uncertain about which intervention is most effective (clinical equipoise). The researcher must justify the control group to the REB, including its consistency with clinical equipoise. |
| Non-randomized | The investigator, the participant's preference, the clinical presentation or another non-random criterion decides the group. | Useful when randomization is impractical or unethical, such as comparing patients who were or weren't exposed to a risk factor that can't be assigned. The groups are more open to confounding: differences at the start that may explain the outcome on their own. |

Simple randomization can leave groups unbalanced by chance, especially in small trials. Stratified randomization keeps key variables such as age, disease severity or site balanced, by randomizing separately within each stratum. Adaptive randomization changes the chances of assignment as data accumulate, for example to assign more participants to the group that appears to do better. Health Canada accepts both under its standard CTA process.

## Choose the comparator

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Medical device and surgery studies (Level II)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Prospective studies not regulated by Health Canada (Level IV)</span></div>

A control is what the treatment under study is compared with. The choice of comparator is both a scientific and an ethical decision, and it decides what the results can claim. Under [TCPS 2](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter11-chapitre11.html), a new therapy should generally be tested against an established effective therapy.

A placebo control is ethically acceptable in a randomized controlled trial only if all four of these conditions are met:

- Its use is scientifically and methodologically sound in establishing the efficacy or safety of the therapy tested.
- It does not compromise the safety or health of participants.
- The researcher gives the REB a compelling scientific justification for it.
- The general principles of consent are respected, and participants or their authorized third parties are told specifically what intervention or therapy will be withdrawn or withheld for the research, and the expected consequences.

Health Canada uses the ICH guideline E10 as its guidance on the choice of control groups. Each kind of comparator has its uses and limits:

| Comparator | What it is | When it fits, and its limits |
|---|---|---|
| Placebo | An intervention made to look like the treatment under study, with no active components. | TCPS 2 accepts a placebo in five situations, among them when no established effective therapy exists, and when patients with decision-making capacity have given an informed refusal of the established therapy and withholding it will cause no serious or irreversible harm. The informed refusal must be determined outside recruitment for the trial and before the trial is offered, and it must be documented. |
| Active comparator | The current established effective treatment. | ICH E10 considers a placebo control generally inappropriate when an available treatment is known to prevent serious harm, such as death or irreversible morbidity. Placebo-controlled trials without an active control give little useful information about comparative effectiveness. A treatment shown better than placebo only hasn't been shown better than, or equivalent to, the treatment already available. |
| None (single-arm trial) | All participants receive the treatment under study. | Common in early Phase I safety studies, and in rare diseases where too few patients exist for a parallel control group. A single-arm trial can't show whether the treatment is better than existing ones without outside comparison data. |
| Crossover | Each participant receives more than one treatment in turn, and serves as their own control. | Needs fewer participants and controls for differences between people. A washout period between treatments prevents carry-over effects. Fits chronic conditions with stable outcomes, not acute treatments or irreversible interventions. |
| External control arm | Comparison data from outside the trial, such as earlier studies, registries or real-world data, instead of a control group enrolled in the trial. | See the section below. |

### Use an external control arm

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Medical device and surgery studies (Level II)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Prospective studies not regulated by Health Canada (Level IV)</span></div>

Under ICH E10, which Health Canada uses as its guidance on control groups, an externally controlled trial should generally be considered only when two conditions are met. Belief in the superiority of the treatment under study to all available alternatives is so strong that other designs appear unacceptable. The disease or condition to be treated has a well-documented, highly predictable course.

Within those two conditions, external controls are most accepted when a randomized control group isn't feasible: too few patients for a parallel group, ethical barriers to randomization, or a rare disease whose natural history is well documented and that has no standard therapy. Their limits make the evidence less certain than a concurrent randomized control, and regulators require a strong justification:

- Selection bias: the external controls may differ systematically from the enrolled participants.
- Time bias: the standard of care may have changed between when the external controls were treated and when the study runs.
- Missing data: real-world sources rarely capture every endpoint the protocol needs.

The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) published a [draft guidance on externally controlled trials](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/considerations-design-and-conduct-externally-controlled-trials-drug-and-biological-products) in February 2023. It covers the quality and relevance of the data, the similarity of the patients, comparability over time, the alignment of the estimand (the precise treatment effect the trial sets out to estimate) and sensitivity analyses specified in advance.

Health Canada has no guidance specific to external control arms, and applies its general Division 5 framework. Both the REB and Health Canada scrutinize the scientific reasons for choosing an external control over a randomized parallel group.

If your protocol plans an external control arm, contact the [Biostatistics Consulting Unit (BCU)](/kb/design-support/bcu) and the [Research Facilitator](mailto:research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca) while you design it. The statistical approach and the choice of external data source must be specified before submission.

## Plan a master protocol

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Studies that need Health Canada authorization</span></div>

A master protocol is one overall protocol that evaluates one or more interventions in one or more disease subgroups, with shared infrastructure. Master protocols are used where a classic parallel-group randomized trial is inefficient, in oncology, rare diseases and precision medicine in particular. ICH E6(R3) and [ICH E8(R1)](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/E8-R1_Guideline_Step4_2021_1006.pdf) both recognize complex innovative designs.

| Design | Structure | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Basket trial | One investigational agent tested in several disease subgroups at once, often sharing a biomarker or a molecular target. | Oncology: a targeted therapy across tumour types that share a driver mutation, such as an NTRK fusion in lung, colorectal and salivary gland cancers. |
| Umbrella trial | Several investigational agents tested in one disease, with participants assigned by biomarker profile. | Oncology: patients with one cancer screened for several molecular targets, then assigned to the matching treatment group. |
| Platform trial | A long-running adaptive structure that lets treatments enter and leave by rules set in advance, while the trial continues. | The RECOVERY trial. Pandemic preparedness. Diseases with several candidate therapies and stable patient populations. |

These designs share three features:

- **Shared infrastructure.** Participants enrol once and a central system assigns them. Shared control groups across comparisons need careful statistical justification.
- **Adaptive rules.** Most master protocols set decision rules in advance: interim analyses that can add or drop groups, change the sample size or change the allocation ratio. The decision rules must be fully specified before the first participant is enrolled. Changing the statistical plan after the fact is a problem of data integrity.
- **Statistical approach.** Adaptive master protocols often use Bayesian methods, which update the probability of success as data accumulate. Health Canada accepts both Bayesian and frequentist approaches. The [BCU](/kb/design-support/bcu) can advise on which suits your study.

Health Canada has no separate pathway for master protocols: they go through its standard Division 5 CTA process. In the CTA, the statistical analysis plan must be complete and specified in advance, with every decision rule for adaptations. Shared control groups must be statistically justified. Each subgroup may need its own consent requirements, if its biomarker changes the balance of risks and benefits for it.

The REB assesses whether the adaptive design keeps clinical equipoise across all groups throughout the trial, and whether the consent documents describe the possibility of groups changing or opening while a participant is in the trial. Consider involving the [BCU](/kb/design-support/bcu) and the [Research Facilitator](mailto:research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca) while you design the protocol, before the CTA or the REB submission.

## Read the design in a protocol

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Medical device and surgery studies (Level II)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Prospective studies not regulated by Health Canada (Level IV)</span></div>

Every protocol you work with states its design: whether the study is interventional, how participants are assigned, who is blinded and what the comparator is. The design elements are the basis on which the REB evaluates the ethics of the study and Health Canada its application. When you read a new protocol, look for:

| Element | What to look for |
|---|---|
| The kind of study | Is it interventional or observational? Does it involve an investigational drug or device, so that it needs a CTA or an ITA? |
| Randomization | How are participants assigned? What system manages the randomization, and who holds the allocation sequence? |
| Blinding | Who is blinded, and who holds the code? What is the emergency unblinding procedure? |
| Comparator | What is the control group? Is a placebo justified? Does the comparator reflect the current standard of care? |

In a double-blind trial, coordinators and research nurses don't know what a participant received unless a clinical reason calls for unblinding. Visit notes and adverse event reports must not reveal the treatment assignment by accident.

## Know what changes for a device study

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Medical device and surgery studies (Level II)</span></div>

Device studies follow the standard ISO 14155 for good clinical practice, rather than ICH E6(R3). Blinding is often impractical, since a participant knows whether they received an implant or a sham procedure. A device's design may change during development, through the amendment process, and its risks differ from a drug's. At the start of a device study, check which standard applies: monitoring, documentation and safety reporting differ from a drug trial's.

## Know how a multicentre study is reviewed

A study at more than one public institution in Quebec's health and social services network is reviewed by one evaluating REB for all the sites. Each other site accepts that review, but must still complete its own institutional feasibility review and obtain authorization from its Personne mandatée. The sites share the ethics review, and each site authorizes the study itself. [Multicentre Studies](/kb/design/multi-centric) explains the steps.

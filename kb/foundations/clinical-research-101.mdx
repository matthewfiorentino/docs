Research rests on rules that grew out of past harm to participants. Several sets of rules apply to the same study at once: international guidelines, federal law and policy, and Quebec law. Knowing how they fit together makes the RI-MUHC's standard operating procedures (SOPs), a submission and the work with participants easier to follow.

## Tell research apart from clinical care

Clinical care and clinical research often happen in the same places, with the same patients and the same health care professionals. Both can lead to someone receiving a treatment. Their purposes differ.

Clinical care serves the patient being treated. Each decision is made in that person's best interest, from the best available evidence. Clinical research aims to produce knowledge that will apply to future patients, beyond the people enrolled.

| | Clinical care | Clinical research |
|---|---|---|
| Who it serves | The patient being treated | Future patients |
| How it uses evidence | Applies the best available evidence | Produces new evidence |
| Its main goal | The patient's benefit | Knowledge that can be generalized |
| The relationship | A clinician treats a patient | A researcher studies a participant |

Patients asked to join a study may assume that what they receive is part of their treatment. This misunderstanding is called therapeutic misconception. The federal research agencies' [*Tri-Council Policy Statement* (TCPS 2)](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter11-chapitre11.html) describes it as participants not understanding that research aims mainly at producing knowledge and may not benefit them.

TCPS 2 [defines research](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter2-chapitre2.html) as an undertaking intended to extend knowledge through a disciplined inquiry or systematic investigation. Quality improvement and program evaluation are not research under TCPS 2 when they are used only for assessment, management or improvement. [Is It Research?](/is-it-research) helps you check a project.

At the MUHC, research involving human participants, their data or their biological material is clinical research. The MUHC governs it under its [*Regulatory Framework for Clinical Research at Santé Québec – McGill University Health Centre: Organization and Good Practices*](https://rimuhc.ca/conduct-research/policies#framework).

Drug trials, device studies, chart reviews, surveys, biobank studies and studies of educational interventions can all be clinical research. TCPS 2 counts as participants the people whose data, biological materials or responses to the researcher's interventions or questions are relevant to answering the research question.

A study that uses only existing records, data or samples is clinical research. A study that develops or tests software, such as an app or an algorithm, with participants or their data is clinical research too.

## Find what kind of study you have

What a study tests, and how it gets its data, decide the training its team needs, whether Health Canada must authorize it and which steps it follows. [Types of Study](/paths) defines the training levels, Health Canada's authorization, the designs and the sponsors, and links each to its steps.

The Research Ethics Board (REB) reviews every study, with a review proportionate to its risk. A full board meeting is the default. Research of no more than minimal risk may receive a [delegated review](/kb/submission/submission-overview), by the REB Chair or by one or more qualified REB members the Chair designates. A new project that could interfere with the integrity of a minor or of an adult who cannot consent cannot receive a delegated review at its initial review.

A study that uses identifiable health information without the participants' consent also needs an approved privacy impact assessment, the <Tooltip tip="The privacy impact assessment, or évaluation des facteurs relatifs à la vie privée, that Quebec's Act respecting health and social services information requires with a researcher's request for access to health and social services information without consent, including access before consent for pre-screening." headline="EFVP (privacy impact assessment)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#efvp">évaluation des facteurs relatifs à la vie privée (EFVP)</Tooltip>. [Whether a study needs an EFVP](/kb/governance/privacy#know-when-an-efvp-is-needed) depends on how it uses the information.

## Know the phases of a drug trial

Before a drug reaches its first human participant, it has usually gone through years of laboratory and animal research. That pre-clinical work gives the first safety profile, which regulators and REBs weigh before the first human dose.

[Health Canada's guidance for clinical trial sponsors](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/applications-submissions/guidance-documents/clinical-trials/guidance-clinical-trial-sponsors-clinical-trial-applications.html) defines four phases of drug trials:

| Phase | What it studies | Usual size |
|---|---|---|
| Phase I | The drug's pharmacokinetics and pharmacological actions, and the side effects of increasing doses. Phase I trials are generally conducted in healthy volunteers, but may enrol patients when giving the drug to healthy volunteers is not ethical, as in many oncology trials. | 20 to 80 participants |
| Phase II | The drug's efficacy in patients with the condition to be treated, diagnosed or prevented, and its side effects and risks. Phase IIa trials often establish proof of concept, and Phase IIb trials refine the dose. | 100 to 400 participants |
| Phase III | Additional information on clinical efficacy and safety, under the proposed conditions of use, after preliminary evidence suggests efficacy. These trials give the main evidence for a submission to market the drug. | Hundreds to several thousand participants |
| Phase IV | Any study within the approved indication after the drug is approved for the market, such as safety studies. | Varies |

A study of interventions intended to benefit participants must start from clinical equipoise: a genuine uncertainty in the relevant expert community about which intervention is most effective.

### Know when Health Canada must authorize a drug trial

When Health Canada finds the evidence in a drug submission sufficient, it issues a Notice of Compliance (NOC), and a Drug Identification Number (DIN) is assigned.

Health Canada must authorize a drug trial before it starts, unless the trial uses a marketed drug within the conditions of its notice of compliance or its drug identification number. Health Canada's guidance calls those trials Phase IV trials. ## Know the roles in a study

A study at an academic medical centre involves many people, each with documented responsibilities. The main roles are these:

| Role | Who holds it | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| Principal investigator (PI) | The person who leads the study at the MUHC. In a drug trial regulated by Health Canada, the <Tooltip tip="The person responsible to the sponsor for the conduct of a drug or natural health product trial at a clinical trial site, who is entitled to provide health care under the laws of the province where the site is located." headline="Qualified Investigator (QI)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#qi">qualified investigator</Tooltip> is entitled to provide health care under the laws of the province where the site is located. The qualified investigator is a physician, or a physician or dentist for a drug used only for dental purposes. The sponsor must ensure that each site has no more than one qualified investigator. Since September 14, 2026, Health Canada's interim policy also accepts a person who is not a physician or dentist, if they meet [its conditions](/kb/governance/laws-regulations#know-what-health-canada-is-changing). At the MUHC, [the RI-MUHC's SOPs require Human Research Privileges](/sops/cr-500#4-0-definitions) of the qualified investigator. [Privileges](/training/compliance-requirements#know-which-credential-you-need) are for physicians and dentists who are members of the MUHC's Council of Physicians, Dentists, Pharmacists and Midwives. | Leads the study and delegates tasks, with each delegation documented. The investigator retains the ultimate responsibility for the delegated tasks. In an investigator-initiated trial, the principal investigator is often also the sponsor, and takes on the sponsor's duties too. In a drug trial that needs Health Canada's authorization, the sponsor's duties include filing the CTA, arranging monitoring and ensuring that the <Tooltip tip="A pharmaceutical form of an active ingredient or placebo, a medical device or a natural health product being tested or used as a reference in a clinical trial." headline="Investigational Product (IP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#ip">investigational product</Tooltip> meets good manufacturing practices. |
| Clinical research coordinator (CRC) | A member of the study team. No order regulates the coordinator's profession in Canada: the institution and the <Tooltip tip="The investigator's list of the appropriately qualified people to whom the investigator has delegated significant study-related tasks." headline="Task Delegation Log (TDL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tdl">Task Delegation Log</Tooltip> set what a coordinator may do. | Runs the study day to day: scheduling, documentation, data capture, participants' visits, regulatory filings and REB submissions. A coordinator who is not a member of a professional order cannot perform activities that Quebec's laws reserve to members of professional orders, such as taking blood specimens or administering prescribed medications. |
| Research nurse | A nurse licensed by the Ordre des infirmières et infirmiers du Québec (OIIQ). | Performs the clinical tasks delegated to them, within the activities the Nurses Act reserves to nurses. Often the person participants see most often during the study. |
| Clinical research associate (monitor) | A person employed by or contracted to the sponsor, not the site. | Visits or reviews the site to verify source documents, consent documentation and protocol adherence. Under guideline E6(R3), the sponsor's monitoring is risk-based, and remote and centralized review can complement on-site visits. |
| Research Ethics Board (REB) | An independent committee. | Reviews every research protocol before any participant is approached. |
| Research pharmacist | A pharmacist. | Manages the investigational product: receipt, storage, dispensing and accounting for every unit. Maintains blinding when the protocol requires it, and takes part in site initiation and monitoring visits. |

Under [TCPS 2](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter6-chapitre6.html), an REB has at least five members, both men and women, among them at least two members with expertise in the relevant research disciplines, one member knowledgeable in ethics, one member knowledgeable in the relevant law and one community member with no affiliation with the institution.

The member knowledgeable in the law should not be the institution's legal counsel or risk manager. An institution may not override an REB's decision to reject a research proposal. Research with minors or with adults who cannot consent, if it could interfere with their integrity, must be approved and monitored by an REB that Quebec's health minister has formed or designated.

In an industry-sponsored trial of moderate complexity, more than twenty people may carry documented responsibilities before the first participant signs a consent form.

## Know where the rules came from

Research ethics rules grew out of specific failures, where the absence of oversight led to harm. Each milestone below added a principle that still applies:

| Year | Milestone | What it added |
|---|---|---|
| 1947 | The Nuremberg Code, from the Nuremberg Doctors' Trial | Ten principles for ethical research, the first of which is that the voluntary consent of the participant is absolutely essential. |
| 1964 | The World Medical Association's [Declaration of Helsinki](https://www.wma.net/policies-post/wma-declaration-of-helsinki/) | A framework for physicians who conduct research. Its 1975 revision introduced review by an independent ethics committee. Its core principle is that the participant's welfare takes precedence over the interests of science and society. It has been revised eight times, most recently in 2024. |
| 1979 | The Belmont Report, in the United States, after the Tuskegee Syphilis Study became public | Three principles: respect for persons, beneficence and justice. |
| 1996 | The International Council for Harmonisation's (ICH) good clinical practice guideline, E6 | One standard for clinical trials across the United States, the European Union and Japan. Health Canada adopted it for drug trials regulated under Division 5. |
| 1998 | The first *Tri-Council Policy Statement* | Canada's national ethics policy. Its current edition is TCPS 2 (2022). |
| 2016 | ICH E6(R2) | An addendum that brought in risk-based quality management and recognized electronic clinical data. |
| 2025 | ICH E6(R3), in effect in Canada since April 1, 2026 | Quality built into the design of a trial: the critical-to-quality factors identified before the trial begins, and controls proportionate to the risks. E6(R3) also covers risk-based monitoring, electronic data and decentralized elements. |

TCPS 2 rests on three core principles of its own: respect for persons, concern for welfare and justice. Institutions that receive funds from the three federal research agencies (the Canadian Institutes of Health Research, the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council and the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council) agree to apply it, so compliance is a condition of federal funding.{/* Correction: the old page said the Belmont principles "directly shaped Canada's TCPS2 and remain its organizing framework" (wave 2 C25: TCPS 2 never names Belmont or "beneficence"). */}

## Know what Quebec adds

Quebec adds rules on what federal policy leaves to the provinces: consent under civil law, the governance of the public health network, and privacy rules for its institutions. [Laws, Regulations and Guidelines](/kb/governance/laws-regulations) explains each.

### Three reviews before MUHC Authorization

A study at the MUHC needs three favourable reviews: a scientific review, an ethics review and an institutional feasibility review. They run in parallel. [Study Submission and Review](/kb/submission/submission-overview) explains each.

- **Scientific review.** It checks the study's scientific relevance and validity. The REB may recognize an earlier review by a recognized peer review committee, such as the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) or the Fonds de recherche du Québec (FRQ). A peer-reviewed funded project is not reviewed again at the MUHC, as long as documentation of its peer review is provided. Otherwise, the REB's scientific members review the study.
- **Ethics review.** The REB reviews the study, with a review proportionate to its risk. Continuing review is required at least once a year.
- **Institutional feasibility review.** It checks whether the MUHC can do the study: its patients, clinical capacity, pharmacy, resources, contract and legal review, and the EFVP where the study needs one.

The <Tooltip tip="The person whom an establishment of Quebec's health network formally mandates to authorize the research conducted under its auspices." headline="Personne mandatée" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#personne-mandatee">Personne mandatée</Tooltip> issues <Tooltip tip="The MUHC's final authorization for a study to begin, which the Personne mandatée issues once the scientific, ethics and institutional feasibility reviews are favourable and, where the study needs it, Health Canada has authorized it." headline="MUHC Authorization" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#muhc-auth">MUHC Authorization</Tooltip> once all three reviews are favourable. At the MUHC, the Personne mandatée is the person to whom the President and Chief Executive Officer has delegated, in writing, the authorization of research projects. Quebec's health ministry requires every establishment in its network to formally authorize all the research under its auspices.

<Warning>
Do not start any research activity, including recruitment, data collection or analysis, until you have both REB approval and MUHC Authorization.

### Rules of the Civil Code on research

Quebec's [Civil Code](https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/CCQ-1991) sets rules for research that could interfere with a person's integrity:

- A capable adult may take part only if the risk is not disproportionate to the benefit that can reasonably be expected. The project must be approved and monitored by a research ethics committee.
- A minor or an adult who cannot consent may take part only if the risk, given their state of health and personal condition, is not disproportionate to the benefit that may reasonably be anticipated.
- If a minor or an adult who cannot consent is the only subject of the research, it must have the potential to benefit their health. If the research is on a group, it must have the potential to produce results that benefit people in the same age group or with the same disease or disability.
- A minor or an adult who cannot consent, and who understands the nature and consequences of the research and objects to taking part, cannot take part, whoever has consented for them. [Who consents for them](/kb/recruitment/consent#who-can-consent) depends on their age and situation. The research must be approved and monitored by a research ethics committee that Quebec's health minister has formed or designated.
- A participant may receive an indemnity for the loss and inconvenience they suffer, and no other financial reward.

National and international ethics guidance allows payment for taking part, such as payment to healthy volunteers in Phase I trials, but at the MUHC it doesn't override the Civil Code's limit. Participants should receive [compensation for the loss and restriction they suffer](/sops/cr-007#5-7-4), on condition that it is not an undue inducement and is paid pro rata.

### Privacy laws in Quebec

Two Quebec laws protect personal information. Law 25 (2021, most provisions in force since September 22, 2023) amended Quebec's public-sector access act and its private-sector privacy act. For health and social services information held by the MUHC, Law 5, the Act respecting health and social services information, applies instead.

To use health records without consent, a researcher attached to the MUHC needs the REB's decision, an approved EFVP and MUHC Authorization. Researchers not attached to a health and social services body apply to Santé Québec's research access centre. Start the EFVP early, while you plan the study. [Privacy and the EFVP](/kb/governance/privacy) explains the steps.

## Keep consent going through the study

Consent is a process, not only a signature. Under [TCPS 2](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter3-chapitre3.html), consent must be free, informed and ongoing, so that participation stays voluntary throughout the study. Consent must also be documented, in a signed consent form or in the researcher's documentation of another appropriate means of consent. In Quebec, [consent to research that could interfere with a person's integrity is given in writing, unless the REB finds that the circumstances justify another way](/kb/recruitment/consent#obtain-consent-before-any-study-procedure).

Participants may withdraw their consent at any time, without giving a reason. Their pre-existing entitlements to care should not be prejudiced by their decision to take part or to withdraw.

Consent does not last unchanged. If important new information that may be relevant to their consent becomes available, it must be communicated to participants in a timely manner, and the REB decides whether they must be re-consented. Initial consent covers participation as the participant understood it at the time. It doesn't cover participation under conditions that did not yet exist.

TCPS 2 lists the information researchers must give prospective participants, as appropriate to the study. It includes:

- The purpose of the research, in plain language.
- What taking part involves: the procedures, the expected duration and the participant's responsibilities.
- The reasonably foreseeable risks and potential benefits.
- That participants are under no obligation to take part and may withdraw at any time without prejudice to pre-existing entitlements.
- What information will be collected about participants, for what purposes, and who will have access to it.
- Whom to contact with questions about the research, and whom to contact outside the research team about ethical issues.

At the MUHC, a consent form needs [an English and a French version](/kb/recruitment/consent#prepare-the-consent-form), unless the REB accepts a justification. Never use a translated or modified <Tooltip tip="The written form that gives a participant the information essential to an informed decision about taking part in a study." headline="Informed Consent Form (ICF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#icf">consent form</Tooltip> without the REB's approval.

Researchers and coordinators who work closely with participants can underestimate how easily participants confuse research with personal care. Plain language, time for questions and regular checks on understanding help keep the difference clear throughout the study.

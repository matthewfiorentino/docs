Good clinical practice protects participants and makes a study's data reliable enough to support decisions. Since April 1, 2026, Health Canada applies the newest version of the international guideline, E6(R3). It asks sponsors and investigators to decide, before a trial begins, what matters most to its quality, and to focus their controls there.

## Know what good clinical practice is

<Tooltip tip="International standard for design, conduct, monitoring, recording, and reporting of clinical studies." headline="Good Clinical Practice (GCP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#gcp">Good clinical practice (GCP)</Tooltip> is an international standard for the design, conduct, monitoring, recording and reporting of clinical studies. It has two purposes: to protect the rights, safety and well-being of participants, and to make the data reliable enough to support regulatory decisions. Data that can't be trusted can't support a decision, and a study that harms participants has no ethical basis whatever its scientific value.

GCP grew out of past failures. The Nuremberg Code (1947), the [Declaration of Helsinki](https://www.wma.net/policies-post/wma-declaration-of-helsinki/) (1964) and the Belmont Report (1979) set out its ethical principles. The International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) first published its GCP guideline, E6, in 1996. It turned those principles into requirements that sponsors, investigators and regulators in its member countries could apply the same way. Regulators in Canada, the United States and Europe could then accept clinical data from studies run under shared standards. ## Know which GCP standard applies to your study

The RI-MUHC's standard operating procedures (SOPs) set the GCP training for each kind of study that Health Canada regulates. Teams on drug and natural health product studies train on [ICH E6](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/applications-submissions/guidance-documents/international-council-harmonisation/guidelines.html). Drug studies also require Health Canada Division 5 training. Teams on medical device studies train on the International Organization for Standardization's (ISO) standard ISO 14155, or on ICH E6 if the sponsor cannot provide ISO 14155 training. [Training and Compliance Requirements](/training/compliance-requirements#training-requirements-by-study-level) gives the details.

Health Canada adopted the ICH GCP guideline for drug trials under Division 5 of the Food and Drug Regulations. For device studies, its guidance on investigational testing expects GCP as set out in ISO 14155. The federal research agencies' [*Tri-Council Policy Statement* (TCPS 2)](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/policy-politique_tcps2-eptc2_2022.html) applies to all research with human participants under the auspices of institutions that receive federal research funds, whatever the kind of study. The standards add up: meeting ICH E6 doesn't meet TCPS 2's ethical requirements, and meeting TCPS 2 doesn't meet ICH E6.

ICH published E6(R2) in 2016, which brought in risk-based monitoring and recognized electronic data systems. E6(R3) was finalized in January 2025, and Health Canada implemented it on April 1, 2026. It carries risk-based thinking through the whole study.

## Know what E6(R3) changed

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span></div>

E6(R3)'s main change is in approach. Earlier versions mostly described what had to be done. E6(R3) asks that a trial's processes be proportionate to the risks to participants and to the importance of the data, and that quality be built into the design of the trial from the start. This approach is called quality by design.

| Area | Before E6(R3) | Under E6(R3) |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | Quality problems found and corrected as they occurred | Risks anticipated before the trial starts, and controls built into its design |
| Monitoring | On-site visits at set intervals, with source data verification of most or all data | Frequency and focus set by the monitoring plan and the site's risks. Remote monitoring recognized. |
| Documentation | A set list of required documents, paper by default | Electronic records and signatures accommodated. Principles rather than set formats. |
| Oversight | Whether the procedures were followed | Whether the critical-to-quality factors, the things that matter most to participants and to the results, were protected |
| Decentralized elements | Not addressed: all activity assumed to happen at a site | Addressed: remote consent, wearable devices, home visits and telemedicine. |

Some obligations did not change: protecting participants, keeping data reliable, reporting safety events, keeping the <Tooltip tip="The QI's collection of documentation demonstrating compliance, study integrity, and participant safety." headline="Investigator Site File (ISF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#isf">investigator site file</Tooltip> current, and an investigator who retains the ultimate responsibility for the trial. The data integrity principles known as ALCOA+, essential records and the reporting deadlines in the RI-MUHC's SOPs all remain.

## Build quality into the design

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span></div>

Quality by design means building the conditions for a good study into it from the start, instead of checking quality afterwards. In the earlier model, the team designed the protocol, had it approved, trained, ran the study and monitored it, and corrected problems as they appeared. Under quality by design, before the study starts, the sponsor identifies what could go wrong, judges which failures would matter most, builds controls to prevent or detect them and sets the limits that call for action.

That work gives three results before the study begins: the critical-to-quality factors, an assessment of their risks and acceptable ranges for them. They go into the protocol, the monitoring plan and the data management plan, so that they work from the first participant's visit. A problem found after data collection is costly to fix, if it can be fixed at all, while the same problem foreseen while the protocol is written costs little to prevent.

## Know the critical-to-quality factors

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span></div>

A critical-to-quality factor is an attribute of a trial that is fundamental to the protection of participants or to the reliability of its results. Not every requirement of a protocol is one. The question is which requirements are so basic that their failure would defeat the study's purpose.

The sponsor or the sponsor-investigator identifies the critical-to-quality factors while the protocol is written. A Phase III cardiovascular trial might name the integrity of informed consent, the measurement of the primary endpoint and the accountability for the <Tooltip tip="A drug, medical device, or natural health product being tested or used as a reference in a clinical trial." headline="Investigational Product (IP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#ip">investigational product</Tooltip>. A chart review has different factors: consent may not be required, but the accuracy and completeness of the data extracted are likely to be critical. Factors that often matter:

| Factor | Why it often matters |
|---|---|
| Eligibility and consent | Enrolling a participant who doesn't meet the eligibility criteria, or whose consent wasn't properly obtained, fails to protect the person the study should serve. |
| The primary endpoint | The primary endpoint is what the study was designed to measure. An assessment done wrongly, outside the protocol's window or by untrained staff compromises the study's main finding. |
| Safety reporting | Serious adverse events that are not identified, assessed or reported correctly leave real harm to participants unrecorded and unanswered. |
| Blinding | In a blinded study, unblinding even one participant by accident can compromise the data. |
| Biological samples | When the primary outcomes depend on samples, as in pharmacokinetic, biomarker or tissue studies, their collection, processing and storage may be critical. A sample frozen at the wrong temperature can't be fixed. |
| Investigational product | In a drug study, dispensing the right dose to the right participant, and accounting for every unit received, dispensed and returned, protect both safety and the dose-response data. |

For each critical-to-quality factor, where relevant, the sponsor should set acceptable ranges in advance, such as quality tolerance limits at the trial level. When a deviation beyond a range is detected, the sponsor should evaluate whether there is a systemic issue and whether action is needed.

For example, a limit could be 5 per cent of participants with a primary endpoint assessment outside the protocol's window. If 5 per cent is reached, the protocol specifies an investigation and a decision on whether the affected data can be included in the analysis. The ranges appear in the monitoring plan, and sometimes in the protocol's statistical section. E6(R3) names them "acceptable ranges", with quality tolerance limits as one example, and sponsors' documents may use either term.

## Work within the quality plan at your site

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span></div>

Most quality-by-design work happens at the sponsor, before the site is involved. When the site receives the protocol and the <Tooltip tip="Compilation of clinical and nonclinical data on the investigational product relevant to the study." headline="Investigator's Brochure (IB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#ib">investigator's brochure</Tooltip>, the critical-to-quality factors and the acceptable ranges are usually set. The site's role is to understand them and work within them.

A protocol written for E6(R3) shows its critical-to-quality factors, openly or not. The procedures given the most detail, the most training at the <Tooltip tip="Monitoring visit to train the site team on the protocol and confirm site readiness, after REB approval and MUHC Authorization." headline="Site Initiation Visit (SIV)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#siv">site initiation visit</Tooltip> and the most checks at monitoring visits are almost always the critical ones. Ask what the study most needs to protect, and why: the answer helps set priorities while the study runs.

- At the site initiation visit, the monitor spends the most time on the critical procedures, which the monitoring plan will check most closely.
- While the study runs, the critical procedures come first. A visit that includes the primary endpoint assessment can't be traded for one that doesn't, and an error in consent is not equivalent to a minor scheduling deviation.
- At monitoring visits under risk-based monitoring, the monitor focuses on the critical factors and on the site's results against the acceptable ranges. A site that manages its critical factors well receives lighter monitoring than one with deviations in critical areas.

### Classify deviations by their effect

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Medical device and surgery studies (Level II)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span></div>

The RI-MUHC's SOPs classify deviations as minor, major or critical. A major deviation may result in undue health risks to participants or others, or could invalidate data. A critical deviation results in fatal, life-threatening or unsafe conditions for participants, and the conduct of unauthorized trials and the falsification of records are critical too. Consider using the critical-to-quality factors when you classify a deviation, since a major or critical deviation usually touches one. [Deviation Management](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/deviation-management) gives the tiers in full.

## Know what risk-based monitoring changes

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span></div>

Risk-based monitoring is the monitoring that quality by design makes possible. Under traditional monitoring, every site was visited at fixed intervals and every data point checked against source documents, whatever the site's performance or the data point's importance. Risk-based monitoring adapts the monitoring to the real risks.

The monitoring plan sets different levels of oversight for different sites and kinds of data. A new team at a new site running a complex protocol receives more monitoring than an experienced site that has run similar studies well. A data point that feeds the primary endpoint is checked more closely than a secondary endpoint or an administrative field.

As a site builds its record, through its deviation log, its results against the acceptable ranges and the quality of its investigator site file, the monitoring is adjusted.

E6(R3) recognizes remote monitoring, a review of data and documents done electronically. At a site with good results and low risk, it may replace or supplement on-site visits.

A site that keeps its investigator site file current, documents deviations promptly, closes monitoring action items on time and manages its critical procedures well receives proportionately lighter monitoring. A site that falls behind receives closer scrutiny, as a proportionate response to risk. Quality by design leaves the site's responsibility unchanged. It changes the question from whether the procedures were followed to whether what matters was protected.

## Check the computerized systems your study uses

E6(R3) addresses computerized systems at the site more fully than earlier versions. Computerized systems used in clinical trials should be fit for purpose. For systems the investigator or institution deploys for a trial, E6(R3) asks that its requirements for computerized systems be met in proportion to the risks to participants and the importance of the data.

When you use computerized systems in a clinical trial, E6(R3) says the investigator or institution should:

- Ensure that the right people have secure and attributable access to the systems the investigator or institution deploys.
- Tell the sponsor when access to a system the sponsor deployed needs to be changed or revoked for someone.
- For systems the investigator or institution deploys for the trial, meet E6(R3)'s requirements for computerized systems in proportion to the risks to participants and the importance of the data.
- Where the investigator provides participants with equipment to collect data, ensure that its traceability is maintained and that participants receive appropriate training.
- Report to the sponsor, and where applicable to the Research Ethics Board (REB), any incident in a system that, in the investigator's or institution's judgement, may have a significant or lasting effect on the trial data or on the system's security.

The RI-MUHC's SOPs give the sponsor or sponsor-investigator the duty to ensure that all electronic software used in a study has been validated and complies with Part 11 of Title 21 of the US Code of Federal Regulations (21 CFR Part 11). In preparation for the sponsor's or sponsor-investigator's site feasibility visit, the study team should [contact IT through IT Rescue](/sops/cr-004#5-1-3) on the RI-MUHC Portal for an evaluation of any study-specific software and an assessment of e-connectivity issues.

For systems the sponsor provides, such as an electronic case report form, an electronic patient-reported outcome tool or a sponsor portal, the sponsor usually holds the validation records. Consider confirming at the site initiation visit that the validation records exist, that the system is used as validated and that each account has the right access.

The RI-MUHC supports REDCap for investigator-initiated electronic case report forms of low or moderate complexity. REDCap is not validated by default: a REDCap database is configured, tested and documented for its use. For a study regulated by Health Canada, ask [Capture and Optimization of Research Data (CORD)](/kb/design-support/cord) for the validation records that cover your use. Inspectors can ask for them.

Consider also answering, for each system the study uses:

- What does the system do in this study?
- What happens if it fails or gives wrong results?
- Is there documented validation, from the vendor or the sponsor?
- Are access controls, an audit trail and backups in place?
- What does the team do if the system is unavailable?

Consider keeping a simple register of the study's systems, one per study, or one per site updated for each study, filed in the investigator site file and reviewed at each monitoring visit:

| Column | What it records |
|---|---|
| System | Its name, such as an electronic case report form, REDCap or a sponsor portal. |
| Use in this study | The data it captures or sends. |
| Validation | Validated by the vendor, the sponsor or the site, or not validated. |
| Audit trail | Whether every change can be traced to a user and a time. |
| Backup and recovery | How often data are backed up, and how they are recovered if the system fails. |
| Access | Who has access, and whether each person's access suits their role. |
| Risk | Low, medium or high, by the consequence of a failure. |

## Delegate tasks and oversee them

E6(R3) asks that delegation and oversight be proportionate, and this changes how a study team works day to day.

The <Tooltip tip="Lists each team member, their qualifications, and specific trial tasks authorized by the QI." headline="Task Delegation Log (TDL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tdl">Task Delegation Log</Tooltip> records delegation, but it is not the delegation itself. The investigator should ensure that each person a task is delegated to is appropriately qualified and adequately informed about the protocol, the investigational product and their tasks. Signing the log documents that assessment, and the assessment must actually take place.

<Warning>
Do not perform a study task until your training for it is documented and the task has been delegated on the Task Delegation Log.
</Warning>

The investigator retains the ultimate responsibility and should oversee the delegated tasks. The level of oversight should depend on the nature of the tasks, and be proportionate to the importance of the data and the risks to participants and to the data. A coordinator with ten years in a therapeutic area may need less oversight for a standard visit than a team member who joined last month.

Consider structuring the investigator's oversight in ways such as these:

- Reviewing primary endpoint assessments, alone or with a qualified sub-investigator.
- Checking consent documentation and source notes regularly.
- Holding a short weekly team meeting on open issues, safety events and upcoming visits.
- Reviewing the deviation log at set intervals and signing off on corrective and preventive actions (CAPA).
- Delegating routine data entry and source documentation to trained coordinators, with periodic review instead of supervision at each visit.

Proportionate oversight doesn't reduce the investigator's responsibility for the study conducted under their name. A finding that a delegated task was done wrongly reflects on the investigator's oversight, not only on the team member who erred. In an inspection, the question is whether the investigator's oversight was proportionate and documented, not whether they did every task themselves.

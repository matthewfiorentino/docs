Good clinical practice protects participants and makes a study's data reliable enough to support decisions. Since April 1, 2026, Health Canada applies the newest version of the international guideline, E6(R3). It asks sponsors and investigators to decide, before a trial begins, what matters most to its quality, and to focus their controls there.

## Know what good clinical practice is

<Tooltip tip="A standard for the design, conduct, performance, monitoring, auditing, recording, analysis and reporting of clinical studies that provides assurance that the data and reported results are credible and accurate, and that the rights, integrity and confidentiality of participants are protected." headline="Good Clinical Practice (GCP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#gcp">Good clinical practice (GCP)</Tooltip> is an international standard for the design, conduct, monitoring, recording and reporting of clinical studies. It has two purposes: to protect the rights, safety and well-being of participants, and to make the data reliable enough to support regulatory decisions. Data that can't be trusted can't support a decision, and a study that harms participants has no ethical basis whatever its scientific value.

GCP grew out of past failures. The Nuremberg Code (1947), the [Declaration of Helsinki](https://www.wma.net/policies-post/wma-declaration-of-helsinki/) (1964) and the Belmont Report (1979) set out its ethical principles. The International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) first published its GCP guideline, E6, in 1996. It turned those principles into requirements that sponsors, investigators and regulators in its member countries could apply the same way. Regulators in Canada, the United States and Europe could then accept clinical data from studies run under shared standards. ## Know which GCP standard applies to your study

Which good clinical practice standard a study follows depends on what it tests. [Health Canada requires the sponsor of a drug or natural health product trial to ensure that it is conducted in accordance with good clinical practices](/kb/governance/laws-regulations#follow-good-clinical-practice-in-a-study-regulated-by-health-canada). [The RI-MUHC's standard operating procedures (SOPs) set the International Organization for Standardization's (ISO) standard ISO 14155 as the good clinical practice standard that all device research should follow](/kb/governance/laws-regulations#follow-good-clinical-practice).

[The RI-MUHC's SOPs set the GCP training for each kind of study that Health Canada regulates](/training/compliance-requirements#training-requirements-by-study-level). Drug studies also require Health Canada Division 5 training.

The federal research agencies' [*Tri-Council Policy Statement* (TCPS 2)](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/policy-politique_tcps2-eptc2_2022.html) applies to all research with human participants under the auspices of institutions that receive federal research funds, whatever the kind of study. The standards add up: meeting ICH E6 doesn't meet TCPS 2's ethical requirements, and meeting TCPS 2 doesn't meet ICH E6.

## Know what E6(R3) changed

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span></div>

E6(R3)'s main change is in approach. Earlier versions mostly described what had to be done. [E6(R3)](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/applications-submissions/guidance-documents/international-council-harmonisation/guidelines.html) asks that a trial's processes be proportionate to the risks to participants and to the importance of the data, and that quality be built into the design of the trial from the start. This approach is called quality by design.

Under quality by design, the sponsor identifies what could go wrong before the study starts and throughout trial conduct, judges which failures would matter most and builds controls to prevent or detect them.

That work gives three results before the study begins: the critical-to-quality factors, an assessment of their risks and, where relevant, acceptable ranges for them. Activities to mitigate those risks may be incorporated, for example, in the protocol and the monitoring plan, so that they work from the first participant's visit.

Some obligations did not change: protecting participants, keeping data reliable, reporting safety events, keeping the <Tooltip tip="The qualified or principal investigator's collection of study documentation that demonstrates compliance with regulations and guidelines, so that the conduct and integrity of the study and the safety of participants at the site can be verified." headline="Investigator Site File (ISF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#isf">investigator site file</Tooltip> current, and an investigator who retains the ultimate responsibility for the trial. The data integrity principles known as ALCOA+, essential records and the reporting deadlines in the RI-MUHC's SOPs all remain.

### Know the critical-to-quality factors

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span></div>

A critical-to-quality factor is an attribute of a trial that is fundamental to the protection of participants or to the reliability of its results. Not every requirement of a protocol is one. The question is which requirements are so basic that their failure would defeat the study's purpose.

The sponsor or the sponsor-investigator identifies the critical-to-quality factors while the protocol is written. A Phase III cardiovascular trial might name the integrity of informed consent, the measurement of the primary endpoint and the accountability for the <Tooltip tip="A pharmaceutical form of an active ingredient or placebo, a medical device or a natural health product being tested or used as a reference in a clinical trial." headline="Investigational Product (IP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#ip">investigational product</Tooltip>. A chart review has different factors: consent may not be required, but the accuracy and completeness of the data extracted are likely to be critical.

For each critical-to-quality factor, where relevant, the sponsor should set acceptable ranges in advance, such as quality tolerance limits at the trial level. When a deviation beyond a range is detected, the sponsor should evaluate whether there is a systemic issue and whether action is needed. E6(R3) names them "acceptable ranges", with quality tolerance limits as one example, and sponsors' documents may use either term.

## Work within the quality plan at your site

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span></div>

Most quality-by-design work happens at the sponsor, before the site is involved. When the site receives the protocol and the <Tooltip tip="A compilation of the clinical and nonclinical data on an investigational product that are relevant to its study in human participants." headline="Investigator's Brochure (IB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#ib">investigator's brochure</Tooltip>, the critical-to-quality factors and the acceptable ranges are usually set. The site's role is to understand them and work within them.

A protocol written for E6(R3) shows its critical-to-quality factors, openly or not. The procedures given the most detail, the most training at the <Tooltip tip="A monitoring visit conducted by a monitor as a final evaluation of the site, and to train the site team on the study protocol and its associated documents and processes." headline="Site Initiation Visit (SIV)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#siv">site initiation visit</Tooltip> and the most checks at monitoring visits are almost always the critical ones. Ask what the study most needs to protect, and why: the answer helps set priorities while the study runs.

- At the site initiation visit, the monitor spends the most time on the critical procedures, which the monitoring plan will check most closely.
- While the study runs, the critical procedures come first. A visit that includes the primary endpoint assessment can't be traded for one that doesn't, and an error in consent is not equivalent to a minor scheduling deviation.
- At monitoring visits under risk-based monitoring, the monitor focuses on the critical factors and on the site's results against the acceptable ranges. A site that manages its critical factors well receives lighter monitoring than one with deviations in critical areas.

## Know what risk-based monitoring changes

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span></div>

Risk-based monitoring is the monitoring that quality by design makes possible. Under traditional monitoring, every site was visited at fixed intervals and every data point checked against source documents, whatever the site's performance or the data point's importance. Under E6(R3), [the sponsor should decide the extent and nature of monitoring based on the risks identified](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/monitoring-audits#risk-based-monitoring-under-ich-e6-r3).

## Check the computerized systems your study uses

Computerized systems used in clinical trials should be fit for purpose. When you use computerized systems in a clinical trial, [E6(R3)](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/applications-submissions/guidance-documents/international-council-harmonisation/guidelines.html) says the investigator or institution should:

- Ensure that the right people have secure and attributable access to the systems the investigator or institution deploys.
- Tell the sponsor when access to a system the sponsor deployed needs to be changed or revoked for someone.
- For systems the investigator or institution deploys for the trial, meet E6(R3)'s requirements for computerized systems in proportion to the risks to participants and the importance of the data.
- Where the investigator provides participants with equipment to collect data, ensure that its traceability is maintained and that participants receive appropriate training.
- Report to the sponsor, and where applicable to the Research Ethics Board (REB), any incident in a system that, in the investigator's or institution's judgement, may have a significant or lasting effect on the trial data or on the system's security.

[The RI-MUHC's SOPs](/sops/cr-004#5-1-3) require the sponsor or sponsor-investigator to make sure that every electronic system used in a study has been validated and complies with Part 11 of Title 21 of the US Code of Federal Regulations (21 CFR Part 11), the US rule on electronic records. [The study team should also ask RI-MUHC information technology to evaluate any software specific to the study](/kb/conduct/data-documentation/data-integrity#know-who-validates-electronic-systems).

For a REDCap database in a study regulated by Health Canada, [ask Capture and Optimization of Research Data (CORD) for the validation records that cover your use](/kb/design-support/cord#use-redcap-for-a-study).

For systems the sponsor provides, such as an electronic case report form, an electronic patient-reported outcome tool or a sponsor portal, the sponsor usually holds the validation records. Consider confirming at the site initiation visit that the validation records exist, that the system is used as validated and that each account has the right access.

## Delegate tasks and oversee them

E6(R3) asks that delegation and oversight be proportionate, and this changes how a study team works day to day.

The investigator retains the ultimate responsibility and should oversee the delegated tasks. The level of oversight should depend on the nature of the tasks, and be proportionate to the importance of the data and the risks to participants and to the data. The investigator should ensure that each person a task is delegated to is appropriately qualified and adequately informed about the protocol, the investigational product and their tasks.

[The RI-MUHC's SOPs](/sops/cr-002#5-6-1) have the <Tooltip tip="The investigator's list of the appropriately qualified people to whom the investigator has delegated significant study-related tasks." headline="Task Delegation Log (TDL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tdl">Task Delegation Log</Tooltip> completed for each team member before they conduct any study task. [The principal investigator's signature on the log is their assurance that the person given the task is qualified and trained to do it](/kb/team/building-your-team#delegate-every-task-on-the-task-delegation-log).

<Warning>
Do not perform a study task until your training for it is documented and the task has been delegated on the Task Delegation Log.
</Warning>

## Decide who is an author

Authorship gives credit, and it also makes the author responsible and accountable for what is published. The [International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE)](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html) recommends basing authorship on four criteria, all of which an author should meet:

- Substantial contributions to the conception or design of the work, or to the acquisition, analysis or interpretation of data for the work.
- Drafting the work or reviewing it critically for important intellectual content.
- Final approval of the version to be published.
- Agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work, in ensuring that questions about the accuracy or integrity of any part of it are appropriately investigated and resolved.

Everyone who meets all four criteria should be identified as an author. Everyone who meets the first criterion should have the opportunity to take part in reviewing, drafting and approving the manuscript, so that they can meet the second and third.

Contributors who meet fewer than all four criteria should not be listed as authors. They should be acknowledged, with their contributions specified. These activities alone don't qualify someone for authorship:

- Obtaining funding.
- General supervision of a research group, or general administrative support.
- Writing assistance, technical editing, language editing and proofreading.

The people who conduct the work decide who meets the criteria, ideally when they plan the work, and adjust as the work progresses. If they can't agree, the institution where the work was done, not the journal, should be asked to investigate.

When data come from low- and middle-income countries, the ICMJE asks editors to watch for local researchers left off the author list. A paper that doesn't include local investigators as authors may be questioned, and may be rejected.

<Warning>
Name as authors all those, and only those, who meet the criteria, and acknowledge every other contributor. The federal research agencies count [invalid authorship and inadequate acknowledgement](/kb/governance/research-integrity#what-counts-as-a-breach-of-the-responsible-conduct-of-research) as breaches of the responsible conduct of research.
</Warning>

## Agree on publication rights before you sign

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Industry-sponsored studies</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Studies you sponsor yourself (sponsor-investigator)</span></div>

In an industry-sponsored trial, [the clinical trial agreement](/kb/funding/clinical-trial-agreements) contains publication clauses that govern how and when the results can be made public. The clauses are negotiated before the study starts, and they bind the site. [Publication of the study's results follows the terms of the clinical trial agreement](/sops/cr-016#5-4-12) or other study agreement.

Authors must avoid agreements with study sponsors that interfere with their access to all of the study's data, or with their ability to analyze and interpret the data and to prepare and publish manuscripts independently, when and where they choose. Journals may ask authors to provide the agreement in confidence.

With sponsored research, the agreement should expressly give authors access to the data that support the results. At least one author from the academic institution should have accessed the primary data and be able to take part in the analysis.

Clinical trial agreements commonly include these publication provisions:

| Provision | What it usually allows |
|---|---|
| Review period | The sponsor typically has 30 to 90 days to review a manuscript before submission, to protect confidential information and check factual accuracy. A review period can delay submission. It is not a right to veto publication. |
| Removal of confidential information | The sponsor may ask for confidential business information to be removed, such as unreleased safety data from other studies or proprietary manufacturing details. |
| Delay for a patent | Publication may be delayed for a defined period, commonly up to 90 days, if the sponsor needs to file a patent application. The delay is limited in scope and duration. |
| Coordination across sites | In a multi-centre trial, publication is often coordinated centrally for consistency. A site's investigators may not publish the site's results on their own without the sponsor's approval. |

In most industry-sponsored multi-centre trials, a steering committee or a writing group that the sponsor coordinates manages the main publication, including the analysis, the drafting and the timing. Site investigators who meet the ICMJE's criteria are listed as co-authors of that paper, and they don't publish independently.

A site's own data usually can't be published separately without the publications committee's approval, and often not until the main paper is out. If you want the right to publish independently, or a say in the timing, consider negotiating it in the clinical trial agreement before you sign it. The [Research Agreements Office](mailto:contracts@muhc.mcgill.ca) can advise on what to ask for, including access to participant-level data rather than only summary results.

No federal law sets publication rights in Canada. Protection against a sponsor holding back results depends on what the clinical trial agreement says. The ICMJE's recommendations and the policies of the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) are not regulations, and they carry no penalties against a sponsor. If a sponsor seeks to block or significantly delay the publication of valid findings, write to the [Research Agreements Office](mailto:contracts@muhc.mcgill.ca) and the [Research Facilitator](mailto:research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca).

In an investigator-initiated study, including one with a sponsor-investigator, the investigator holds all publication rights. There is no sponsor review period unless the investigator agrees to one with a co-funder.

## Trial registration

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Medical device and surgery studies (Level II)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Prospective studies not regulated by Health Canada (Level IV)</span></div>

Registering a trial publicly before it recruits helps prevent selective publication of results. [Every clinical trial shall be registered before the first participant is recruited](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter11-chapitre11.html), in a publicly accessible registry that the World Health Organization (WHO) or the ICMJE accepts. Every field of the WHO's 24-item Trial Registration Data Set must be completed for the trial to count as fully registered. Give the REB evidence of registration, such as the registration number.

[The sponsor or sponsor-investigator registers the trial](/sops/cr-007#5-8-3) before the first participant is recruited. In an investigator-initiated trial, the study team should check that the investigator has registered it. For a trial funded by an agency, check whether the agency has registered it. If it hasn't, the sponsor or sponsor-investigator must.

A sponsor-investigator who registers on ClinicalTrials.gov uses [the MUHC's organization account](/kb/governance/quality-assurance#register-an-investigator-initiated-trial-on-clinicaltrials-gov). To get access, write to [Quality Assurance](mailto:QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca). Quality Assurance requests the opening of the sponsor-investigator's account if they hold valid RI-MUHC Privileges or Researcher Status.

[During the informed consent discussion](/sops/cr-007#5-8-5), tell each potential participant that registering the study is required. The consent form must give [the registry's name, its web address and the trial's registration number](/kb/recruitment/consent#prepare-the-consent-form). If the results will be posted in a database other than the registry, the sponsor-investigator must give the REB and the participants its name and web address.

After registration, keep the registry up to date with new information that may affect participants' welfare or consent, with safety and, where feasible, efficacy reports, with the reasons if the trial stops early, and with where the findings can be found.

The ICMJE recommends that journals consider a trial for publication only if it was registered in a public registry at or before the time the first participant consented. The ICMJE also recommends publishing the registration number at the end of the abstract.

The most commonly used registry at the MUHC, for industry-sponsored and investigator-initiated trials, is [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov).

### Choose a registry

The ICMJE accepts registration in any primary registry of the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform that includes the 24-item trial registration data set, or in ClinicalTrials.gov. The choice depends on the funder's requirements, the sponsor's preference and the obligations to post results.

| Registry | Study types | Who registers | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov) | All types | The sponsor, the sponsor-investigator or the site team | The most widely indexed registry. CIHR accepts it, like any WHO primary registry or ICMJE-approved registry, and most industry sponsors require it. US law (the Food and Drug Administration Amendments Act of 2007) also requires registration and results posting for applicable clinical trials, whoever funds them. These are interventional trials of drugs or biologics regulated by the US Food and Drug Administration (other than phase 1) and of devices (other than feasibility studies) that have a US site, run under a US investigational new drug application or device exemption, or use a product made in the US and exported for the study. |
| [ISRCTN](https://www.isrctn.com/) | All types. Frequently used for observational and device studies. | The study team, by a manual process | Recognized by the WHO. Some funders and journals prefer ISRCTN for studies that don't test a drug. |
| [WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP)](https://trialsearch.who.int/) | A search portal only | Fed by the primary registries | Not a place to register. It gathers data from the registries the WHO recognizes, for a global search. |
| Health Canada's [Canadian Clinical Trial Search Portal](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/clinical-trials/search-portal.html) | Drug trials in patients that Health Canada has approved since April 1, 2013 (phases 1 to 3) | Nobody registers. Health Canada fills the portal automatically, from its own systems and from international registries. | A search portal, not a registry. It replaced the Clinical Trials Database on July 29, 2026. It does **not** meet the registration requirements of the *Tri-Council Policy Statement* (TCPS 2) or of CIHR. |
| [EU Clinical Trials Register (EU CTR)](https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu/) | Interventional trials of medicines in the European Union or the European Economic Area that started after May 1, 2004 | The company or organization responsible for the trial provides the information. | The member states approve clinical trial applications. The register holds no information on trials of medical devices, surgical procedures or psychotherapeutic procedures. Publicly searchable. |

For most MUHC studies, ClinicalTrials.gov is the appropriate registry. ISRCTN is an acceptable alternative where the sponsor or the journal prefers it. Appearing in Health Canada's search portal doesn't replace registration in a registry that the WHO or the ICMJE accepts.

## Make the results public

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Medical device and surgery studies (Level II)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Prospective studies not regulated by Health Canada (Level IV)</span></div>

Registering a trial and making its results public are separate obligations. Summary results can be posted in the trial's registry. Which rules apply depends on who funds the trial and whether Health Canada regulates it.

{/* Sources: CIHR's FAQ e/52810 (summary results on the trial registry); Health Canada's registration guidance ("sponsors are expected to submit the summary results of their clinical trial to the same registry where the sponsor first registered the trial"). */}

### CIHR-funded trials

[CIHR-funded clinical trials must be registered](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/52820.html) before the first visit of the first participant. The registry must be publicly available, free to access and searchable, and comply with the WHO's international standards.

For a clinical trial grant from CIHR funded on or after January 1, 2022, these are conditions of funding:

- Summary results must be made public within 12 months of the last visit of the last participant for collecting data on the primary outcome. Posting them on the trial registry, with every field of the WHO Trial Registration Data Set completed, meets this condition.
- Publications that describe the trial's results must be open access from the date of publication.
- Every publication must include the trial's registration number in its summary or abstract.

Consider including open access publication fees in the trial's budget when you plan it.

The nominated principal investigator must meet these conditions to stay eligible for new CIHR funding. CIHR checks compliance every year and publishes the aggregate results. Activities that purposely go against the policy result in new CIHR funds being withheld until the unmet requirements are met.

### Health Canada direction

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Studies that need Health Canada authorization</span></div>

Health Canada's [guidance on the registration of clinical trials and public disclosure of results](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/applications-submissions/guidance-documents/guidance-registration-clinical-trials-public-disclosure-results.html) took effect on July 29, 2026. The guidance applies to sponsors of trials of drugs and natural health products in phases 1 to 3, and of trials of Class II to IV medical devices. The guidance doesn't cover:

- Comparative bioavailability studies.
- Single patient trials, also called open label individual patient trials.
- Phase 4 trials of drugs and natural health products used within their marketing authorization.
- Trials of Class I devices, or of licensed devices used within their marketing authorization.

The guidance's expectations are strongly advised but not required by law. Health Canada describes them as a preparatory step before it would propose regulations. [The RI-MUHC's standard operating procedures (SOPs)](/sops/cr-007#5-1-7) still require the sponsor to register the trial before recruitment begins.

Health Canada expects sponsors to register a trial in a registry that meets the WHO's international standards. Registration should ideally come before the trial opens for enrolment in Canada, and no later than 21 calendar days after the first participant is enrolled. Health Canada recommends including the trial's protocol code, in exactly the form used in the clinical trial application, as a secondary identifying number in the registry.

Sponsors should submit the trial's summary results to the same registry within 12 months after the final visit at which primary outcome data are collected from the last participant in the global study. They should also keep the registry record up to date.

When the principal investigator is also the sponsor of a trial in the guidance's scope, these expectations apply to them directly. [Laws, Regulations and Guidelines](/kb/governance/laws-regulations#know-what-health-canada-is-changing) says what Health Canada is changing.

## Report the study in a journal

The requirements below follow the current [ICMJE Recommendations](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/), which the ICMJE updated in [January 2024](https://www.icmje.org/news-and-editorials/updated_recommendations_jan2024.html) and [January 2026](https://www.icmje.org/news-and-editorials/updated_recommendations_jan2026.html). Check the target journal's own policies too.

### AI disclosure in manuscripts

Chatbots and other AI-assisted tools should not be listed as authors, because they cannot be responsible for the accuracy, integrity and originality of the work, which authorship requires. Humans are responsible for any submitted material that used AI-assisted technologies. Authors should carefully review and edit what an AI tool produces, because the output can be incorrect, incomplete or biased.

The ICMJE recommends that journals require authors to disclose at submission whether they used AI-assisted technologies, such as large language models, chatbots or image creators, to produce the submitted work. Authors who used them should describe how, in both the cover letter and, where applicable, the appropriate section of the submitted work:

- AI used for writing assistance, in the acknowledgements.
- AI used for data collection, analysis or figure generation, in the methods.

Referencing AI-generated material as the primary source is not acceptable. Not disclosing AI use may require corrective action, and may be construed as misconduct in some circumstances. Section V of the ICMJE's Recommendations, added in January 2026, covers the use of AI by authors, reviewers and editors. Many journals have adopted requirements beyond the ICMJE's, so check the target journal's AI policy.

If AI tools were used in the research itself, such as in study design, data analysis or clinical decision support, [the rules for AI tools in a study](/kb/planning/ai-in-clinical-research) apply as well.

### Disclose funding and relationships

Authors are responsible for disclosing all relationships and activities that might bias, or be seen to bias, their work. ICMJE member journals require authors to use the ICMJE's disclosure form. Financial relationships, such as employment, consultancies, stock ownership or options, honoraria, patents and paid expert testimony, are the most easily identified. Personal relationships or rivalries, academic competition and intellectual beliefs can also be conflicts.

The article should declare:

- The authors' relationships and activities.
- The sources of support for the work, with each source's role, if any, in the study design, in the collection, analysis and interpretation of the data, and in the writing of the report, and any restrictions it set on submitting the report for publication. If a source had no such involvement or restrictions, the article should say so.
- Whether the authors had access to the study data, and the nature and extent of that access.

The funding statement should include only direct support of the work described. General institutional support for an author's time should be distinguished from the direct funding of the work. Purposely failing to report the relationships or activities on the journal's disclosure form is a form of misconduct. For the REB and McGill, see [Conflict of Interest](/kb/governance/conflict-of-interest).

## Follow a reporting guideline

Reporting guidelines say what information a publication must include so that readers can judge the validity of the methods and the reliability of the results. Journals and REBs increasingly require, or strongly recommend, the guideline that fits the study design. The ones most relevant to clinical research at the RI-MUHC are:

- [CONSORT](https://www.consort-statement.org) (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials), for randomized controlled trials. It covers randomization, blinding, participant flow and outcome reporting. Most major journals require a CONSORT checklist.
- [STROBE](https://www.strobe-statement.org) (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology), for cohort, case-control and cross-sectional studies.
- [SPIRIT](https://www.spirit-statement.org) (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials), for trial protocols submitted for publication.

The [EQUATOR Network](https://www.equator-network.org) keeps a library of reporting guidelines by study type. Consider choosing the guideline when you design the study, not when you write it up, because it shapes how the data are collected and analyzed.

## Write a lay summary

A lay summary tells participants and the public, in plain language, what the study found. Results should be made available to the people, groups and communities who took part in a culturally appropriate and meaningful format, [such as reports in plain language](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter4-chapitre4.html), in addition to technical reports. Researchers should make sure participants are told how to access the results. CIHR's conditions for clinical trials don't require a plain-language summary.

Some protocols, particularly those with patient partners, include a lay summary in the plan for disseminating results that goes to ethics review. Consider providing a lay summary of the results to the patient partners on your research team as part of the patient engagement plan.

Consider structuring a lay summary in five parts:

- What the study set out to find, and why it matters to patients or the health system.
- What the study did: who took part, what they received or did, and for how long.
- What it found, including whether the main outcome was met.
- What the findings mean for patients or care, what they don't mean, and what questions remain.
- What happens next, such as follow-up studies.

Consider writing for someone without a clinical or scientific background, avoiding acronyms and statistical methods, and having someone outside the research team read a draft. If patient partners will review the summary, the [Research Facilitator](mailto:research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca) can advise on its language.

## Share data after publication

Funders, journals and ethics policies increasingly expect research data to be shared after publication, and in some cases require it.

### Tri-Agency Research Data Management Policy

The federal research agencies' [Research Data Management Policy](https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/interagency-research-funding/policies-and-guidelines/research-data-management/tri-agency-research-data-management-policy) applies to recipients of funding from CIHR, the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada (NSERC) and the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council of Canada (SSHRC). Where a funding opportunity requires a data management plan, the plan describes whether and how data will be shared and where they will be deposited, restricted access included.

The agencies will phase in a requirement to deposit data. Under it, grant recipients will deposit the digital data, metadata and code that directly support their publications in a digital repository by the time of publication. The agencies have yet to phase it in. CIHR's 2008 requirement to deposit bioinformatics, atomic and molecular coordinate data still applies to CIHR-funded researchers.

The policy doesn't require grant recipients to share their data. The agencies expect researchers to provide appropriate access to the data where ethical, cultural, legal and commercial requirements allow, in keeping with the FAIR principles and the standards of their disciplines.

### FAIR principles

The [FAIR principles](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata201618) (Wilkinson and others, 2016) are the internationally recognized standard for managing and sharing research data:

- Findable: data have a persistent identifier, such as a DOI, and are described with rich metadata.
- Accessible: authorized users can retrieve the data through a standard protocol, and the conditions of access are documented.
- Interoperable: data use standard formats and controlled vocabularies that allow analysis across studies.
- Reusable: data and metadata are described well enough for another researcher to reproduce or build on the work.

FAIR doesn't mean open. Participants' health data can be FAIR without being public: a restricted-access repository with documented access criteria meets the principles.

### Share individual participant data

Reports of clinical trials submitted to ICMJE journals must contain a data sharing statement. A trial that began enrolling on or after January 1, 2019, must also include a data sharing plan in its registration. The statement must say:

- Whether individual de-identified participant data, including data dictionaries, will be shared. "Undecided" is not an acceptable answer.
- What data will be shared.
- Whether related documents, such as the protocol and the statistical analysis plan, will be available.
- When the data will become available, and for how long.
- The access criteria: with whom, for what kinds of analysis and by what mechanism.

If the data sharing plan changes after registration, the change should be reflected in the statement submitted and published with the manuscript, and updated in the registry record.

Any sharing of individual participant data must be consistent with what participants were told in the consent form. If the consent form didn't provide for data sharing, sharing identifiable information requires new consent, or the REB's approval of the secondary use without consent, on [TCPS 2's conditions](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter5-chapitre5.html).

If participants' information will leave Quebec, for example to a repository that researchers outside Quebec can reach, [a privacy impact assessment (EFVP) may be required](/kb/governance/privacy#know-when-an-efvp-is-needed). Write to [efvp@muhc.mcgill.ca](mailto:efvp@muhc.mcgill.ca) before you deposit data in any external repository.

## What GCP is, and why it exists

<Tooltip tip="International standard for design, conduct, monitoring, recording, and reporting of clinical studies." headline="Good Clinical Practice (GCP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#gcp">Good Clinical Practice</Tooltip> is an international standard for the design, conduct, monitoring, recording, and reporting of clinical studies. Its purpose is twofold: to protect the rights, safety, and wellbeing of research participants, and to ensure that the data generated by a study are credible enough to support regulatory decisions. Both purposes are inseparable. Data that cannot be trusted cannot support a decision, and a study that harms participants has no ethical basis regardless of its scientific value.

GCP did not emerge from theory. It emerged from failure. The Nuremberg Code (1947), the Declaration of Helsinki (1964), and the Belmont Report (1979) established the ethical principles. The ICH GCP guideline, first published in 1996 as E6(R1), translated those principles into operational requirements that sponsors, investigators, and regulators across participating countries could apply consistently. For the first time, regulators in Canada, the US, and Europe could accept clinical data from studies conducted under shared documentation and conduct standards.

### Canadian GCP

Health Canada adopted the ICH GCP guideline for drug studies under Division 5 of the Food and Drug Regulations. For medical device studies, the equivalent standard is ISO 14155. At the RI-MUHC, ICH GCP E6(R3) applies to all drug and natural health product studies; ISO 14155:2020 applies to medical device studies. TCPS2 applies to all human research regardless of study type. These standards are additive — meeting ICH GCP does not satisfy the ethical requirements of TCPS2, and vice versa.

For twenty years, the operational substance of GCP was largely stable. Then, in 2016, ICH published E6(R2) — a revision that introduced risk-based monitoring and acknowledged electronic data systems as a legitimate medium for clinical research. E6(R3), published in 2023 and in effect in Canada as of April 1, 2026, extended that logic across the entire study.

## How ICH GCP E6(R3) changed things

The central shift in E6(R3) is philosophical before it is procedural. Previous versions of GCP described what had to be done. E6(R3) asks why — and whether the approach is proportionate to the actual risks of the study. That shift — from uniform compliance to proportionate, risk-based quality — is the concept of quality by design.

E6(R3) extends risk-based thinking across the entire study — not just monitoring, but design, conduct, documentation, and oversight. The question applied at every stage: what could genuinely go wrong here, and what is proportionate to prevent or detect it?

| Area | Before E6(R3) | Under E6(R3) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quality approach | Reactive — identify and correct quality failures as they occur | Proactive — anticipate risks before the study starts and build controls into the design |
| Monitoring | On-site visits at regular intervals; SDV of most or all data points | Frequency and focus driven by the monitoring plan and actual site risk profile; remote monitoring formally recognized |
| Documentation | Specific list of required documents; paper-primary by default | Electronic records and signatures explicitly accommodated; principle-based rather than format-prescriptive |
| Oversight focus | Process compliance — were the procedures followed? | Critical outcomes — did the things that matter most get protected? |
| Decentralized elements | Not addressed; assumed all activity occurs at a fixed site | Explicitly addressed — remote consent, wearables, home visits, and telemedicine all have a framework |

### What stayed the same

E6(R3) did not change the fundamental obligations: protecting participants, maintaining data integrity, reporting safety events, keeping the <Tooltip tip="The QI's collection of documentation demonstrating compliance, study integrity, and participant safety." headline="Investigator Site File (ISF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#isf">ISF</Tooltip> current, and operating under a <Tooltip tip="The physician responsible to the sponsor for the conduct of a clinical trial at the site. One QI per study per Canadian site." headline="Qualified Investigator (QI)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#qi">QI</Tooltip>/PI who holds ultimate accountability. The ALCOA+ principles, the essential documents framework, the reporting timelines in SOP-CR-012 and SOP-CR-026 — these remain in effect. E6(R3) changes the approach to quality, not the underlying ethics or the core regulatory requirements.

## Quality by design in practice

Quality by design means building the conditions for a good study into the study from the beginning — not auditing quality after the fact. The analogy is manufacturing: a product designed with quality tolerances built into the process will consistently meet those tolerances. A product inspected at the end of the line and rejected if it fails has already wasted the resources to produce it. Clinical research works the same way.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="The old model" icon="clock-rotate-left">
    Design the protocol. Submit and get it approved. Train the team. Run the study. Monitor for compliance. When something goes wrong, correct it. Inspect the ISF at the end and look for findings. Quality is a post-hoc check, not a design constraint.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Quality by design" icon="shield-check">
    Before the study starts: identify what could genuinely go wrong, assess which failures would matter most, build controls to prevent or detect them, and define thresholds that signal when action is needed. Quality is a design decision, built in from the start.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

In practical terms, quality by design produces three outputs before the study begins: a set of critical-to-quality factors, a risk assessment, and a set of quality tolerance limits. These are then embedded in the protocol, the monitoring plan, and the data management plan — so they are operational from the first participant visit, not reviewed for the first time at a monitoring visit six months in.

<Tip>
A quality issue identified after data collection is expensive to fix, if it can be fixed at all. The same issue anticipated during protocol development costs almost nothing to prevent.
</Tip>

## Critical-to-quality factors and what they protect

A critical-to-quality factor (CTQ) is an element of a study that, if it fails, would directly compromise participant safety or the integrity of the data that supports the study's primary conclusions. Not every protocol requirement is a CTQ — the question is which requirements are so fundamental that their failure would undermine the purpose of the study.

Identifying CTQs is a judgment exercise that happens during protocol development, led by the sponsor or sponsor-investigator. A Phase III cardiovascular outcomes trial might identify: informed consent integrity; the primary endpoint measurement; and <Tooltip tip="A drug, medical device, or natural health product being tested or used as a reference in a clinical trial." headline="Investigational Product (IP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ip">IP</Tooltip> accountability. A retrospective chart review has a completely different CTQ profile — consent may not be required, but data extraction accuracy and completeness are likely critical.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Participant safety — eligibility and consent">
    Enrolling a participant who does not meet eligibility criteria, or whose consent was not properly obtained, is not a process deviation — it is a fundamental failure to protect the person the study is supposed to serve. These are almost always CTQs.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Data integrity — primary endpoint reliability">
    The primary endpoint is what the study was designed to measure. If the measurement is unreliable — because the assessment was performed incorrectly, outside the protocol window, or by untrained staff — the study's central finding is compromised.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Safety reporting — SAE detection and reporting">
    Serious adverse events that are not identified, assessed, or reported correctly do not just violate GCP — they mean that real harm to participants is not being captured and acted on. SAE processes are CTQs in almost every interventional study.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Study validity — blinding integrity">
    In a blinded study, unintentional unblinding — even of a single participant's treatment assignment — can compromise data integrity and trigger analysis consequences. Blinding maintenance is typically a CTQ in Phase III efficacy trials.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Study-specific — biospecimen handling">
    For studies whose primary outcomes depend on biological samples — pharmacokinetic studies, biomarker studies, tissue studies — the collection, processing, and storage conditions may be CTQs. A sample frozen at the wrong temperature is not a fixable error.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="IP management — dose accuracy and accountability">
    For interventional drug studies, accurate dispensing of the correct dose to the correct participant, and complete accountability for every unit received, dispensed, and returned, is a CTQ — for both safety and for the dose-response data the study is generating.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

<Warning>
**Quality tolerance limits.** For each CTQ, the sponsor defines a quality tolerance limit (QTL) — a threshold that, if crossed, signals that the study's quality may be compromised and that action is required. A QTL is not a target; it is a trip wire. For example: a QTL of 5% of participants with a primary endpoint assessment outside the protocol window. If a site reaches 4%, the monitoring plan may specify an increase in visit frequency. If 5% is reached, the protocol specifies an investigation and a decision on whether the affected data can be included in the analysis. QTLs appear in the monitoring plan and sometimes in the protocol statistical section.
</Warning>

## What this means at the site level

Most of the QbD work happens at the sponsor level, before the site is ever involved. By the time the site receives the protocol and <Tooltip tip="Compilation of clinical and nonclinical data on the investigational product relevant to the study." headline="Investigator's Brochure (IB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ib">Investigator's Brochure</Tooltip>, the CTQs have been identified and the QTLs have been set. The site's role is not to redesign the quality framework — it is to understand it and operate within it.

In practical terms, this changes how the research team reads a protocol and a monitoring plan. A well-written E6(R3)-aligned protocol will identify its CTQs, explicitly or implicitly. The procedures that receive the most detailed specification, the most explicit training requirements at the <Tooltip tip="Monitoring visit to train the site team on the protocol and confirm site readiness, after REB approval and MUHC Authorization." headline="Site Initiation Visit (SIV)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#siv">SIV</Tooltip>, and the most frequent verification during monitoring visits are almost always the CTQs. Reading a protocol through this lens — asking "what is this study most trying to protect, and why?" — makes it easier to set priorities during active conduct.

<CardGroup cols={3}>
  <Card title="At the SIV" icon="chalkboard">
    The SIV is where CTQs are translated into site-specific training. Procedures the monitor spends the most time on are signalling which elements are critical. That emphasis reflects what the monitoring plan will verify most closely.
  </Card>
  <Card title="During conduct" icon="magnifying-glass">
    When prioritizing competing tasks, CTQs come first. A protocol visit that includes the primary endpoint assessment is not interchangeable with a visit that doesn't. A consent procedure error is not equivalent to a minor scheduling deviation.
  </Card>
  <Card title="At monitoring visits" icon="clipboard-check">
    Under risk-based monitoring, the monitor's focus is proportionate to the study's CTQs and the site's performance against QTLs. A site managing its CTQs well will have a lighter monitoring footprint than one accumulating deviations in critical areas.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

### The deviation classification connection

The three-tier deviation classification in SOP-CR-026 (minor, major, critical) maps directly onto the QbD framework. A deviation is major or critical precisely because it affects a CTQ — participant safety, primary endpoint reliability, or data integrity. A deviation is minor because it falls outside the critical elements. Understanding which protocol requirements are CTQs makes deviation classification more intuitive and more defensible.

## Risk-based monitoring and what it changes

Risk-based monitoring (RBM) is the monitoring approach that QbD makes possible. Under traditional monitoring, every site received visits at fixed intervals and every data point was verified against source documents — regardless of whether the study was performing well or poorly, and regardless of whether the data point was critical or incidental. RBM replaces this uniform approach with one tailored to actual risk.

In practice, RBM means the monitoring plan specifies different levels of oversight for different sites and different data categories. A new site with a new team running a complex protocol will receive more intensive monitoring than an experienced site that has run similar studies successfully. A data point that feeds the primary endpoint will receive more verification than a secondary endpoint or an administrative field. As a site's track record develops — deviations logged, QTL performance tracked, ISF quality assessed — the monitoring intensity is adjusted accordingly.

Remote monitoring visits, formally recognized in E6(R3), are a direct product of RBM. When a site is performing well and the risk profile is low, a remote visit — reviewing data and documents electronically — may replace or supplement on-site visits, freeing on-site capacity for sites where the risk profile warrants closer attention.

<Note>
**What this means for site staff.** A site that maintains a current ISF, documents deviations promptly, closes monitoring action items on time, and manages its CTQ procedures well will receive proportionately lighter monitoring. A site where the ISF is behind, monitoring letters are not responded to, and deviations are accumulating in critical areas will receive heavier scrutiny — not as punishment, but as proportionate risk management.
</Note>

Quality by design does not reduce accountability at the site. It shifts the question from "did we follow the procedures?" to "did we protect the things that matter?"

Before you can navigate the SOPs, manage a submission, or work with participants, you need a shared understanding of what clinical research is, how the regulatory system is structured, and what makes Quebec distinctive. This guide covers that foundation — study types, trial phases, key roles, the history behind the rules, the Quebec regulatory layer, and what informed consent actually means in practice.

## 01 — Research vs. clinical care

Clinical care and clinical research look similar from the outside. Both take place in clinical settings. Both involve patients and healthcare professionals. Both can lead to someone receiving a treatment. But they serve fundamentally different purposes.

Clinical care is oriented toward the **individual patient in front of you**. Every decision is made in that person's best interest, using the best available evidence. Clinical research is oriented toward **generating that evidence** — producing knowledge that will be generalizable and useful for future patients, not just the person enrolled today.

Every participant in a clinical study is contributing to the care of people they will never meet.

<Info>
Canada's national standard, the **Tri-Council Policy Statement (TCPS2)**, explicitly names the risk of **therapeutic misconception** — the tendency for participants to experience a research intervention as personalized treatment. Understanding it is part of designing a consent process that works.
</Info>

| Dimension | Clinical Care | Clinical Research |
|-----------|--------------|-------------------|
| Aimed at | The individual patient | Future patients |
| Uses | Best available evidence | Generates new evidence |
| Primary goal | Patient benefit | Generalizable knowledge |
| Relationship | Therapeutic | Scientific investigation |

**What counts as research?** TCPS2 defines research broadly: any undertaking intended to extend knowledge through systematic investigation. This covers drug trials, device studies, chart reviews, psychosocial surveys, biobank studies, and educational interventions. If the goal is generalizable knowledge involving human data or participants, ethics governance applies. Quality improvement and program evaluation are generally not research under TCPS2 — provided the goal is improving local practice, not producing knowledge intended for broader application.

How a study is classified shapes everything downstream: your regulatory pathway, required training level, oversight intensity, and whether Health Canada needs to authorize anything before the first participant is approached.

## 03 — Phases of a clinical trial

By the time a compound reaches the first human participant, it has typically travelled through years of laboratory and animal research. Understanding that arc makes the operational structure make sense.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Pre-clinical — Laboratory and animal studies">
    Safety parameters, pharmacokinetics, and early biological activity signals are established. This work generates the initial safety profile that regulators and ethics boards evaluate before approving the first human dose.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Phase I — First-in-human: safety and tolerability (20–80 participants)">
    The central question is **safety**: how does the body respond, and at what doses do adverse effects appear? Dose-escalation designs with intensive safety monitoring are standard. In oncology, Phase I trials enroll patients — the compounds involved are too toxic for healthy volunteers.

    **Note for Canadian sites:** Health Canada's 30-day default review process for Phase I CTAs is faster than many comparable jurisdictions, which contributes to Canada's appeal as a first-in-human trial destination.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Phase II — Proof of concept and dose optimization (100–400 participants)">
    Does the intervention do what it is supposed to do, and at what dose? Phase IIa establishes proof of concept; Phase IIb refines the dose. These studies are often randomized, sometimes blinded. Compounds either build a case for pivotal testing here — or are discontinued.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Phase III — Confirmatory: is it better than what exists? (hundreds to several thousand)">
    Large randomized controlled trials generating the primary evidence base for regulatory submission. **Clinical equipoise must genuinely exist at the outset** — if one option is clearly better, it is not ethical to assign anyone to the other. These pivotal studies determine whether a compound receives authorization.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Regulatory review — Notice of Compliance and Drug Identification Number">
    Health Canada evaluates the full submission — safety data, efficacy data, manufacturing quality. A **Notice of Compliance (NOC)** issues if the standard is met; a **Drug Identification Number (DIN)** is assigned. Standard review: 300 days. Priority review: 180 days.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Phase IV — Post-market: the real world">
    The drug is authorized, but questions remain: how does it perform in populations not well-represented in Phase III? What are the long-term safety signals? No CTA is required for within-label Phase IV studies, though **GCP and REB oversight still apply**.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## 04 — The key roles

A clinical study at an academic medical centre involves a dense web of documented accountability. Here are the key roles, what each is responsible for, and how they connect.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Principal Investigator / Qualified Investigator — Required, one per site">
    In Health Canada's Division 5 language, this person is the **Qualified Investigator** — a physician or dentist licensed in the province. Delegation is documented and legal. **Accountability does not transfer.** In investigator-initiated trials, the PI is often also the sponsor, a combined role that adds substantial regulatory obligation including filing a CTA, arranging independent monitoring, and ensuring that the <Tooltip tip="A drug, medical device, or natural health product being tested or used as a reference in a clinical trial." headline="Investigational Product (IP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ip">investigational product</Tooltip> meets GMP standards.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Clinical Research Coordinator — Site operations">
    The day-to-day operational lead. Scheduling, documentation, data capture, participant coordination, regulatory filings, REB submissions. There is no regulated CRC profession in Canada — scope of practice is defined institutionally and by the <Tooltip tip="Lists each team member, their qualifications, and specific trial tasks authorized by the QI." headline="Task Delegation Log (TDL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#tdl">delegation log</Tooltip>. A CRC without a nursing credential cannot perform reserved health acts in Quebec: **blood draws, medication administration, clinical assessment**. Those require a licensed research nurse.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Research Nurse — Licensed by OIIQ">
    Licensed by the OIIQ in Quebec, able to perform the full range of delegated clinical tasks. Often the most consistent human presence for participants across the study — coordinating visits, responding to questions, fielding calls outside of business hours. Bridges clinical practice and research protocol.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Clinical Research Associate (Monitor) — Sponsor-side, verification role">
    Employed by or contracted to the sponsor, not the site. Conducts monitoring visits — reviewing source documents, confirming consent documentation, verifying protocol adherence. Under ICH E6(R3), monitoring is explicitly **risk-based**: remote and centralized review formally complement on-site visits. The CRA is there to verify, not to serve the site's operational needs.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Research Ethics Board (REB / CER) — Independent, cannot be overridden">
    An independent committee reviewing every research protocol before any participant is approached. In Quebec: minimum five members including researchers, an ethicist, a legal expert (not the institution's own counsel), and a community member with no institutional affiliation. **The REB's decision is independent — institutional management cannot override a rejection.** MSSS-designated REBs hold specific authority to review research with minors and incapacitated adults under Article 21 CCQ.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Research Pharmacist — Dual framework: Division 5 and pharmacy law">
    Manages the investigational product: receiving, storage, dispensing, and accounting for every unit. Maintains blinding when required. Participates in site initiation and monitoring visits. In Quebec, obligations flow from both **Health Canada Division 5 and provincial pharmacy practice legislation** — making the research pharmacy function distinct from standard clinical pharmacy.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

<Note>
In a moderately complex industry-sponsored trial, more than **twenty people** carry documented accountability before the first participant signs anything — investigators, coordinators, nurses, pharmacists, ethics board members, monitors, and institutional administrators.
</Note>

## 05 — Where the rules came from

Research ethics governance did not develop from first principles. It developed in response to specific failures — cases where the absence of structured oversight led to harm. Understanding the history makes the obligations feel necessary rather than arbitrary.

<Steps>
  <Step title="1947 — The Nuremberg Code">
    The Nuremberg Doctors' Trial produced ten foundational principles for ethical research. First and most developed: **voluntary informed consent is an absolute requirement**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="1964 — Declaration of Helsinki">
    The World Medical Association developed this framework for physicians conducting research. Its 1975 revision introduced the requirement for **independent ethics committee review**. Core principle: participant welfare takes precedence over the interests of science and society. Revised eight times, most recently in 2024.
  </Step>
  <Step title="1979 — The Belmont Report">
    Following public exposure of the Tuskegee Syphilis Study, US congressional hearings produced this landmark document articulating three principles: **respect for persons, beneficence, and justice**. These directly shaped Canada's TCPS2 and remain its organizing framework.
  </Step>
  <Step title="1996 — ICH GCP E6(R1): international harmonization">
    The International Council for Harmonization published the first unified Good Clinical Practice standard, aligning clinical trial requirements across the US, EU, and Japan. **Canada adopted it as the operational standard for regulated drug trials.**
  </Step>
  <Step title="2016 — ICH GCP E6(R2): risk-based monitoring">
    An addendum to R1 formalizing **risk-based quality management** and acknowledging the shift to electronic clinical data. It set the expectation that monitoring strategies be tailored to study risk — the conceptual foundation for the more complete redesign in R3.
  </Step>
  <Step title="1998–2022 — TCPS2: Canada's national ethics standard">
    The Tri-Council Policy Statement (first published 1998, current version 2022) governs all human participant research at institutions receiving CIHR, NSERC, or SSHRC funding. Compliance is a **condition of federal funding**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="2026 — ICH GCP E6(R3): in effect in Canada April 1, 2026">
    The most significant GCP revision in three decades. Core shift: from reactive <Tooltip tip="All planned and systematic actions to ensure trial data are generated, documented, and reported in compliance with GCP and SOPs." headline="Quality Assurance (QA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#qa">quality assurance</Tooltip> to **quality by design** — identify critical-to-quality factors before the study begins, build controls proportionate to those risks. Formalizes risk-based monitoring, electronic data governance, and decentralized trial elements.
  </Step>
</Steps>

**The regulatory stack — all layers apply simultaneously**

| Layer | Instruments |
|-------|------------|
| International | ICH GCP E6(R3), Declaration of Helsinki |
| Federal — Canada | Food and Drug Regulations Part C Division 5, Medical Devices Regulations (ITA), TCPS2 |
| Provincial — Quebec | Civil Code Arts. 20–25, <Tooltip tip="Quebec's Act to modernize personal information protection law (in force Sept 2023). Requires EFVP before sharing personal data without consent." headline="Law 25 / Loi 25" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#law-25">Law 25</Tooltip>, <Tooltip tip="Quebec's Act respecting health and social services information (key provisions July 2024). Governs researcher access to health data through RSSS institutions." headline="Law 5 / Loi 5" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#law-5">Law 5</Tooltip>, MSSS *Cadre de référence*, LSSSS |

Quebec's Civil Code creates legally enforceable obligations beyond federal policy. Each provincial layer adds requirements; none replaces the others.

## 06 — The Quebec layer

Quebec does not apply the federal rules and stop there. It adds obligations that address what federal frameworks leave to the provinces: consent under civil law, governance of the public health network, and privacy requirements specific to Quebec institutions.

### The triple evaluation

Before any research project can begin at a Quebec <Tooltip tip="Quebec's public health and social services network (Réseau de la santé et des services sociaux), including the RI-MUHC." headline="RSSS" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#rsss">RSSS</Tooltip> institution, three distinct evaluations must be completed. They can run simultaneously — but final authorization issues only when all three are complete and positive.

<Steps>
  <Step title="01 — Scientific evaluation: Is the science sound?">
    Assesses merit and methodological feasibility. For projects already peer-reviewed by a recognized funding body (CIHR, FRQ-Santé), that review is generally accepted. Otherwise, an institutional scientific committee evaluates it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="02 — Ethics evaluation: Are the participants protected?">
    Conducted by the CER following TCPS2 proportionality principles. Lower-risk studies may receive delegated review by the chair; higher-risk protocols go to the full board. Annual continuing review is required throughout.
  </Step>
  <Step title="03 — Institutional feasibility: Can this institution do this work?">
    Patient population alignment, clinical capacity, pharmacy readiness, resource availability, contract review, legal review — and the *ÉFVP* (privacy impact assessment) when personal health data is involved.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### *La personne formellement mandatée*

Every RSSS institution designates a ***<Tooltip tip="Individual formally authorized to issue the final written authorization for each research project at a Quebec RSSS institution." headline="Personne mandatée (PFM)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#personne-mandatee">personne formellement mandatée</Tooltip>*** (PFM): an individual authorized by the Board of Directors to issue the final written authorization for each research project. No study begins — not even participant identification — without the PFM's authorization letter. The requirement reflects a provincial governance obligation, not an administrative preference.

### The Civil Code of Quebec — legally enforceable

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Art. 20 CCQ — Proportionality of risk">
    Research affecting personal integrity may only proceed when the risk is **not disproportionate to the expected benefit** and the project has REB approval. The threshold is proportionality, not zero risk.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Art. 21 CCQ — Minors and incapacitated adults">
    Governs research with **minors and adults who cannot consent**. A minor or an incapacitated adult who understands the research and objects cannot be enrolled — regardless of what a substitute decision-maker has authorized. Only MSSS-designated REBs may review Article 21 research.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Art. 25 CCQ — Compensation restrictions">
    Prohibits financial compensation beyond **actual loss reimbursement**. Stricter than both TCPS2 and ICH GCP, which permit payment to healthy Phase I volunteers. In Quebec, compensation must be calibrated to avoid constituting undue inducement.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

### Privacy: Laws 25 and 5

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Law 25 — Privacy modernization (in force Sept. 2023)" icon="shield">
    Requires a **Privacy Impact Assessment (*ÉFVP*)** before communicating personal information without consent for research, or when deploying systems that access personal data. Non-compliance penalties: up to $25 million or 4% of worldwide turnover.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Law 5 — Health information framework (key provisions July 2024)" icon="database">
    Creates a dedicated framework for health and social services information. Affiliated researchers access health data through their institution's information officer. Unaffiliated researchers go through **Santé Québec**. The *ÉFVP* is not a form completed at the end — it requires early engagement.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

**CATALIS FAST TRACK:** CATALIS Québec operates a FAST TRACK Evaluation Service targeting institutional authorization in **eight weeks or less** for qualifying industry-sponsored trials. As of early 2026, 37 trials across 14 institutions had been authorized through the program. Ask the Research Facilitator whether FAST TRACK eligibility applies to your study.
</Info>

## 07 — The participant at the centre

Informed consent is a process, not a signature. Under both TCPS2 and Quebec's Civil Code, it must be continuous, documented, and designed to ensure that participation remains genuinely voluntary throughout — not just at the moment of enrolment.

The right to withdraw at any time, without consequence to care — this is not a formality. It is a legal and ethical guarantee that every research participant retains throughout the study.

Consent is not permanent. If new safety information emerges, participants must be re-consented before continuing. Initial consent authorizes participation as understood at the time. It does not authorize participation under conditions that did not yet exist.

**Elements of valid consent — TCPS2 and Civil Code:**

- **Nature and purpose** of the study
- **What participation involves** — procedures, visits, time, potential discomfort
- **Known risks** and potential benefits
- **Right to withdraw** at any time, without consequences to care
- What happens to **their data**
- **Who to contact** with questions or concerns

<Note>
In Quebec, consent forms at public institutions must be available in French under the *Charte de la langue française* (Bill 96). In practice they are typically provided in both official languages. Never use a translated or modified <Tooltip tip="The written document providing the participant with information essential to making an informed decision. Both parties must sign." headline="Informed Consent Form (ICF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#icf">ICF</Tooltip> without REB approval.
</Note>

<Warning>
**Therapeutic misconception in practice:** Researchers and coordinators who work closely with participants can underestimate how easy it is for participants to conflate research participation with personalized care. A well-designed consent process — plain language, time for questions, periodic checks on understanding — is the primary tool for keeping that distinction clear throughout the study.
</Warning>

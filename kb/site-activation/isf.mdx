## What the ISF is

The <Tooltip tip="The QI's collection of documentation demonstrating compliance, study integrity, and participant safety." headline="Investigator Site File (ISF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#isf">Investigator Site File</Tooltip> is the <Tooltip tip="The physician responsible to the sponsor for the conduct of a clinical trial at the site. One QI per study per Canadian site." headline="Qualified Investigator (QI)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#qi">QI</Tooltip>/PI's collection of all essential documents for a study at their site. It is not a filing cabinet — it is the complete documentary record that, taken together, demonstrates that the study was conducted in compliance with the approved protocol, <Tooltip tip="International standard for design, conduct, monitoring, recording, and reporting of clinical studies." headline="Good Clinical Practice (GCP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#gcp">GCP</Tooltip>, applicable regulations, and the rights and safety of participants.

The essential documents in the ISF serve two purposes simultaneously. During the study, they support the day-to-day conduct of the trial: the approved protocol, the <Tooltip tip="Lists each team member, their qualifications, and specific trial tasks authorized by the QI." headline="Task Delegation Log (TDL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tdl">delegation log</Tooltip>, the training records, and the consent forms are working documents that the team refers to. After the study ends, those same documents become the audit trail — the record that allows a Health Canada inspector, a sponsor auditor, or a court to reconstruct the conduct of the study years later. This dual purpose is why ISF maintenance cannot be deferred. A document that should have been filed in March cannot be reconstructed in October without creating a contemporaneity problem.

The ISF structure is defined by the essential documents list in ICH GCP E6(R3), Appendix C, reproduced as Appendix 4 of [SOP-CR-015](/sops/cr-015). The essential documents are organized into three phases: before the study commences, during conduct, and after completion. This article covers the ISF across all three phases — what goes in it, how it is maintained during active conduct, and how it is kept in a condition that would survive an inspection at any point.

<Note>
**Device studies:** For medical device trials, the applicable essential documents standard is ISO 14155 GCP, Annex E, not ICH GCP E6(R3) Appendix C. The structure and maintenance principles described here apply to both, but the specific document list differs. Confirm the applicable standard at the start of any device study.
</Note>

## The ISF and the Trial Master File

The ISF is the site's collection of essential documents. The <Tooltip tip="The sponsor's collection of documentation demonstrating compliance across all study sites." headline="Trial Master File (TMF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tmf">Trial Master File</Tooltip> (TMF) is the sponsor's collection — a single file containing essential documents from every participating site, across every country involved in the study. The two files are complementary and are intended to mirror each other: any document in the ISF that was generated by or provided to the sponsor should also appear in the TMF, and vice versa for site-level documents.

The practical implication is that the monitor checks the ISF against the TMF at every monitoring visit. Discrepancies — a document filed at the site that the sponsor has not received, or a sponsor document that the site has not filed — are monitoring findings. Keeping the ISF current is not just a site obligation; it is what makes monitoring visits productive rather than remedial.

For Sponsor-Investigators running a single-site study, the ISF and the TMF are effectively the same file. Best practice is still to maintain them as logically distinct collections within the same binder structure, because the distinction becomes meaningful if the study is later audited or expanded to additional sites.

For Sponsor-Investigators running multicentre studies, the TMF has a three-level structure: trial-level documents, country-level documents, and site-level documents. The filing and archiving structure for this is in [SOP-CR-015](/sops/cr-015) Appendix 6. The Sponsor-Investigator is responsible for collecting and filing essential documents from all sites.

## The paper-primary rule

Until the RI-MUHC has implemented a validated, 21 CFR Part 11-compliant electronic document management system, the ISF must be maintained primarily in paper form, organized in binders. Electronic copies may be kept as backup on a shared drive, but they are supplementary — the paper binder is the primary record.

The paper-primary rule exists because an electronic signature or an electronic document is only legally compliant for essential documents if the system that produced it meets the FDA's 21 CFR Part 11 validation requirements (audit trail, access controls, backup, security). Most shared drives and standard document management tools do not meet this standard. If a document was signed electronically outside a validated system, the signature is not GCP-compliant for essential document purposes.

Any document that requires a wet signature — the signed protocol, the signed Investigator's Brochure, executed agreements, signed consent forms, paper CRFs, and any document initialled and dated by the QI/PI or a participant — must be retained in its original paper state. These cannot be destroyed in favour of scanned copies.

Superseded documents that exist only as PDFs — older versions of blank ICFs, training slides, study manuals, blank source form templates — may be stored electronically, with a Note to File in the current paper binder indicating their location.

<Warning>
**Scanning is not archiving.** Scanning a signed document and discarding the original does not satisfy the retention requirement. The original signed document must be kept. Scanned copies may supplement the original but do not replace it.
</Warning>

## Maintaining the ISF during active conduct

The ISF is a live document. It must be updated continuously throughout the study — not as catch-up at monitoring visits, not reconstructed at close-out. A monitor or inspector can visit at any time, and the ISF they review must reflect the current state of the study.

During active conduct, the ISF accumulates documents in several categories. Each monitoring visit generates a Monitoring Letter and the site's written response — both are essential documents that must be filed promptly and initialled and dated by the QI/PI. Each REB submission generates an approval or acknowledgement letter — filed, version-controlled, cross-referenced against the document versions listed in the approval. Each amendment to the protocol, ICF, or IB arrives from the sponsor with a signature page — signed and dated by the QI/PI and filed before the amendment is implemented.

Team changes require immediate ISF updates. When a team member is added, their CV, training records, and signed delegation log entry must be in the ISF before they perform any protocol tasks. When a team member leaves, the delegation log must be updated with an end date and the QI/PI must sign off the change. An undated delegation entry — one that does not specify when a person's delegation began or ended — is an inspection finding.

Participant files are maintained separately from the regulatory binders but are part of the overall ISF. Each participant has a source document file containing consent forms, AE assessment logs, visit notes, lab results, and any other data generated during their participation. These files are the source from which <Tooltip tip="Document designed to record all protocol-required information to be reported to the sponsor for each participant." headline="Case Report Form (CRF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#crf">CRF</Tooltip> data is transcribed and against which the monitor performs Source Data Verification.

## Medical oversight documentation

GCP requires the QI/PI to document medical oversight of specific categories of documents throughout the study. This is not a formality — it is the mechanism by which the record reflects that a physician reviewed safety-relevant information and made a professional judgment about its implications.

Documents requiring QI/PI initials and date include: all SAE reports and external SUSAR reports received from the sponsor, all IB amendments or updates, all sponsor safety communications, protocol clarifications, and any document pertaining to participant safety or data integrity. The QI/PI's initials and date on these documents are what establish that they were actually read — not just received and filed.

If the QI/PI confirms oversight by email — for example, replying to a sponsor email to confirm receipt and review of an IB amendment — that email must be printed and filed in the ISF. The email should list the specific documents reviewed, including their version numbers and dates, so the record is unambiguous. An email that simply says "acknowledged" without identifying the documents is not sufficient documentation of oversight.

Any document bearing QI/PI initials and date must be retained in its original paper state. These documents cannot be stored electronically as substitutes for the signed originals.

## Superseded documents

Every time an essential document is revised — the protocol, the ICF, the IB, a source form template, an SOP — the previous version becomes superseded. Superseded documents must be retained in the ISF, clearly marked, and distinguishable from current versions. They are not discarded.

The convention for superseded paper documents is to draw a single line through the first page, write "superseded," and add initials and date. If superseded documents are kept in the same binder as the current version, they must be placed behind the current version with a clear tab separator. If they are moved to a separate binder or stored electronically, a Note to File in the current binder indicates where they can be found.

The reason superseded documents are retained is audit-trail integrity. An inspector reviewing a safety event that occurred in Month 6 of a study needs to know which version of the protocol was in effect at that time. A site that can only produce the current version — without the earlier versions that were operative during the relevant period — cannot demonstrate that the study was conducted in compliance with the approved documents at the time of the event.

Version control is inseparable from this. Every document in the ISF must carry a version number and date. Documents without version identifiers cannot be reliably positioned in time. This applies not only to sponsor-provided documents but to site-generated documents: the delegation log, source form templates, working instructions, and any other document the site creates or adapts for the study.

## The communications section

The ISF must include a communications section for each study. This section captures all significant written and verbal communications relevant to the conduct of the study — correspondence with the sponsor, the monitor, the REB, the pharmacy, and Health Canada; telephone and video conference logs; and any other communication that documents a decision, a finding, or an instruction that affected the study.

Email correspondence relevant to the study should be printed and filed in the ISF during the course of the study, organized chronologically. This includes: monitoring visit confirmations and follow-up correspondence, sponsor communications about protocol changes or safety information, REB correspondence, and any written exchange that documents a study-related decision. An ISF that contains only forms and approvals, with no record of the communications that shaped how the study was conducted, presents a significant gap during an audit.

Telephone and video conference logs — a brief record of who was on the call, the date, the topics discussed, and any decisions or action items — are best documented at the time of the call and filed as contemporaneous records. Reconstructing a conference log after the fact is possible but should be clearly labelled as retrospective.

A separate communication log may be maintained for participant communications — contacts about visits, adverse events reported by telephone, re-consent discussions. This log is part of the participant file, not the regulatory binder.

## Retention and archiving

ISF retention requirements and the archiving process at close-out are covered in Study Close-Out. Retention periods run from the date of study closure — not from last participant visit.

| Study type | Retention period | Notes |
|------------|-----------------|-------|
| Health Canada-regulated studies (drug, device, NHP) | 15 years from study closure | Sponsor-held records: 25 years. Destruction requires written sponsor authorization. |
| Non-regulated studies (observational, retrospective, secondary data) | 7 years from study closure | Institutional policy minimum — individual funding agencies may require longer. |

What matters during the active study is that the ISF is maintained in a condition ready for archiving at any point. A study that is well-maintained throughout will require relatively little preparation at close-out. A study where ISF maintenance was deferred will require a significant reconstruction effort before archiving — and reconstruction of documents after the fact is a documentation integrity risk in its own right.

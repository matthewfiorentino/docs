## Protect the information of participants

<Warning>
Do not allow information that identifies a participant to leave the institution unless the REB has approved it, and then only in accordance with institutional policies.
</Warning>

An AI tool that runs outside the institution, such as a public chatbot, is one way information can leave it. Participants authorize access to their data through their consent, in the belief that the information will be kept confidential.

[The RI-MUHC's SOPs](/sops/cr-014#5-3-2) require you to collect data only for the REB-approved study, and to manage study data in a way that meets the standards for privacy and confidentiality of:

- The REB.
- Quebec's [Act respecting health and social services information](/kb/governance/privacy#which-law-applies-to-the-information-in-your-study) (Law 5).
- The *Tri-Council Policy Statement* (TCPS 2).
- The RI-MUHC's institutional policies and procedures.
- The confidentiality agreement.
- The clinical trial agreement.

Before you enter study data into an AI tool, [check the kind of data and the controls that apply to it](/kb/conduct/data-documentation/data-security#apply-the-controls-for-each-kind-of-data). Consider entering into an AI tool only content you would be comfortable placing in a public document.

If participants' personal information is collected, used or disclosed in the study in a way the REB didn't approve, [report it to the REB](/kb/governance/privacy#report-information-that-is-lost-or-seen-by-the-wrong-person).

## Check what an AI tool produces

An AI tool can produce output that sounds authoritative and can be incorrect, incomplete or biased. Journals and funders hold the people who submit a document responsible for it, whatever tool helped draft it.

### Prepare or review a funding application

Canada's three federal research agencies and the Canada Foundation for Innovation accept that applicants may use generative AI to draft or summarize parts of an application. The named applicant is accountable for the complete contents. [Fully verify](https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/interagency-research-funding/policies-and-guidelines/frequently-asked-questions-use-artificial-intelligence-development-and-review-research-grant) all application content that generative AI produces, and take responsibility for its accuracy, completeness and relevance.

A generative AI tool may not protect the privacy of your application material. Before you use one with sensitive or proprietary information, the agencies strongly advise you to review its privacy policies, data retention practices, reuse terms and security controls.

The agencies don't require applicants to disclose that they used generative AI. If a generative AI tool identifies works or sources that significantly inform your application, you should acknowledge and reference those original sources.

If you review applications for the agencies, do not upload application material, your notes or your draft reviews into an online generative AI tool, or into one your institution has procured and deployed.

The Fonds de recherche du Québec (FRQ) also [recognize that applicants may use generative AI to prepare an application](https://frq.gouv.qc.ca/position-sur-lutilisation-de-lintelligence-artificielle-generative-dans-les-processus-des-fonds-de-recherche-du-quebec-frqnt-frqs-frqsc/) (French only), for example to structure ideas, search the literature or revise, summarize or translate text. Applicants remain responsible for the entire content of their application. Members of the FRQ's evaluation committees are not allowed to enter any part of an application into a generative AI tool.

### Submit a manuscript

The [International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE)](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/) says that AI tools should not be listed as authors. The ICMJE recommends that journals require authors to disclose at submission whether they used AI. [Where to describe that use](/kb/close-out/publications#ai-disclosure-in-manuscripts) depends on what the tool was used for.

### Draft a protocol or another study document

Consider treating anything an AI tool drafts as a first draft, and checking every factual claim, reference, dose and procedure in it against its source before it goes into the protocol or another study document. That includes checking that each reference the tool gives exists and says what is attributed to it.

Consider checking AI-drafted eligibility criteria against each other and against the rest of the protocol too. Once the REB approves the protocol, [only participants who meet the criteria of its most recently approved version](/sops/cr-009#3-0-responsibilities) may be enrolled.

If an AI tool helps you draft [an answer to a data query](/kb/conduct/data-documentation/edc-query-management#answer-queries), consider checking the answer against the source records before you enter it or send it to the sponsor.

## Check an AI tool before your study uses it

If your study will use study-specific software, an AI tool included, the study team should ask [RI-MUHC IT](mailto:ri.it@muhc.mcgill.ca) to evaluate it, through IT Rescue on the RI-MUHC Portal.

[The RI-MUHC's SOPs](/sops/cr-004#5-1-3) require the sponsor or sponsor-investigator to ensure that all electronic software used in the study has been [validated](/kb/conduct/data-documentation/data-integrity#know-who-validates-electronic-systems) and complies with Part 11 of Title 21 of the US Code of Federal Regulations (21 CFR Part 11), the US rule on electronic records.

In a clinical trial that follows the good clinical practice guideline E6(R3) of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH), computerized systems should be fit for purpose, for example through risk-based validation where appropriate. The ICH also sets [what the investigator or the institution should do for the computerized systems used in the trial](/kb/foundations/gcp-quality-by-design#check-the-computerized-systems-your-study-uses).

### Use an AI tool in a drug trial with decentralized elements

## Get approvals before an AI tool handles participant information

An AI tool that the study acquires, develops or alters to collect, keep, use, communicate or destroy participants' information [may require a privacy impact assessment (EFVP)](/kb/governance/privacy#know-when-an-efvp-is-needed). An AI tool that sends participants' information outside Quebec may require an EFVP too. Ask [efvp@muhc.mcgill.ca](mailto:efvp@muhc.mcgill.ca) whether your study needs one.

To add an AI tool to a study the REB has already approved, where the change affects what the REB approved, such as how you recruit or how study data are handled, [submit an amendment](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/study-amendments).

A tool that looks through patients' records for possible participants needs the same approvals as any other screening of records before consent: [an REB waiver of consent and a reviewed EFVP form](/kb/recruitment/recruitment-enrollment#screen-candidates), and [MUHC Authorization to access the records](/kb/governance/privacy#get-authorization-before-using-health-records-without-consent). Whatever a tool suggests, [the final medical decision on each participant's eligibility](/kb/recruitment/recruitment-enrollment#screen-candidates) remains the responsibility of the principal investigator who leads the study at the MUHC.

## Plan a project that develops or tests an AI tool

For a research project that develops an AI technology, such as an algorithm, from data collected earlier, the MUHC's Centre for Applied Ethics is piloting [a protocol template for AI projects](/kb/recruitment/consent-templates#choose-a-protocol-template). The template guides research teams on the information the scientific and ethics reviews expect.

Using information first collected for another purpose, such as care or another study, to develop or train an AI tool is a secondary use. Identifiable information may be used this way without the participants' consent only if the researchers satisfy the REB that [TCPS 2's conditions](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter5-chapitre5.html) are met. For health records held by the MUHC, [get authorization before you use them without consent](/kb/governance/privacy#get-authorization-before-using-health-records-without-consent).

### Test an AI tool that may be a medical device

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Medical device and surgery studies (Level II)</span></div>

An AI tool intended for a medical purpose may be a medical device, and it can be [software as a medical device (SaMD)](/kb/design/software-medical-devices#know-when-software-is-a-medical-device). Health Canada calls a device that uses machine learning, in part or in whole, to achieve its intended medical purpose a machine learning-enabled medical device (MLMD). Testing one in your study may need [Health Canada's authorization](/kb/governance/laws-regulations#check-whether-a-device-study-needs-an-investigational-testing-authorization).

Health Canada wrote its [pre-market guidance for machine learning-enabled medical devices](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medical-devices/application-information/guidance-documents/pre-market-guidance-machine-learning-enabled-medical-devices.html) for manufacturers applying for, or amending, a licence for a Class II, III or IV MLMD. The guidance introduces the predetermined change control plan (PCCP), a way for Health Canada to authorize planned changes to the machine learning system in advance where that is needed to address a known risk.

Health Canada expects the data used to develop an MLMD, or to show that it is safe and effective, to reflect the population the device is intended for. Reflecting the population could include, for example, considering skin pigmentation and biological differences between sexes.

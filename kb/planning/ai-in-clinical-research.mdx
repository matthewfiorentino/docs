Investigators and research coordinators are already using AI tools — ChatGPT, Copilot, and others — to draft protocols, search literature, respond to queries, and summarize documents. This is not prohibited. But it is not without obligations, and those obligations depend on what you are using AI for, which data you are feeding it, and whether the system is used in a regulated context.

<Warning>
  Feeding identifiable participant data into any public AI system (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Claude, etc.) likely constitutes a privacy breach under Quebec Law 25 and may violate your study's consent terms. See [Privacy and Data Protection](/kb/recruitment/privacy) before using AI with any study-related data.
</Warning>

## Why this is a regulated area

Three frameworks converge on AI use in clinical research at RI-MUHC:

**[ICH E6(R3)](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/ICH_E6(R3)_Step4_FinalGuideline_2025_0106.pdf) — computerized systems.** Effective in Canada April 1, 2026, E6(R3) applies computerized systems validation (CSV) requirements to any electronic system used to generate, process, or store data that supports regulatory submissions. An AI tool used to draft case report forms, pre-screen eligibility, or generate a clinical study report is a computerized system under this definition. Fit-for-purpose validation is required; the level of validation scales with the risk and regulatory impact of the system's outputs.

**Quebec Law 25 / LPRPSP.** Since September 2023, any organization doing business in Quebec must conduct a Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA / ÉFVP) before implementing a technology that processes personal information. Feeding participant data into any AI tool — including a cloud-based LLM — requires a PIA and likely invokes the cross-border transfer provisions. See [Privacy and Data Protection](/kb/recruitment/privacy) for the ÉFVP workflow.

**[TCPS2 (2022)](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/policy-politique_tcps2-eptc2_2022.html) — secondary use and consent scope.** Participant data collected for a specific research purpose cannot be used for a different purpose without satisfying the conditions in TCPS2 Article 5.5 or obtaining fresh consent. Using participant data to prompt or fine-tune an AI system is a secondary use that falls outside original consent unless explicitly addressed in the ICF.

### Health Canada and FDA guidance

[Health Canada's ML-MD pre-market guidance (Feb 2025)](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medical-devices/application-information/guidance-documents/pre-market-guidance-machine-learning-enabled-medical-devices.html) and the associated Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP) framework apply to AI-enabled medical devices submitted for regulatory authorization — not to researcher use of general-purpose LLMs. However, the PCCP concept (defining in advance the changes an AI system is allowed to make, and under what validation conditions) is increasingly applied to AI tools used in regulated trial operations.

The [FDA's January 2025 draft guidance on AI for regulatory decisions](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/07/2024-31542/considerations-for-the-use-of-artificial-intelligence-to-support-regulatory-decision-making-for-drug) introduces a seven-step credibility framework for AI used in regulatory submissions. It is a US document, but global sponsors operating at RI-MUHC will apply this standard — and it is instructive for any regulated AI use regardless of jurisdiction.

<Note>
  In 2024, the FDA's internal AI assistant (Elsa) was [reported to produce false citations](https://www.appliedclinicaltrialsonline.com/view/fda-elsa-ai-tool-raises-accuracy-and-oversight-concerns) in research-related outputs. Hallucination is not a minor error in a regulated context — a false citation in a protocol or regulatory submission has real consequences. Always verify AI-generated content against primary sources before it enters any document.
</Note>

## Which context applies to your use

The applicable obligations depend on whether your use is **informal** (drafting, summarizing, literature support — outputs reviewed and rewritten by a human before entering any regulated document) or **regulated** (a system whose outputs directly enter trial records, regulatory submissions, or safety decisions).

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Informal / researcher use">
    This category covers AI used to support researcher tasks where a human reviews and takes responsibility for the final output before it enters any regulated document.

    **Examples:** Drafting a protocol section from a scientific rationale, summarizing a systematic review, generating a grant application, converting clinical jargon to plain language for a lay summary, producing a first-pass budget justification.

    **Applicable obligations:**
    - Do not include identifiable participant data in prompts
    - Verify all factual claims, citations, and regulatory references against primary sources
    - Disclose AI use in manuscripts per ICMJE guidelines ([see below](#manuscript-disclosure))
    - Confirm MUHC's current position on institutional vs. public LLMs before using any AI tool with study-related content

    **Not required:** formal CSV validation, PIA (unless participant data is involved), sponsor approval
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Regulated / data-generating use">
    This category covers any AI system whose outputs directly enter a trial record, regulatory submission, eCRF, or decision that affects participant safety — without complete human re-review before entry.

    **Examples:** AI-assisted eligibility pre-screening where the system's output is recorded without independent re-review, automated query generation linked to the eCRF, AI-generated CSR sections submitted to a regulator.

    **Applicable obligations:**
    - Fit-for-purpose CSV under ICH E6(R3) — document intended use, risk classification, validation evidence, and deviation procedures
    - Sponsor and REB notification if the system processes participant data
    - PIA / ÉFVP under Law 25 if any personal information is processed
    - Full audit trail: the system must reconstruct who did what, when, and with what result
    - PCCP if the system is designed to update itself over time

    Contact the [Research Facilitator](mailto:Research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca) and your sponsor before deploying any AI tool in this category.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Practical guidance by use case

### Protocol drafting

**Allowed, with verification.** AI can generate a first-pass protocol section from a scientific rationale, eligibility framework, and endpoint description you provide. The output must be treated as a starting draft — every factual claim, regulatory citation, dose, and procedure reference must be verified against source documents and current guidance before the draft enters the official protocol file.

Do not copy AI-generated eligibility criteria without independently checking them for internal consistency and alignment with the current protocol version. Eligibility criteria are regulatory commitments; errors propagate through the entire study.

### Literature review

**Allowed, with citation verification.** AI tools can identify relevant papers, generate summaries, and cluster findings by theme. Every citation must be confirmed to exist and to say what the AI claims. Hallucinated references are common, particularly for niche regulatory guidance documents, conference abstracts, and grey literature.

### Query response (EDC / sponsor queries)

**Allowed for drafting; not for final response without human review.** AI can generate a draft response to a sponsor or CRA query. The final response entered into the EDC or sent to the sponsor must be reviewed by the CRC or PI before submission. Query responses are source-verified records — an AI-generated error in a query response is a data integrity issue.

### Eligibility pre-screening

**Conditional.** AI-assisted review of referral notes or medical records to identify potentially eligible participants is permitted as a triage tool, provided a trained team member independently re-reviews every case before an eligibility decision is made and documented. The AI's output cannot be the final eligibility determination. Document the pre-screening process in your study procedures and notify the REB if participant data is processed by an external system.

## Institutional vs. public LLM

<Warning>
  RI-MUHC's position on institutional AI tools (Microsoft Copilot with a data processing agreement, an institutional ChatGPT Enterprise instance, etc.) relative to public LLMs (ChatGPT.com, Claude.ai, Gemini) should be confirmed with the [Research Facilitator](mailto:Research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca) before using any AI tool with study-related content. Institutional tools with data-processing agreements in place carry materially lower privacy risk than public tools with no such agreement. When in doubt, use only content you would be comfortable placing in a public document.
</Warning>

## Manuscript disclosure {#manuscript-disclosure}

ICMJE requires authors to disclose AI use in manuscript preparation — in the Methods section (2024 requirement) and in the cover letter (added in 2026). AI tools cannot be listed as authors. Authors are fully accountable for the accuracy of any AI-generated content.

See [Publications and Authorship](/kb/close-out/publications#icmje-updates-2024-and-2026) for the full disclosure requirements, including what changed between the 2024 and 2026 updates and the implications for industry-sponsored studies.

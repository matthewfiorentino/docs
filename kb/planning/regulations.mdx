Clinical research at The Institute must follow federal, provincial, and institutional requirements to ensure participant safety, data integrity, and ethical standards. These requirements come from four overlapping layers of authority: international standards, federal (Canada) law, provincial (Quebec) law, and institutional (MUHC/RI-MUHC) policy. Each layer adds obligations on top of the previous one — a requirement from one layer is not cancelled or satisfied by a requirement from another. They stack.

<Note>
  <Tooltip tip="International standard for design, conduct, monitoring, recording, and reporting of clinical studies." headline="Good Clinical Practice (GCP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#gcp">GCP</Tooltip> training covers the international and parts of the federal layer. It does not address the Quebec provincial layer or the MUHC institutional layer — which together produce many of the requirements that seem unfamiliar to researchers trained elsewhere.
</Note>

Every requirement you encounter in clinical research at the MUHC comes from one of four sources. Understanding which layer a requirement comes from is usually the fastest way to understand why it exists.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Layer 1 — International (ICH GCP, Declaration of Helsinki, ISO 14155)">
    The international layer covers participant protection principles, data integrity, sponsor and investigator obligations, and safety reporting. ICH GCP E6(R3) and ISO 14155 have no direct legal force on their own — their binding effect in Canada comes from the federal layer, which requires compliance with these standards as a condition of conducting regulated trials.

    ICH E6(R3) was adopted by Health Canada effective April 1, 2026. Health Canada implemented Annex 2 (covering non-traditional trial designs including decentralized and real-world data trials) on November 4, 2026.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Layer 2 — Federal (Health Canada / TCPS2)">
    Health Canada regulates clinical research involving investigational drugs, natural health products, and medical devices under the authority of the Food and Drugs Act. These regulations set when a trial needs Health Canada's authorization, and they require SUSARs to be reported to Health Canada.

    The sponsor of a drug trial may proceed if Health Canada does not object within 30 days of receiving the CTA; Health Canada usually sends a <Tooltip tip="Letter from Health Canada confirming a CTA is satisfactory and authorizing the sponsor to proceed." headline="No Objection Letter (NOL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#nol">No Objection Letter</Tooltip>. For a natural health product, Health Canada authorizes the trial by sending a notice. For a medical device, Health Canada's 30-day review of an ITA application is an estimated target, and Health Canada issues the authorization once it finds the application satisfactory.

    TCPS2 is Canada's national research ethics policy. It is not statute — it is a policy condition imposed by the three federal research funding bodies (CIHR, NSERC, SSHRC) on any institution that receives their funding. The MUHC <Tooltip tip="The ethics committee that reviews and approves research involving human participants. Also called CER at French-language institutions." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#reb">REB</Tooltip> has adopted TCPS2 as binding policy for all research under its jurisdiction, regardless of funding source.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Layer 3 — Provincial (Quebec)">
    Quebec provincial law creates a distinct layer that applies because the MUHC is a Quebec public health institution. Researchers who trained or worked in other provinces frequently encounter these requirements as unfamiliar — they are not covered by GCP training.

    **Civil Code consent rules (Articles 20, 21, 25):**
    - **Article 20 CCQ:** A capable adult may take part in research that could interfere with their integrity only if the risk is not disproportionate to the benefit that can reasonably be expected, and the project must be approved and monitored by a research ethics committee.
    - **Article 21 CCQ:** Governs research with minors and adults who cannot consent. A minor or an adult who cannot consent, and who understands the nature and consequences of the research and objects to taking part, cannot take part, whoever has consented for them. The research must be approved and monitored by a research ethics committee that the Minister of Health and Social Services has formed or designated; the MUHC REB is a designated one.
    - **Article 25 CCQ:** Allows only an indemnity for the loss and inconvenience a participant suffers, and no other financial reward. Stricter than TCPS2 and ICH GCP, which permit payment to healthy Phase I volunteers.

    The RI-MUHC's SOPs add that [the treating physician should not be the one who obtains consent](/kb/recruitment/consent#choose-who-obtains-consent), unless the REB approves.

    **MSSS Cadre de référence:**
    The _Cadre de référence ministériel_ (MSSS, 2020) establishes the governance structure for human research in Quebec's public health network. It is the source of the Personne formellement mandatée (PFM) requirement, Human Research Privileges and Researcher Status, and the triple review (scientific review, ethics review, institutional feasibility review).

    **Law 25 and Law 5:**
    Any research project at the MUHC that involves access to identifiable health data without participant consent, transfers personal health data outside Quebec, or deploys technology to collect personal data must complete a privacy impact assessment (EFVP) before those activities begin.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Layer 4 — Institutional (MUHC / RI-MUHC)">
    The institutional layer operationalizes the three layers above into site-specific practice. The RI-MUHC SOPs translate federal and provincial requirements into step-by-step procedures calibrated for the MUHC environment.

    The RI-MUHC <Tooltip tip="The RI-MUHC's Quality Assurance department, which checks investigators' credentials and training, audits research and maintains the SOPs. In GCP, quality assurance is the planned, systematic actions that ensure a trial complies with GCP." headline="Quality Assurance (QA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#qa">Quality Assurance</Tooltip> Department sits within the review process as the party responsible for confirming that training levels are appropriate, that investigator credentials are current, and that the study is feasible from a compliance standpoint before submission proceeds to the Personne mandatée.

    Key institutional requirements not covered by GCP training include: Human Research Privileges or Researcher Status, the MUHC Confidentiality Agreement, <Tooltip tip="The MUHC's final authorization for a study to begin, issued by the Personne mandatée once the scientific, ethics and institutional feasibility reviews are favourable." headline="MUHC Authorization" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#muhc-auth">MUHC Authorization</Tooltip> letter, RI-MUHC SOP compliance, QA feasibility review, OACIS access protocol, and departmental agreements (pharmacy, lab, nursing).
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Health Canada submissions

Health Canada regulates clinical trials involving drugs, natural health products, and medical devices. Depending on your study, you may need Health Canada's authorization before it starts ([Study Submission and Review](/kb/submission/submission-overview#health-canada-authorization)).

<Note>
  Health Canada does not issue a formal "approval" for clinical trials. It issues a letter confirming it has no objection to the trial proceeding, or an authorization to conduct investigational testing. The terminology differs by track.
</Note>

### Submission tracks

| Study type | Submission required | HC response | HC directorate | RI-MUHC SOP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Drug, biologic, radiopharmaceutical, or gene/cell therapy | Clinical Trial Application (CTA) | No Objection Letter (NOL) | TPD or BGTD | [SOP-CR-018](/sops/cr-018) |
| Natural health product (NHP) | NHP Clinical Trial Application (NHP CTA) | Notice of Authorization (NOA) | NNHPD | [SOP-CR-023](/sops/cr-023) |
| Medical device or IVDD (Class II, III, IV) that [needs an ITA](#medical-device-classification) | Investigational Testing Authorization (ITA) | Investigational Testing Authorization (ITA) | MDB | [SOP-CR-024](/sops/cr-024) (investigator-initiated, non-IVDD) |
| Observational study or biological specimens only | Not required | — | — | — |

### What Health Canada issues — and what it means

| Document | Applies to | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| No Objection Letter (NOL) | CTA (drugs) | Health Canada reviewed the submission and has no objection to the trial proceeding. It does not constitute formal approval of the drug or the study design. |
| Notice of Authorization (NOA) | NHP CTA | Health Canada authorizes the clinical trial involving the natural health product. |
| Investigational Testing Authorization (ITA) | Medical devices | Health Canada authorizes the investigational testing of the medical device. |

## Medical device classification

Not all medical device studies require an ITA. Class I devices, the lowest-risk class, are exempt; a device's class depends on its labelling and on how the study uses it. A Class II, III or IV device needs an ITA when it is not licensed in Canada, or when a licensed one is used outside its licensed indications in a study the manufacturer sponsors or that is meant to support a licence application. A study that uses a licensed device within its licensed indications needs none. Neither does a study that the manufacturer does not sponsor and that uses a licensed device outside its indications without aiming to support a licence application (off-label use), nor a physician's use, at one site and only on their own patients, of a device of which they are the legal manufacturer, when they alone conduct the testing. Health Canada has separate guidance for in vitro diagnostic devices. If you are unsure whether your study needs an ITA, ask Health Canada's investigational testing division (it-ee@hc-sc.gc.ca); the RI-MUHC's SOPs strongly recommend asking it before testing a device a physician made.

| Class | Risk level and examples |
|---|---|
| Class I | Lowest risk. Examples: tongue depressors, gauze bandages, wheelchairs (manual or powered). |
| Class II | Low to moderate risk. Examples: air-conduction hearing aids, certain diagnostic imaging devices, surgical gloves. |
| Class III | Moderate to high risk. Examples: orthopedic implants such as shoulder prostheses, CT scanners, external (non-closed-loop) defibrillators. |
| Class IV | High risk, often life-sustaining or life-supporting. Examples: implantable cardiac pacemakers, implantable cardioverter defibrillators, coronary stents. |

## Sponsor-investigator studies outside Canada

If your study is conducted in another country, you must comply with that country's regulatory requirements. These may include obligations for regulatory submissions, importation of study drugs or devices, reporting of safety events, documentation standards, and conduct requirements.

For example, studies conducted in the United States must meet U.S. FDA regulations, which may require an Investigational New Drug (IND) or Investigational Device Exemption (IDE) submission.

To confirm whether FDA or other international regulatory requirements apply to your study, contact the [Research Facilitator](mailto:research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca).

A well-designed study addresses six interdependent dimensions before the first submission. Gaps in any one of them can stall <Tooltip tip="The ethics committee that reviews and approves research involving human participants. Also called CER at French-language institutions." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#reb">REB</Tooltip> approval, delay <Tooltip tip="The RI-MUHC's electronic study submission and management platform. All studies requiring MUHC Authorization are submitted through Nagano." headline="Nagano (RI-MUHC)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#nagano">Nagano</Tooltip> submission, or create problems at activation. This page maps each dimension and identifies who at The Institute can help.

Study design is not a methodological preference. In Canada, the category of research a study falls into determines which regulatory authorizations are required before a single participant can be enrolled, what <Tooltip tip="International standard for design, conduct, monitoring, recording, and reporting of clinical studies." headline="Good Clinical Practice (GCP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#gcp">GCP</Tooltip> standard applies, how the REB reviews the submission, and what training the research team must hold. The classification comes first — the regulatory pathway, the design, the training requirements all follow from that initial determination.

## Study type classification

### Interventional vs. observational

An **interventional study** assigns participants to receive a health-related intervention — a drug, device, procedure, behaviour change, or other action — and measures its effect. The investigator actively modifies what happens to participants beyond their standard clinical care. Because investigators are introducing something into participants' lives, the ethical threshold is higher: consent must cover the specific risks of the intervention, participants must be able to withdraw without consequence to their care, and safety monitoring must be in place throughout.

An **observational study** does not modify participants' standard care. Investigators collect and analyze data — from existing records, biological samples, questionnaires, or direct observation — without assigning any treatment. Observational research does not require a CTA or ITA, but it is not exempt from ethics oversight. In Quebec, any study accessing identifiable health data triggers an EFVP under <Tooltip tip="Quebec's Act to modernize personal information protection law (in force Sept 2023). Requires EFVP before sharing personal data without consent." headline="Law 25 / Loi 25" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#law-25">Law 25</Tooltip>, and REB approval is still mandatory.

## Randomization

Randomization assigns participants to treatment groups by chance rather than by choice — by the investigator, the participant, or any other party. Its purpose is to distribute known and unknown factors evenly across groups so that, at baseline, the groups are comparable. Without randomization, differences observed between groups at the end of a trial may reflect pre-existing differences rather than the effect of the treatment.

TCPS2 states that randomization is ethically justified only when **clinical equipoise** exists — genuine uncertainty within the expert community about which intervention is superior. You cannot randomize participants to an arm you already know to be inferior.

Simple randomization can produce imbalanced groups by chance, particularly in smaller trials. **Stratified randomization** ensures balance on key variables by randomizing separately within each stratum. **Adaptive randomization** adjusts allocation probabilities as the trial accumulates data. Both are accepted by Health Canada under standard CTA processes.

## Blinding

Blinding controls for expectation effects by concealing treatment allocation from some or all of the people involved.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Open-label">
    All parties know which treatment is administered. Common when blinding is impractical — surgical procedures, device implantation, or lifestyle interventions where concealment cannot be maintained.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Single-blind">
    Typically the participant does not know their treatment assignment, but the investigator and research team do.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Double-blind">
    Neither the participant nor the investigator knows treatment assignment. The research pharmacy maintains the allocation code. Most Phase II–III drug trials use this design.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Triple-blind">
    Participant, investigator, and data analyst are all blinded. The allocation code is held independently, often by the <Tooltip tip="Independent committee that reviews interim safety and efficacy data and recommends whether to continue, modify, or stop the study." headline="Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#dsmb">Data Safety Monitoring Board</Tooltip>, until pre-specified unblinding criteria are met.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

<Note>
  **Emergency unblinding** occurs when a participant experiences a serious <Tooltip tip="Any untoward medical occurrence in a participant, whether or not related to the investigational product." headline="Adverse Event (AE)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ae">adverse event</Tooltip> and the treating physician needs to know what they received to manage it safely. The research pharmacy holds the code and must be reachable at all times. Any emergency unblinding is a reportable event and must be documented and communicated to the sponsor and the REB.
</Note>

## Controls and comparators

A control is what the investigational treatment is being compared against. The choice of comparator is both a scientific decision and an ethical one. Using a placebo when an effective standard treatment exists is ethically impermissible under TCPS2 Article 11.4 — participants in the control arm must not be denied care they would otherwise receive.

| Comparator type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Placebo control** | An inert intervention designed to be indistinguishable from the experimental treatment. Appropriate only when no established effective treatment exists, or when compelling scientific reasons justify its use. |
| **Active comparator** | The current best available treatment. ICH guideline E9 recommends active comparators over placebo when standard treatments exist. |
| **No comparator (single arm)** | All participants receive the experimental intervention. Common in early Phase I safety work or rare disease research. Single-arm trials cannot show whether the treatment is better than existing alternatives without external comparison data. |
| **Crossover design** | Each participant receives more than one treatment in sequence, serving as their own control. Requires a washout period. Appropriate for chronic conditions with stable outcomes; not suitable for acute treatments or irreversible interventions. |

## Reading a protocol: what to look for

When you review a new protocol, four design elements determine the compliance baseline:

1. **Classification** — Interventional or observational? Does it involve an investigational drug or device? This determines whether a CTA or ITA is required.
2. **Randomization** — How are participants allocated? What system manages the randomization? Who holds the allocation sequence?
3. **Blinding** — Who is blinded? Who holds the code? What is the emergency unblinding procedure?
4. **Comparator** — What is the control arm? Is placebo use justified? Does the comparator reflect current standard of care?

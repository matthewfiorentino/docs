When a researcher both initiates and conducts a study, they hold the obligations of a Sponsor and a <Tooltip tip="The physician responsible to the sponsor for the conduct of a clinical trial at the site. One QI per study per Canadian site." headline="Qualified Investigator (QI)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#qi">Qualified Investigator</Tooltip> simultaneously. Understanding what that means before committing to a study is what makes the difference between a manageable project and an overwhelming one.

## How researchers become Sponsor-Investigators

Most clinical research at the MUHC is industry-sponsored: a pharmaceutical or device company holds the Sponsor role, develops the protocol, submits to Health Canada, runs the monitoring program, and bears primary accountability for participant safety reporting and data integrity. The site QI/PI is a responsible local partner, but someone else is managing the regulatory infrastructure above them.

Some studies work differently. An investigator has their own scientific question — a drug approved for one indication that they want to test off-label in another population, a modified surgical technique, a new diagnostic biomarker, a care pathway that has never been formally studied. They apply for funding to pursue it. When that funding comes through, they are now the Sponsor. They initiated the study; there is no company holding the protocol above them, no CRO providing monitoring, no Regulatory Affairs department handling Health Canada submissions.

Funding for Sponsor-Investigator studies typically comes from grants (CIHR, FRQS, foundation grants, hospital foundation funding), departmental budgets, or philanthropic sources. Occasionally a company will supply an <Tooltip tip="A drug, medical device, or natural health product being tested or used as a reference in a clinical trial." headline="Investigational Product (IP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ip">investigational product</Tooltip> at no cost without taking on the Sponsor role — the investigator still holds all regulatory accountability. **The source of funding does not determine Sponsor status; initiating and holding accountability for the study does.**

Common examples at the MUHC: a surgeon formalizing a modified technique as an RCT; an oncologist running a Phase II study of a drug combination not yet approved for that indication; a rheumatologist testing a repurposed drug in a new population; a researcher validating a new diagnostic tool. In all of these, there is no industry Sponsor to develop the protocol, submit to Health Canada, or report safety events to regulators. The investigator does all of it.

<Warning>
The decision point is early. Investigators sometimes do not realize the full scope of Sponsor obligations until they are already committed to a study — often after a grant has been awarded. The time to understand whether a study requires a <Tooltip tip="Application to Health Canada for authorization to conduct a drug trial under Division 5 of the Food and Drug Regulations." headline="Clinical Trial Application (CTA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#cta">CTA</Tooltip>, an independent monitor, and a GMP-compliant IP supply chain is before writing the grant, not after receiving it.
</Warning>

## What the dual role actually means

A Sponsor-Investigator is simultaneously responsible for two distinct sets of obligations that in an industry study are held by separate organizations.

**As Sponsor**, they are responsible for: developing the protocol; submitting and holding the Health Canada authorization (CTA, NHP CTA, or <Tooltip tip="Health Canada authorization to conduct a clinical investigation of a medical device. The device equivalent of a CTA." headline="Investigational Testing Authorization (ITA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ita">ITA</Tooltip> as applicable); maintaining the <Tooltip tip="The sponsor's collection of documentation demonstrating compliance across all study sites." headline="Trial Master File (TMF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#tmf">Trial Master File</Tooltip>; developing and executing a monitoring plan using an independent monitor; managing the investigational product supply chain including GMP compliance; and reporting SUSARs and safety events directly to Health Canada.

**As QI/PI**, they hold all the standard site-level responsibilities: obtaining <Tooltip tip="The ethics committee that reviews and approves research involving human participants. Also called CER at French-language institutions." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#reb">REB</Tooltip> approval, conducting the study per protocol, obtaining informed consent, maintaining the <Tooltip tip="The QI's collection of documentation demonstrating compliance, study integrity, and participant safety." headline="Investigator Site File (ISF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#isf">ISF</Tooltip>, and ensuring the team is trained and delegated appropriately.

None of these Sponsor-level obligations are reduced or waived because the Sponsor is also the investigator. Health Canada does not distinguish between commercial and non-commercial sponsors. The same Division 5 requirements that apply to a multinational pharmaceutical company apply to an academic investigator running a single-site investigator-initiated trial.

## Monitoring independence

The monitoring requirement is one of the most practically significant obligations for Sponsor-Investigators, and the one most commonly underestimated at the planning stage. Under ICH E6(R3) and RI-MUHC SOPs, a Sponsor-Investigator must develop a written, risk-based monitoring plan and arrange for an independent monitor to execute it. The monitor must be independent of the site operations — they cannot be on the site team and they cannot be the QI/PI themselves.

In practice this means contracting an external Clinical Research Associate, engaging a <Tooltip tip="The team member who handles day-to-day administration and coordination of the clinical trial." headline="Clinical Research Coordinator (CRC)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#crc">CRC</Tooltip> from an affiliate institution, or working with a Clinical Research Organization. The monitor is not listed on the site <Tooltip tip="Lists each team member, their qualifications, and specific trial tasks authorized by the QI." headline="Task Delegation Log (TDL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#tdl">Task Delegation Log</Tooltip> — they are part of the Sponsor-Investigator structure, not the site team. Any site-level quality control — review of source documents, for example — must be conducted by the site, not the monitor, to preserve that independence.

<Warning>
You cannot monitor your own study. A QI/PI who acts as their own monitor is not independent. This is not a technicality — it is a fundamental <Tooltip tip="International standard for design, conduct, monitoring, recording, and reporting of clinical studies." headline="Good Clinical Practice (GCP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#gcp">GCP</Tooltip> requirement. If a Health Canada inspection or an audit finds that monitoring was not conducted independently, the study data is at risk. Arranging monitoring before study activation is not optional.
</Warning>

The monitoring plan must describe the strategy (on-site, remote, or a combination), the rationale for the approach, how the monitor will be trained, and what events would trigger a revision to the plan. [SOP-CR-013](/sops/cr-013) (Study Monitoring and Communication) includes a monitoring plan template and describes the monitor's required qualifications and training documentation.

## Investigational product and GMP

For drug and natural health product studies, the Sponsor — whether a company or a Sponsor-Investigator — is responsible for ensuring that the investigational product meets Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards. In an industry study, the company manages this and the site pharmacy handles local dispensing and accountability. In a Sponsor-Investigator study, you are responsible for sourcing an IP from a GMP-compliant manufacturer or obtaining it through a mechanism that satisfies Health Canada's requirements.

This has real planning implications. A drug being used off-label for a new indication may be commercially available, but confirmation that it meets the applicable standards for research use, how it will be labelled, how accountability will be documented, and whether pharmacy at the MUHC is equipped to handle it must all be resolved before submission. IP management at the site is still delegated to an appropriately trained pharmacist under [SOP-CR-010](/sops/cr-010) — but the Sponsor-Investigator retains accountability for the supply chain above the pharmacy level.

For studies involving a controlled substance, a CTA must be submitted to Health Canada and an <Tooltip tip="Letter from Health Canada confirming a CTA is satisfactory and authorizing the sponsor to proceed." headline="No Objection Letter (NOL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#nol">NOL</Tooltip> obtained before applying separately for an exemption under the Controlled Drugs and Substances Act (CDSA). The CDSA exemption authorizes the specific activities with the controlled substance that would otherwise be prohibited by law, and is valid for two years.

## The QIU and the FDA 1572

For Health Canada-regulated drug studies (Division 5), the Qualified Investigator must complete and sign a **Qualified Investigator Undertaking (QIU)** form. The QIU commits the investigator to conducting the study in accordance with Canadian regulations and guidelines. In a Sponsor-Investigator study, the same person signs as both Sponsor and QI.

The QIU is the Canadian equivalent of the US FDA Form 1572. **RI-MUHC investigators should not sign the FDA 1572.** That form commits the signatory to following US FDA regulations — foreign law that takes precedence over Canadian law when the two conflict. The RI-MUHC and MUHC REB operate under ICH E6, Health Canada Food and Drug Regulations Part C Division 5, TCPS2, and the Declaration of Helsinki. They are not governed by foreign regulations unless contractually obligated.

<Note>
If a US sponsor requests a 1572, use the RI-MUHC ICH GCP Attestation Form (Appendix 4 of [SOP-CR-002](/sops/cr-002)) as a substitute. It captures the equivalent information — investigator qualifications, site details, Sub-Investigator names, REB information — without committing to US regulations. Canadian sites can participate in US IND studies without being listed under the IND, and data collected at Canadian sites can be used to support a US marketing application. Contact QAclinicalresearch@muhc.mcgill.ca for guidance on any specific sponsor request.
</Note>

## TMF and ISF

In an industry study, the sponsor maintains the Trial Master File and the site maintains the Investigator Site File. These are parallel structures — the TMF holds Sponsor-level documents (monitoring reports, regulatory submissions, <Tooltip tip="Suspected Unexpected Serious Adverse Reaction — unexpected in nature, severity, or frequency; must be reported to Health Canada and the REB." headline="SUSAR" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#susar">SUSAR</Tooltip> logs, the master randomization list), the ISF holds site-level documents (signed consent forms, delegation log, local regulatory approvals, lab certifications).

A Sponsor-Investigator maintains both. For single-site studies, the TMF and ISF can be combined into one file, with Sponsor-level and site-level sections clearly distinguished. For multi-site studies — where the Sponsor-Investigator is the QI/PI at their own site but other sites are also participating — best practice is to maintain a separate TMF (covering the whole study) and ISF (covering the MUHC site only). Each participating site maintains its own ISF.

The structure and required contents of both files at RI-MUHC are governed by [SOP-CR-015](/sops/cr-015). The retention and archiving requirements apply equally to the TMF and ISF; both must be accessible for the full retention period, which begins at study closure.

## The 100-series SOPs

The [SOP-CR-100](/sops/cr-100) to 107 series exists specifically for Sponsor-Investigators. These SOPs cover the Sponsor-side operational infrastructure that a commercial company's data management and regulatory teams would normally handle. A QI/PI on an industry-sponsored study does not need these SOPs. A Sponsor-Investigator does.

| SOP | Topic |
|-----|-------|
| [SOP-CR-100](/sops/cr-100) | CRF Design — how to design case report forms that capture protocol-required data accurately and completely |
| [SOP-CR-101](/sops/cr-101) | Study Analysis and Reporting — statistical analysis plan, study report structure |
| [SOP-CR-102](/sops/cr-102) | Protocol Development — ICH E6-compliant protocol structure, verification checklists for protocol and <Tooltip tip="Compilation of clinical and nonclinical data on the investigational product relevant to the study." headline="Investigator's Brochure (IB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ib">IB</Tooltip> content |
| [SOP-CR-103](/sops/cr-103) | Data Management Plan — required for all Sponsor-Investigator studies; also relevant for 21 CFR Part 11 compliance when electronic source data is used |
| [SOP-CR-104](/sops/cr-104) | Database Set-up — validation, user access, audit trail requirements |
| [SOP-CR-105](/sops/cr-105) | Database Maintenance and Management — ongoing data quality, query management, system change control |
| [SOP-CR-106](/sops/cr-106) | File Transfer — secure transfer of data and documents between sites or to third parties |
| [SOP-CR-107](/sops/cr-107) | Database Lock and Archiving — procedures for locking the database after final data cleaning and archiving study data |

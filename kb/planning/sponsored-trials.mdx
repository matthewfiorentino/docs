title: How Sponsored Trials Come to the RI-MUHC
description: How industry-sponsored trials arrive, how sites are selected, what the feasibility process involves, and what sponsor accountability means for site teams.
---

Most industry-sponsored trials arrive through a sponsor or CRO that has already identified the RI-MUHC as a candidate site. Understanding how that selection happens — and what sponsors are evaluating — gives context to every step that follows.

## Who initiates a sponsored trial

In an industry-sponsored trial, the sponsor — typically a pharmaceutical, biotechnology, or medical device company — owns the protocol, funds the study, and bears ultimate regulatory responsibility for its conduct. The sponsor designs the study, holds any applicable Health Canada authorization, and is accountable for data integrity and safety reporting throughout.

Sponsors rarely manage the operational work of running a multi-site trial themselves. Most delegate site selection, contract management, monitoring, and data oversight to a **contract research organization (CRO)** — a specialized service provider acting on the sponsor's behalf. When a CRO is involved, it typically becomes the primary point of contact for the site. The sponsor's accountability does not transfer to the CRO; delegation changes who does the work, not who is responsible for it.

<Note>
Under ICH E6(R3), the sponsor retains ultimate responsibility for trial quality and subject protection regardless of what has been delegated to a CRO or other third party. This principle shapes how CROs operate and why their oversight of sites can be intensive.
</Note>

The research team at the site — the <Tooltip tip="US equivalent of the Canadian Qualified Investigator (QI)." headline="Principal Investigator (PI)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#pi">PI</Tooltip>, CRCs, research nurses, and other staff — is where the study is actually conducted. The site recruits participants, obtains consent, delivers study procedures, collects data, and reports events. Despite working under a sponsor-designed protocol, site staff operate under their own professional and institutional obligations as well.

## How sites get selected

Site selection is driven by the sponsor, usually with CRO support. The process is largely **outbound**: sponsors and CROs identify candidate sites and reach out, rather than waiting for sites to apply. This does not mean sites are passive — how a PI builds their track record and reputation directly affects whether they appear on a sponsor's list.

Sponsors look for a combination of factors when selecting sites:

**Patient population** — Access to the patient type and volume described in the protocol is the primary driver. A site with a large cardiology program will be competitive for cardiac trials; a site known for rare disease populations will be sought for rare disease studies.

**PI expertise and track record** — Publications in the relevant disease area signal scientific credibility. Prior participation in sponsored trials demonstrates that the PI understands <Tooltip tip="International standard for design, conduct, monitoring, recording, and reporting of clinical studies." headline="Good Clinical Practice (GCP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#gcp">GCP</Tooltip> obligations, can manage a research team, and can deliver on enrollment targets.

**Infrastructure** — Sponsors evaluate whether the site has the physical space, equipment, pharmacy capabilities, and data systems required by the protocol.

**Regulatory and SOP readiness** — Sites with well-maintained regulatory binders, trained staff, and documented SOPs present less risk. A site that has been through monitoring visits or audits without significant findings is a more attractive partner.

**Institutional relationships** — Professional connections formed at specialty conferences, through co-investigators on previous studies, or through a PI's training history all contribute to how sponsors find and evaluate PIs.

CROs also maintain proprietary site databases and networks. PIs can register with major CROs to signal availability and interest in taking on new studies, which increases the likelihood of being contacted for feasibility evaluation.

<Warning>
Enrollment performance matters. Sponsors track whether sites meet their enrollment commitments. A site that consistently under-enrolls is unlikely to be selected for future studies by the same sponsor. CRCs and PIs who understand what was promised during feasibility — and flag recruitment challenges early — protect the site's long-term reputation.
</Warning>

## The feasibility questionnaire

When a sponsor or CRO has identified the RI-MUHC as a candidate site, the first formal step is a **site feasibility questionnaire** — a structured document asking for information about the site's patient population, staffing, infrastructure, regulatory status, and the PI's prior experience. It is the sponsor's primary tool for deciding whether to proceed to a site visit.

The feasibility questionnaire typically covers:
- Estimated number of eligible patients the site could screen per year for this protocol
- PI qualifications, GCP training currency, and relevant prior trial experience
- Availability of key study staff (CRCs, research nurses, pharmacist if applicable)
- Facilities: clinic space, pharmacy, freezer capacity, lab capabilities
- Data systems in use (e.g. REDCap, EDC platforms)
- Institutional review process and expected timelines for <Tooltip tip="The ethics committee that reviews and approves research involving human participants. Also called CER at French-language institutions." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#reb">REB</Tooltip> approval
- Any competing studies that might affect enrollment or staff availability

If you are considering having your study operated through the CIM, engage them at this stage — they can assist in assessing institutional feasibility and coordinating the response. If CIM will not be running the study, they do not typically engage at the feasibility questionnaire stage for sponsored trials. In either case, if you are uncertain how to proceed with a feasibility inquiry, contact the Research Facilitator before responding.

If the questionnaire response is promising, the sponsor will typically follow up with a **pre-study site visit** — a visit to inspect the facility, meet the research team, and verify that what was described in the questionnaire matches reality. This is the site's primary opportunity to demonstrate preparedness. Organized regulatory binders, trained and knowledgeable staff, and a PI who has clearly reviewed the protocol make a strong impression.

If selected following the pre-study visit, the site moves into contract and budget negotiation, then formal activation. This process is described in more detail in the Study Feasibility article.

<Note>
If your study will be operated through the CIM, engage them early — before responding to the feasibility questionnaire. They support contract review, budget negotiation, and study activation for studies running through their services. If you are unsure whether CIM is the right fit for your study, contact the Research Facilitator.
</Note>

## Investigator-initiated trials

Not all studies at the RI-MUHC are initiated by an external sponsor. In an **investigator-initiated trial (IIT)**, sometimes called an investigator-initiated study (IIS), a PI at The Institute conceives the research question, develops the protocol, and takes on the regulatory and operational responsibilities that would otherwise belong to an external sponsor. The PI in this case is also the regulatory sponsor — referred to as a **sponsor-investigator**.

Sponsor-investigators carry a significantly expanded set of obligations. In addition to PI responsibilities (ensuring GCP compliance, protecting participants, maintaining the study file), they must also fulfill sponsor obligations: holding any applicable Health Canada authorization, selecting qualified personnel at any participating sites, overseeing safety reporting, and ensuring data integrity across the study.

A pharmaceutical company may still be involved in an IIT — for example, by providing the <Tooltip tip="A drug, medical device, or natural health product being tested or used as a reference in a clinical trial." headline="Investigational Product (IP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ip">investigational product</Tooltip> at no cost, or by contributing a research grant — without taking on the role of regulatory sponsor. The financial relationship does not determine regulatory accountability.

For guidance specific to sponsor-investigator responsibilities and the additional requirements that apply at the RI-MUHC, see the [Sponsor-Investigator Studies](/kb/planning/sponsor-investigator) article.

## What this means for your work

For CRCs, research nurses, and coordinators who are new to a sponsored study, understanding how the study arrived at the RI-MUHC helps explain several things you will encounter during the study.

**Why the protocol is fixed** — The sponsor designed and owns the protocol. Proposed changes require a protocol amendment, which must go back through REB and possibly Health Canada review. The site cannot modify study procedures unilaterally.

**Why monitoring visits happen** — Sponsors are required to monitor their studies. A clinical research associate (CRA) visiting your site is verifying that the data you collected matches the source records and that the study is being conducted as approved. Organized source documents and a maintained regulatory binder make monitoring visits go faster.

**Why enrollment targets matter** — The PI agreed to enroll a certain number of participants during feasibility assessment. That commitment was part of why the site was selected. Falling significantly short affects the sponsor's trial timeline and the site's standing for future studies.

**If your study is run through CIM** — Budget queries, contract amendments, and sponsor communication flow through the CIM team, who act as the institutional point of contact for the sponsor or CRO on administrative matters. This applies to studies that engaged CIM at the outset — it is not a default for all sponsored trials at the RI-MUHC.

Research involving Indigenous peoples in Canada is governed by specific ethical requirements that go beyond standard research ethics review. TCPS2 (2022) Chapter 9 establishes the framework; community-specific principles and agreements add further obligations. This page covers the requirements that apply at the RI-MUHC.

## TCPS2 Chapter 9

Chapter 9 of the Tri-Council Policy Statement (2022) applies to all research funded by CIHR, NSERC, or SSHRC — and by extension to all research conducted at institutions that receive Tri-Council funding, including the RI-MUHC. It applies when:

- The research involves **Indigenous peoples, communities, or organizations** as participants
- The research involves **Indigenous identity, language, culture, or knowledge** as subject matter
- The research uses **data or biological materials** identifiable as originating from Indigenous peoples

### Key principles

- **Community engagement** is required before, during, and after research. Researchers must engage with relevant community leaders or organizations before submitting for ethics review.
- **Research agreements** between researchers and communities should address data ownership, community review of findings, dissemination plans, intellectual property, and community benefit.
- **Respect for community customs and codes of research practice** — communities may have their own research review processes that must be completed in addition to institutional REB review.
- **Benefit sharing** — research should produce outcomes that benefit the participating community, not only the researcher or institution.

## OCAP® principles

OCAP® stands for **Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession**. It is a registered trademark of the **First Nations Information Governance Centre (FNIGC)** and applies specifically to research involving First Nations peoples and communities.

| Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Ownership** | A First Nations community collectively owns its cultural knowledge, data, and information |
| **Control** | First Nations communities have the right to control all aspects of research and information management affecting them |
| **Access** | First Nations communities must have access to data and information about themselves, and be involved in decisions about who else may access it |
| **Possession** | Physical control of data should be with the First Nation or an Indigenous-controlled custodian |

<Warning>
**OCAP® is First Nations-specific.** It does not apply to Inuit or Métis peoples, who have developed their own data governance frameworks. Applying OCAP® broadly to all Indigenous peoples is a common error. Use the framework appropriate to the community you are working with.
</Warning>

FNIGC offers an **OCAP® Fundamentals course** that researchers should complete before undertaking research involving First Nations data or communities.

## Inuit-specific considerations

Research involving Inuit communities in Quebec (Nunavik) follows the **National Inuit Strategy on Research (NISR)**, published by Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami (ITK). The NISR emphasizes Inuit self-determination in research, Inuit access and ownership of data, and alignment of research with Inuit priorities.

For research involving Nunavik communities specifically, researchers should consult with:

- The **Nunavik Research Centre** (operated by Makivik Corporation)
- The **Kativik Regional Government**
- The **Nunavik Nutrition and Health Committee** (for health-related research)

Unlike Nunavut (which has a formal research licensing process through the Nunavut Research Institute), Nunavik does not have a centralized research licensing body. Community-level engagement and consent are the primary mechanisms.

## Research involving the Cree Community

The RI-MUHC has established specific procedures for research involving the Cree community. The MUHC REB has an SOP governing the review of projects that involve the Cree Community, developed in partnership with the Cree Board of Health and Social Services of James Bay (CBHSSJB).

Under these procedures, research involving the Cree community requires review and approval by the CBHSSJB in addition to MUHC REB approval. The CBHSSJB's research review process is guided by the **Miyupimaatisiiun Research Principles**, which emphasize reciprocal relationships between researchers and community members.

<Note>
Contact the MUHC REB early if you are planning research that involves any Indigenous community. The community engagement and review processes take time, and they must be initiated before — not after — the REB submission.
</Note>

## Practical requirements for researchers

1. **Engage the community first.** Before developing a protocol, establish contact with relevant community organizations. Community engagement is a prerequisite for REB submission, not a step that happens after approval.
2. **Develop a research agreement.** A written agreement between the researcher and the community covering data governance, intellectual property, benefit sharing, and dissemination should be in place before the study begins.
3. **Plan for community review of results.** Communities should have the opportunity to review findings before publication — both to verify accuracy and to assess potential community impact.
4. **Budget for engagement.** Community engagement, travel, translation, and benefit-sharing activities should be included in the study budget.
5. **Complete relevant training.** FNIGC's OCAP® Fundamentals course (for First Nations research) and the TCPS2 Chapter 9 tutorial are recommended before beginning any research involving Indigenous communities.

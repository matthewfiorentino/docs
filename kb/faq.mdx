## Getting started

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="How do I start a new study at the RI-MUHC?">
    Start with [My Study Roadmap](/kb/my-roadmap). Answer a few questions about your study and it generates a personalized, phase-by-phase plan covering everything you need to do, in order. It also notifies the Research Facilitator, who will follow up to help map your study to the right approvals, training, and infrastructure. From there, all submissions go through [Nagano](/kb/submission-overview), the RI-MUHC's electronic submission platform. Don't finalize your protocol or assemble your team before having an early conversation — feasibility and design decisions made before submission are much cheaper to change than those made after.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What's the difference between REB approval and MUHC Authorization?">
    They are separate approvals issued by different bodies, and **both are required** before any study activity can begin. The [Research Ethics Board (REB)](/kb/submission-overview) reviews scientific merit, ethics, and participant safety. The **Personne mandatée** is the MUHC institutional authority who verifies that the institution can support the study and grants final authorization. REB approval alone does not permit research activities — the PM Final Authorization must also be in your ISF. See [Submission Overview](/kb/submission-overview).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Do I need Human Research Privileges before I can run a study?">
    Yes. All QIs and PIs must hold valid **Human Research Privileges** or **Researcher Status** at the RI-MUHC before any study activity begins. This is verified at the submission stage — missing privileges will hold up your Nagano submission regardless of where the rest of your file stands. See [Investigator Orientation](/kb/roles/pi-orientation) for how to obtain them.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is My Study Roadmap and why should I use it?">
    [My Study Roadmap](/kb/my-roadmap) is a short questionnaire that captures your study's design, team, and stage. It generates a personalized, phase-by-phase plan covering every step you need to take — credentials, pre-submission, submission, activation, conduct, and close-out — with only the items that apply to your specific study. It also notifies the Research Facilitator, who can help you identify required approvals, flag ÉFVP obligations, and connect you with the right services before your protocol is locked. It takes about five minutes and can prevent weeks of downstream delays.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Who is the Research Facilitator and what do they do?">
    The Research Facilitator is the RI-MUHC's primary point of contact for investigators navigating institutional processes. They help with study setup, Nagano submissions, regulatory questions, connecting you with support services (BCU, CORD, CIM), and identifying what approvals your study needs. They are not the REB — they don't make ethics decisions — but they know the process end to end. Reach them via the [Contact](/contacts) page or the Study Intake Form.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Study classification and Health Canada

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="How do I know what level my study is (I–V)?">
    Study levels I–V are assigned based on design type and regulatory requirements. Level I = drug/biologic trials (Health Canada CTA required). Level II = medical device investigations (ITA required). Level III = other interventional studies (NHP, cell therapy, etc.). Level IV = prospective observational and minimal-risk intervention studies. Level V = retrospective, chart review, and anonymized biobank studies. Your level determines your training requirements, SOP obligations, and which role guides apply. See [Investigator Orientation](/kb/roles/pi-orientation) and [Clinical Research 101](/kb/clinical-research-101).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Does my study need Health Canada approval?">
    It depends on whether your study involves an investigational product. **Drug or biologic** → Clinical Trial Application (CTA); you need a No Objection Letter (NOL) before first participant. **Medical device (Class II–IV)** → Investigational Testing Authorization (ITA). **Natural health product** → NHP CTA. Observational studies and retrospective studies using existing data or specimens generally don't require a Health Canada submission. When in doubt, ask the Research Facilitator early — the classification affects your submission timeline. See [Clinical Research 101](/kb/clinical-research-101).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Can REB review and Health Canada review happen at the same time?">
    Yes — and they should. Health Canada review and REB review run in parallel, not sequentially. Health Canada has a default 30-day review clock. Submitting to Health Canada after REB approval adds a month to your activation timeline unnecessarily. Plan both submissions together. See [Submission Overview](/kb/submission-overview).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the triple evaluation and does every study go through it?">
    Quebec research institutions use a three-part review: (1) **Scientific and ethics review** by the REB, (2) **Institutional feasibility review** by the Personne mandatée, and (3) **Health Canada review** for studies involving investigational products. Observational and retrospective studies typically go through steps 1 and 2 only. The Nagano F11 submission triggers both the REB and the PM review simultaneously. See [Clinical Research 101](/kb/clinical-research-101).
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Training and compliance

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="What training do I need before joining a study team?">
    All research staff must complete two baseline requirements: (1) the **RI-MUHC SOP Reader** — tracked acknowledgement of each applicable SOP through TalentLMS — and (2) the **Competency Assessment**, renewed every 2 years for interventional studies and every 5 years for observational or retrospective work. Drug studies (Level I) additionally require **ICH GCP** and **Health Canada Division 5** training; device studies (Level II) require GCP and **ISO 14155**. No team member may perform any study task before their training is documented and they appear on the Task Delegation Log with the QI/PI's countersignature. See [Training Requirements](/training/compliance-requirements).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="How often do I need to renew my GCP certification?">
    GCP certification must be renewed every **2 years** for Level I and II studies at the RI-MUHC. Many GCP certificates state a 3-year validity period — the RI-MUHC accepts certificates as valid for 2 years only, regardless of what the certificate itself says. Your certificate must be current at the time of study activation and remain current throughout the conduct period. A lapsed certificate is a protocol deviation and will appear on an inspection. See [Training Requirements](/training/compliance-requirements).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What can I delegate and what must I do myself as the QI/PI?">
    The QI/PI can delegate clinical tasks to qualified team members via the Task Delegation Log, but several responsibilities cannot be delegated: signing the protocol, signing the Clinical Trial Agreement, issuing and countersigning the TDL itself, and making final protocol deviation assessments. The QI/PI also bears ultimate responsibility for the safety of participants and the integrity of data at the site, regardless of what is delegated. See [Investigator Orientation](/kb/roles/pi-orientation).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the Task Delegation Log and why is it critical?">
    The Task Delegation Log (TDL) is the document that specifies which tasks each team member is authorized to perform, their qualifications, and the QI/PI's explicit delegation. No team member — regardless of seniority or qualifications — may perform any study task before they appear on the TDL with documented training and a QI/PI countersignature. The TDL is one of the most frequently cited documents in Health Canada inspections. See [CRC Orientation](/kb/roles/crc-orientation).
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Consent and recruitment

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Can I approach a potential participant before they've consented?">
    It depends on how you identify them. Reviewing existing medical records (including OACIS) to identify potential participants before contact requires privacy authorization — this access is typically covered by the ÉFVP process. Initial contact may be made by the treating physician or by someone already in a care relationship with the patient. Researchers cannot independently access identifiable records to generate a recruitment list without authorization. See [Privacy](/kb/privacy) and [Consent](/kb/consent).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Can I perform any study procedures before I have signed consent?">
    No. No study-specific procedure may occur before a participant has signed the current, REB-approved version of the informed consent form — and before the QI/PI or authorized delegate has reviewed it with them and answered their questions. Pre-consent activities are one of the most common REB and inspection findings. The only exception is emergency research with a waiver explicitly granted by the REB. See [Consent](/kb/consent).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the age of consent for research in Quebec?">
    In Quebec, participants aged **14 and over** may generally consent to research independently, subject to the nature of the study and the risk level. For minors under 14, and for adults who lack capacity, consent must be obtained from a legally authorized representative (LAR) — parental authority holder or legal guardian, depending on the situation. Assent should also be sought from minors who are old enough to understand. See [Consent](/kb/consent).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="When must I re-consent a participant during an active study?">
    Re-consent is required whenever a protocol amendment changes information that could affect a participant's decision to remain in the study — new or increased risks, changes to procedures, updated safety data. The re-consent must use the new REB-approved ICF version. You cannot wait for the next scheduled visit; if the change is material, re-consent must happen as soon as practicable. See [Consent](/kb/consent).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Can I obtain consent electronically or remotely?">
    eConsent and remote consent are permitted in certain circumstances, but require REB review and approval of the process — not just the ICF content. The process must ensure identity verification, opportunity for questions, and documentation equivalent to a wet signature. Bill 96 / Quebec language requirements also apply: a French ICF must be available for francophone participants. Consult the Research Facilitator before implementing any remote consent workflow. See [Consent](/kb/consent).
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Data, privacy, and documentation

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="What is an ÉFVP and when do I need one?">
    An Évaluation des facteurs relatifs à la vie privée (ÉFVP) is a privacy impact assessment required under Quebec law whenever your study accesses personal health information **without participant consent**. This includes screening access to medical records and retrospective chart review. In Nagano, there are two forms: **F17** (pre-screening access before consent — e.g., reviewing OACIS to identify potential participants) and **F18** (access without consent — e.g., retrospective chart review or registry linkage). Both are submitted alongside the F11 as part of the initial submission. See [Privacy](/kb/privacy).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What's the difference between de-identified and anonymized data?">
    **De-identified data** has had direct identifiers removed, but a linking key exists — re-identification is possible by someone with access to that key. Only the PI has access to the key. **Anonymized data** has been irreversibly stripped of all identifiers with no possibility of re-identification. The ÉFVP requirement, security obligations, and data access procedures differ between the two categories. Always request the lowest-risk category that meets your research need. See [Data Security](/kb/data-security).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Can I store research data on my personal laptop or external drive?">
    No. Research data — especially identifiable or de-identified data — must be stored on institution-approved, encrypted infrastructure. Personal laptops, personal cloud accounts (Google Drive, iCloud, Dropbox), and unencrypted USB drives are not permitted. Approved options include the RI-MUHC secure file share and REDCap for eCRF data. Data sent to collaborators must go over secure, encrypted channels. See [Data Security](/kb/data-security).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the ISF and what goes in it?">
    The Investigator Site File (ISF) is the QI/PI's collection of all essential documents demonstrating regulatory compliance, study integrity, and participant safety — from feasibility through close-out. It includes the protocol, REB and PM approvals, signed ICFs, TDL, training records, monitoring reports, SAE reports, and correspondence. The ISF must be maintained inspection-ready at all times — not assembled before inspections. See [Investigator Site File](/kb/isf).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="How long must I keep study records after the study closes?">
    Retention periods depend on study type. For **Health Canada-regulated studies** (Level I–III: drugs, devices, NHPs), the ISF/TMF must be retained for a minimum of **15 years** from study closure. For **non-regulated studies** (Level IV–V: prospective observational, retrospective), the minimum is **7 years**. Sponsor agreements may specify longer retention — the more stringent requirement always applies. Note that MUHC Medical Records Services holds participant health records separately under its own institutional retention policy; that obligation rests with the institution, not the research team. See [Privacy and Confidentiality](/kb/privacy) for the full retention framework.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the MUHC Data Warehouse and can I use it for my study?">
    The MUHC Data Warehouse is a patient-centric database integrating clinical and administrative data from 18+ MUHC hospital systems — 4.9 million patients, 7.5 billion facts, going back in most domains to 2003–2008. It is a **research service**, not a quality improvement tool. Accessing DW data requires REB approval, PM Final Authorization, and a Data Use Agreement at minimum; de-identified and identifiable data also require an ÉFVP. Start with a consultation before finalizing your protocol — the DW team can help define your cohort and flag variable availability. See [Data Warehouse](/kb/data-warehouse).
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Safety reporting

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="What is the difference between an AE, an SAE, and a SUSAR?">
    An **Adverse Event (AE)** is any untoward medical occurrence in a participant, regardless of causality. A **Serious Adverse Event (SAE)** is an AE that results in death, is life-threatening, requires hospitalization, or causes persistent significant disability. A **SUSAR** (Suspected Unexpected Serious Adverse Reaction) is an SAE that is both unexpected (not in the Investigator's Brochure) and reasonably related to the investigational product. SUSARs have strict expedited reporting timelines: **7 calendar days** for fatal or life-threatening events, **15 calendar days** for all others — to both Health Canada and the REB. See [Reportable Events](/kb/reportable-events).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="When do I report to the REB versus reporting only to the sponsor?">
    All SAEs are reported to the sponsor per the protocol timeline. REB reporting applies to events that affect the risk-benefit assessment of your study — SUSARs, unanticipated problems, protocol deviations with participant impact, and any event that requires an amendment to the consent form. Routine AEs are captured in the sponsor's safety database and reviewed at the annual REB renewal, not reported individually. See [Reportable Events](/kb/reportable-events).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is a protocol deviation and when must I report it?">
    A protocol deviation is any departure from the approved protocol, regardless of intent. Deviations are classified as **minor** (no effect on safety or data integrity), **major** (potential impact on safety or data integrity), or **critical** (significant risk to participants or data). Major and critical deviations must be reported to the sponsor and REB promptly. Critical deviations require a Corrective and Preventive Action (CAPA) plan. Minor deviations are logged and reported at the next annual renewal. See [Reportable Events](/kb/reportable-events).
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Closing your study

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="When can I officially close my study?">
    A study may be closed when all participants have completed or been withdrawn, all data queries are resolved, all investigational product is reconciled and returned or destroyed, and all outstanding safety reports have been submitted. The QI/PI must notify the REB and the Personne mandatée of closure and submit the final REB report. Closure is a formal process — do not simply stop activities without completing it. See [Close-Out](/kb/close-out).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is a close-out visit and what happens during it?">
    A Close-Out Visit (COV) is conducted by the sponsor's monitor (or by the QI/PI for investigator-initiated studies) to confirm that all study activities are complete, data is clean and locked, the ISF is complete, and investigational product is accounted for. The COV generates a final monitoring report. For investigator-initiated studies without a sponsor monitor, the QI/PI conducts a self-audit against a close-out checklist. See [Close-Out](/kb/close-out).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Do my obligations continue after the study formally closes?">
    Yes. Record retention obligations run for 15 years post-closure (see above). Safety reporting for late-emerging adverse events continues if your product remains under investigation. If results are published, authorship and registration obligations (ClinicalTrials.gov results posting) apply on publication timelines. The ISF must remain accessible for inspection during the full retention period. See [Close-Out](/kb/close-out).
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Research support services

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="When should I contact the BCU (Biostatistics Consulting Unit)?">
    Contact the BCU **as early as possible** — ideally before writing your protocol. Sample size calculations, statistical analysis plans, and randomization schemes are much harder to revise after data collection has started. The BCU also supports grant applications, manuscript analysis, DSMB reports, and Phase II–IV trial biostatistics. Early Career Researchers receive the first 4 hours of consultation free per grant application (up to 2 grants per year). See [Biostatistics Consulting Unit](/kb/bcu).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What does CORD do and when should I engage them?">
    CORD (Capture and Optimization of Research Data) builds and maintains REDCap databases, reviews CRF designs and data dictionaries, and provides ongoing data management support. Engage them **before your protocol is finalized** — database architecture decisions are difficult to change after data collection begins. REDCap is the standard eCRF platform at the RI-MUHC and is validated for 21 CFR Part 11 compliance. The first hour of support per initiative is free. See [CORD](/kb/cord).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What does CIM offer and is it right for my study?">
    The Centre for Innovative Medicine (CIM) provides dedicated clinical trial infrastructure: research nursing and coordination, Phase I overnight pods, oncology infusion, functional testing, specimen processing, and regulatory support — at the Glen and Montreal General. CIM is best suited for interventional studies requiring dedicated trial staffing, specialized facilities, or complex logistics. Contact the Research Facilitator or see the [CIM services page](/cim/services) to assess fit.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the ACT-CTU and how do I access it?">
    The ACT-CTU (Accelerating Clinical Trials — Clinical Trials Unit) offers independent protocol review sessions, access to the OHRI Methods Unit for methodology depth, and connections to seed funding for early-stage investigator-initiated studies. Contact Amanda Lovato to schedule a protocol review or access the OHRI Methods Unit service agreement. See [Study Design](/kb/study-design).
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

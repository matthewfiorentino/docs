Blood, tissue and other specimens taken from study participants pass through many hands between collection and destruction. Each step, from the collection tube to the freezer and the courier, is documented, and some carry legal duties, such as the training certificate needed to ship dangerous goods. A gap at any step can make a specimen unusable or put the people who handle it at risk.

## Prepare before specimen collection starts

The principal investigator, who leads the study at the MUHC, is responsible for making sure that specimen handling meets every applicable regulatory, good clinical practice, sponsor and local requirement. Any part of the work may be delegated to trained team members, but the principal investigator keeps the ultimate responsibility.

Before the study is activated, [obtain detailed instructions for managing the specimens](/sops/cr-011#5-1-1) from the sponsor or sponsor-investigator, the central laboratory or both, and make sure all supplies are available. The protocol, the laboratory manual or other study documentation should include:

- The central laboratory's contact information.
- The requirements for collecting, labelling, processing and storing specimens.
- The supplies.
- The packaging and shipping specifications.

[Assess the health and safety risks](/sops/cr-011#1-1-2) involved in collecting and analyzing the specimens, and the precautionary measures needed to prevent accidents. Make the safety information available to everyone who comes in contact with or handles the specimens, and make sure they receive training in the appropriate safety practices. For biosafety training schedules, write to the [RI-MUHC Environmental Health and Safety Officer](mailto:ri.ehs@muhc.mcgill.ca).

Make sure that staff are qualified and trained on the study tasks delegated to them. During collection, ensure the participants' safety and well-being.

<Warning>
Do not perform a study task until your training for it is documented and the task has been delegated on the <Tooltip tip="The investigator's list of the appropriately qualified people to whom the investigator has delegated significant study-related tasks." headline="Task Delegation Log (TDL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tdl">Task Delegation Log</Tooltip>.
</Warning>

## Collect and process specimens

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Medical device and surgery studies (Level II)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Prospective studies not regulated by Health Canada (Level IV)</span></div>

[Collect specimens as the protocol requires](/sops/cr-011#5-1-5), and identify them as the protocol and the <Tooltip tip="A body, not affiliated with the sponsor, whose main mandate is to approve the start of biomedical research involving human participants and to review it periodically, to protect the participants' rights, safety and well-being." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#reb">research ethics board (REB)</Tooltip> specify. The sponsor's laboratory manual sets the collection tubes, volumes, timing windows and processing requirements for each sample type. Process the samples as the laboratory manual requires.

Departures from the laboratory manual, such as the wrong tube, a missed window or the wrong processing temperature, must be documented as protocol deviations and reported to the sponsor.

The tasks delegated to the clinical research coordinator or another research staff member include:

- Where the study uses laboratory kits, checking that they have not expired, ordering replacements if they have, and using the kit that corresponds to the study and the visit.
- Storing a copy of the central laboratory's requisition with the samples in the freezer or refrigerator.
- Ordering dry ice, where the study needs it.
- Completing the Lab Sample Processing and Storage Log at the time the samples are processed.

## Review laboratory results

[All laboratory abnormalities should be reported](/sops/cr-011#1-1-3) to the principal investigator for assessment. The principal investigator should print, initial and date each assessment of clinical significance ("c.s." for clinically significant, "n.c.s." for not clinically significant). Abnormalities judged clinically significant should be documented as the protocol describes. Clinically significant abnormalities may also need to be reported as <Tooltip tip="Any untoward medical occurrence in a participant who has been given an investigational product, whether or not it is related to that product." headline="Adverse Event (AE)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#ae">adverse events</Tooltip> ([Reportable Events](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/reportable-events)).

The principal investigator should know the results of the analyses related to a participant's follow-up within a reasonable time, with reference to the protocol. By the end of the study, or when the protocol and the consent form specify, the principal investigator should be in a position to know all the results.

Keep the laboratory records with the essential study documents. They include:

- The normal ranges for the study tests.
- The laboratory's certification or accreditation.
- Its established quality control or external quality assessment.
- The laboratory results and reports.
- X-ray films, digital images, microfilms and compact disks.

## Store specimens and monitor their temperature

[Store specimens in a secure and suitable environment](/sops/cr-011#5-2-1) that meets the requirements of the protocol or other study documentation, such as the sponsor's laboratory manual. Establish and maintain controlled access for authorized personnel. Respect the storage time that the protocol or other study documentation sets, and file the storage records with the essential study documents.

Any departure from the required storage conditions is a protocol deviation and must be documented and reported.

The principal investigator should make sure that the refrigerators, freezers and rooms that store specimens:

- Are adequate and undergo regular maintenance.
- Have a single calibrated minimum and maximum thermometer, or another centralized monitoring system.
- Are linked to back-up power in case of a power outage.
- Have an alarm, or are linked to the central alarm system, so that a malfunction or a temperature excursion is detected quickly.

The appropriate Biomed department should be told of any equipment malfunction: MUHC Biomed for MUHC equipment, RI-MUHC Biomed for equipment of the Centre for Innovative Medicine. Make sure that equipment such as centrifuges, storage refrigerators and freezers is [calibrated and checked on a regular maintenance schedule](/sops/cr-011#5-1-3). Keep any documentation of the equipment's maintenance with the essential study documents.

Prepare an emergency response plan to protect the specimens. The principal investigator or a designee is responsible for written procedures for when the alarm goes off or the power goes out, and for moving the samples. The study team should be trained on these procedures, and the training documented on a central Training Log.

If no centralized temperature monitoring system is used, the principal investigator should make sure that a temperature log is kept for each refrigerator and freezer that stores study specimens. The log should record:

- The equipment's brand name, model and serial number.
- Its location.
- The name and telephone number of the person to contact in an emergency.
- The identity and location of the back-up equipment.
- The minimum, maximum and actual temperature each day.
- The date of calibration and its expiry date.
- Comments or an explanation of any temperature excursion.

The initials of the person who records the temperatures, and the date, should be kept on file and made available on request. [The RI-MUHC's Refrigerator/Freezer/Room Temperature Log](/sops/cr-025#appendix-1) is an example that can be used to meet the documentation requirements. The log is an essential document and must be filed in the <Tooltip tip="The qualified or principal investigator's collection of study documentation that demonstrates compliance with regulations and guidelines, so that the conduct and integrity of the study and the safety of participants at the site can be verified." headline="Investigator Site File (ISF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#isf">investigator site file (ISF)</Tooltip>.

<Warning>
If a storage unit goes outside the acceptable range, document the excursion at once, notify the sponsor, and follow the sponsor's instructions before you move or use the affected specimens. Do not assume the specimens are still usable: the sponsor or the central laboratory decides.
</Warning>

## Ship specimens as dangerous goods

Biological specimens shipped off site, to a central laboratory, a sponsor's facility or another institution, may be dangerous goods under Canadian and international rules. Their classification determines the packaging, labelling and documents they need. The classification should be in the packaging and shipping specifications that the protocol, the laboratory manual or other study documentation gives the site.

[Make sure that the packaging, labelling and shipping meet](/sops/cr-011#1-2-1) the Transportation of Dangerous Goods Regulations for ground transport and the standards of the International Air Transport Association (IATA) for air transport. The shipping process should be documented, and the documents kept with the essential study documents. Record each shipment in a specimen shipment log.

| Kind of specimen | What it is | What the shipment needs |
|---|---|---|
| Category A infectious substance | An infectious substance which, if it is released and humans or animals come in physical contact with it, is capable of causing permanent disability or life-threatening or fatal disease. | Classification as UN 2814 or UN 2900, a Type P620 means of containment, specific labelling, and a completed Shipper's Declaration for Dangerous Goods. |
| Category B biological substance | An infectious substance that doesn't meet the criteria for Category A, such as most diagnostic or clinical specimens. | Classification as UN 3373, and triple packaging in a Type P650 means of containment. For ground transport, no shipping document is needed if the package is at least 100 mm by 100 mm on one side and shows the Category B mark, the shipping name and a 24-hour telephone number. |
| Exempt human specimen | A specimen for which a competent professional has determined that there is minimal likelihood that infectious substances are present. | For ground transport, a means of containment marked "Exempt Human Specimen" that will not release the specimen under normal conditions of transport. For ground transport, most of the Regulations' other requirements, including training, documents and dangerous goods marks, don't apply. |

Under the ground transport rule, a competent professional who determines that a specimen is exempt must take into account the person's medical history, symptoms and relevant circumstances, and the endemic local conditions.

For most clinical trial biomarker samples, Category B applies.

Ground transport in Canada is governed by the federal Transportation of Dangerous Goods Act and its Regulations. Air transport is governed by Part 12 of the same Regulations and by the International Civil Aviation Organization's (ICAO) Technical Instructions. Air carriers apply IATA's Dangerous Goods Regulations.

Dry ice is regulated too. For air transport, specimens shipped on dry ice need the UN 1845 designation and specific quantity declarations. Anyone who ships dry ice by air must be [adequately trained for it and hold a training certificate, or work in the presence and under the direct supervision of someone who is adequately trained and holds a training certificate](#hold-a-valid-shipping-certificate). The training certificate states the aspects of transport its holder is trained for.

By road or rail, dry ice in a container designed to release carbon dioxide, so that pressure can't build up and rupture it, is outside the Transportation of Dangerous Goods Regulations except Parts 1 and 2.

## Hold a valid shipping certificate

Anyone who handles, offers for transport or transports dangerous goods, including biological specimens that are dangerous goods, must be adequately trained and hold a valid training certificate. Otherwise, they must work in the presence and under the direct supervision of someone who is adequately trained and holds one.

The Transportation of Dangerous Goods Regulations set this requirement. [The RI-MUHC's standard operating procedures (SOPs)](/sops/cr-011#1-2-2) also require that the person responsible for shipping specimens is trained and holds a valid certification for ground or air transport.

The RI-MUHC's [Environmental Health and Safety team](mailto:ri.ehs@muhc.mcgill.ca) provides the training for the certificate and issues the certificate. As the study team's employer, the principal investigator must not direct or allow anyone on the team to handle, offer for transport or transport dangerous goods unless that person is adequately trained and holds a training certificate, or works in the presence and under the direct supervision of someone who is adequately trained and holds one.

| Transport | The certificate expires | What the certificate shows |
|---|---|---|
| Ground (road or rail, in Canada) | 36 months after it is issued. | The employer's name and business address, the employee's name, the expiry date after the words "Expires on", and the aspects of transporting dangerous goods the employee is trained for. The employee and the employer, or another employee acting for the employer, sign it. |
| Air | 24 months after it is issued. | The same information. Air carriers require it. |

Arrange recertification before a certificate expires. A team member whose certificate has expired must not ship specimens until recertified, unless they work in the presence and under the direct supervision of someone who holds a valid certificate.

Keep each certificate with the training documents in the essential study documents. Anyone who handles, offers for transport or transports dangerous goods, or directly supervises someone who does, must give their training certificate, or a copy of it, to an inspector immediately on request.

Online training is available through the link to the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) under Continuing Education on the RI-MUHC Portal. ## Keep the specimen records

Specimen management produces several essential documents. Keep them throughout the study and for [the required retention period](/kb/close-out/close-out#supply-reconciliation-and-archiving) after it. They include:

- The specimen collection log. The log records the collection date and time, the processing details (the centrifuge's serial number, speed, duration and temperature), the freezer's serial number, the time the sample was placed in storage and the staff's initials for each sample at each visit. [The RI-MUHC's Human Sample Collection, Processing and Shipping Log](/sops/cr-011#appendix-1) is an example. File the log with the participant's research chart, keep it complete and up to date, and make it available for monitoring, audit or inspection.
- The shipment log. The log records the date of shipment, the sample type, the shipping conditions (ambient or dry ice), the courier's waybill number, and any issues or deviations.
- The temperature logs for each storage unit used for study specimens, where no centralized temperature monitoring system is used.
- The TDG or IATA certificates of every team member who ships specimens, filed with the training documents.
- The current laboratory accreditation and normal ranges of every laboratory that processes study specimens.

Monitors check specimen documents during monitoring visits, when they verify source data. Gaps in the collection log, such as missing times, unsigned entries or undocumented deviations, are among the most common findings. Complete the log at the time the samples are processed, not afterwards.

## Bank specimens for future research

When specimens are stored for future research that isn't yet specified, rather than for one study's protocol, consent and governance need more attention. Creating a repository requires the REB's review. The repository is then subject to continuing research ethics review, following the proportionate approach of the [*Tri-Council Policy Statement* (TCPS 2)](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter3-chapitre3.html). For a biobanking consent form or a biobank management framework, [the Centre for Applied Ethics asks teams to consult the REB](https://muhc.ca/cae).

### Choose the consent model

Consent to store specimens for future research can take several forms:{/* TCPS 2 sets the rules for consent to a specific project and for broad consent (chapter 3, articles 3.2 and 3.13; chapter 12 refers to them). It does not name tiered consent: the third item is the Hub's own description of a common practice. Chapters 3, 4, 11 and 12 read Nov. 15, 2026 (evidence/web/tcps2-2022-chapter12_fetched-2026-11-15/). Until that day the lead-in said "Under TCPS2 (2022) Chapter 12" (facts-21, P3-23). */}

- Specific consent: consent for one defined research project only. Using the specimens for other purposes then needs further consent or [the REB's approval of their use without consent](/paths/retrospective#decide-whether-the-study-needs-consent).
- Broad consent: consent to storage and use in future research that isn't yet specified, always within specific restrictions, for example to a field of study or a disease, or excluding use by private industry.
- Tiered consent: participants choose the kinds of research they consent to, for example cancer research only, publicly funded research only or specific institutions only.

To seek broad consent, researchers shall give prospective participants the information that [TCPS 2, article 3.13](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter3-chapitre3.html), lists, as appropriate to the project. The information includes the areas of uncertainty where the risks can't be estimated, access to a general description of the repository and its governance, and any limits on withdrawal.

Researchers or repositories that intend to make their specimens available to researchers who are not subject to TCPS 2 should consider the repercussions for participants. Participants must be informed that the specimens may be made available to such researchers.

When consent to a specific project and consent to storage for future research are sought at the same time, prospective participants must be given the option to consent to each separately, on separate forms or in separate sections of the same form.

<Note>
In Quebec, a part of the body, whether an organ, tissue or other substance, removed from a person during their care may be used for research only with the person's consent, or that of the person qualified to consent on their behalf. After the person's death, the consent of the person who could have consented to their care is needed. Ask the [REB](mailto:reb@muhc.mcgill.ca) how a broad consent under TCPS 2 meets the Civil Code's requirement before you rely on it.
</Note>

### Release banked specimens for other research

Biobank specimens held at the RI-MUHC are institutional assets. Releasing specimens for external research requires:

- A formal access request reviewed by the biobank's governance committee or its designate.
- The REB's approval of the proposed use, or a documented REB waiver where it applies.
- A Material Transfer Agreement (MTA) when specimens leave the institution, managed by the [Research Agreements Office](mailto:contracts@muhc.mcgill.ca).
- A record of the consent's scope, confirming that the proposed use falls within what participants consented to.

An MTA governs the transfer of biological materials between institutions. The agreement sets the permitted uses, intellectual property rights, liability, biosafety requirements and what happens to the remaining material when the research ends, such as its return or destruction. The Research Agreements Office drafts every agreement for studies conducted at the MUHC or the RI-MUHC, and investigators should not transfer materials without an executed MTA.

Every clinical study runs on a set of core documents that define what is being done, why, and by whom. Understanding what each document is for — not just what it contains — is what allows you to use them well.

## The protocol

The protocol is the master plan for the study. It describes the scientific rationale, the research question, the study population, the procedures participants will undergo, the data that will be collected, and the statistical methods that will be used to analyze it. Everything the site does during the study should be traceable back to a specific protocol requirement.

Protocols are written by the sponsor — or by the <Tooltip tip="The physician responsible to the sponsor for the conduct of a clinical trial at the site. One QI per study per Canadian site." headline="Qualified Investigator (QI)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#qi">qualified investigator</Tooltip> (QI) in a sponsor-investigator study — and must be approved by Health Canada (for drug and device studies) and the <Tooltip tip="The ethics committee that reviews and approves research involving human participants. Also called CER at French-language institutions." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#reb">Research Ethics Board</Tooltip> (REB) before any participant can be enrolled. The version and date of the approved protocol must appear on every consent form and <Tooltip tip="Document designed to record all protocol-required information to be reported to the sponsor for each participant." headline="Case Report Form (CRF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#crf">case report form</Tooltip> used in the study. Before the site agrees to take on a study, the QI reviews the full protocol as part of the feasibility assessment under [SOP-CR-004](/sops/cr-004); once the study proceeds, the formal study initiation process under [SOP-CR-005](/sops/cr-005) is where documents are assembled, reviewed, and filed for the first time.

<Note>
**Regulatory basis:** [ICH GCP E6(R3)](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/ICH_E6(R3)_Step4_FinalGuideline_2025_0106.pdf), Section 6, specifies the minimum content required in a protocol. Health Canada Division 5 (for drug trials) and the Food and Drug Regulations require a <Tooltip tip="Application to Health Canada for authorization to conduct a drug trial under Division 5 of the Food and Drug Regulations." headline="Clinical Trial Application (CTA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#cta">Clinical Trial Application</Tooltip> (CTA) that references the approved protocol version.
</Note>

From a practical standpoint, the protocol is the document you consult when you have a question about whether something is required, permitted, or prohibited in a specific study. If the protocol is silent on a point, escalate to the sponsor — do not assume.

## The Investigator's Brochure

For interventional drug or device studies, the sponsor provides an <Tooltip tip="Compilation of clinical and nonclinical data on the investigational product relevant to the study." headline="Investigator's Brochure (IB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ib">Investigator's Brochure</Tooltip> (IB). The IB is a compilation of the clinical and non-clinical data relevant to the <Tooltip tip="A drug, medical device, or natural health product being tested or used as a reference in a clinical trial." headline="Investigational Product (IP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ip">investigational product</Tooltip> — its chemistry, pharmacology, toxicology, and any prior human experience. Its purpose is to give the research team enough scientific context to understand the risks they are managing on behalf of participants.

The IB is not a public document, and its distribution is controlled by the sponsor. Each version must be stored in the <Tooltip tip="The QI's collection of documentation demonstrating compliance, study integrity, and participant safety." headline="Investigator Site File (ISF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#isf">Investigator Site File</Tooltip>, and when the sponsor issues an update, the site must file the new version and review it. New safety information in an IB update can trigger obligations — such as a protocol amendment, consent form revision, or expedited <Tooltip tip="Any untoward medical occurrence in a participant, whether or not related to the investigational product." headline="Adverse Event (AE)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ae">adverse event</Tooltip> report under [SOP-CR-012](/sops/cr-012) — so these updates should never be filed without being read.

<Warning>
**Do not overlook IB updates.** Filing an updated IB without reviewing it for new safety signals is a compliance gap. The QI is responsible for ensuring that relevant changes are assessed and acted on.
</Warning>

Observational and retrospective studies typically do not have an IB because there is no investigational product. In those study types, the protocol itself contains the scientific background relevant to the research question. For Phase IV drug studies, a Product Monograph may be used in place of an IB. Sponsor-Investigators who need to prepare their own IB should refer to [SOP-CR-102](/sops/cr-102) (Protocol Development), which includes verification checklists for both protocol and IB content.

## Informed consent forms

The <Tooltip tip="The written document providing the participant with information essential to making an informed decision. Both parties must sign." headline="Informed Consent Form (ICF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#icf">informed consent form</Tooltip> (ICF) is both a process document and an evidentiary document. As a process document, it structures the conversation between the research team and the potential participant. As an evidentiary document, the signed form is proof that the participant understood what they were agreeing to, at the time they agreed to it. The content requirements for a valid consent form, and the process for obtaining consent, are governed by [SOP-CR-006](/sops/cr-006) (Informed Consent Forms) and [SOP-CR-008](/sops/cr-008) (Informed Consent Process) respectively.

At RI-MUHC, preparing the ICF means more than adapting a sponsor template — it means meeting a set of Quebec and MUHC-specific requirements that apply on top of <Tooltip tip="International standard for design, conduct, monitoring, recording, and reporting of clinical studies." headline="Good Clinical Practice (GCP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#gcp">GCP</Tooltip> content. **Always start from the CAE templates** (available at muhc.ca/cae). These already contain the mandatory provincial wording, shown in red text, required by Quebec law. If a sponsor provides their own ICF template, that wording must be inserted before submitting to the REB. Without it, the submission will be rejected outright. The Verification of ICF Document (Appendix 1 of [SOP-CR-006](/sops/cr-006)) is the checklist to confirm everything required is present before submission.

For studies involving an investigational product — drug, natural health product, or medical device — the ICF must be printed on MUHC hospital letterhead and include the MUHC Medical Records barcode **FMU-9997**. This barcode ensures the research ICF is filed in the Alert section of the participant's OACIS electronic medical record, making it visible to treating clinicians in an emergency. The CAE templates already include it. Studies that do not involve an investigational product do not require it.

All participant-facing materials, including the ICF, must be available in both English and French. At least one language version must be submitted for initial REB review; the translated version must be submitted and approved before the study opens to participants. Translation should be done by a qualified translator; a Translation Certificate (Appendix 2 of [SOP-CR-006](/sops/cr-006)) must be completed. Submit participant materials to the REB in editable Word format — not PDF.

<Warning>
**Version numbering on every page.** Every ICF page must carry the version number and date, and the file name must reflect the same. Health Canada inspection findings have specifically cited ICF file names where the version was not updated when content changed. This applies to all participant-facing materials, not just the ICF.
</Warning>

<Note>
**Version matching in the ISF.** Every signed consent form must reference the protocol version that was current at the time of signature. A mismatch between the consent version and the protocol version at the time of enrolment is an audit finding.
</Note>

If your study is a clinical trial, the TCPS2 requires registration in a publicly accessible registry before the first participant is enrolled. The registry name, URL, and unique registration number must appear in the REB-approved ICF. Register on ClinicalTrials.gov before submitting.

When the protocol changes in ways that affect participant risk, procedures, or rights, the consent form must be updated and re-approved before the change takes effect. Participants already enrolled and affected by the change must be re-consented.

## Recruitment materials

All materials used to recruit participants — posters, flyers, social media content, phone scripts, press releases, MUHC screen content, videos — must be submitted to the REB for approval via <Tooltip tip="The RI-MUHC's electronic study submission and management platform. All studies requiring MUHC Authorization are submitted through Nagano." headline="Nagano (RI-MUHC)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#nagano">Nagano</Tooltip> as part of the initial study application. This is not optional and not a post-approval step. The REB reviews recruitment materials for undue influence, coercive tone, and accuracy of content. [SOP-CR-009](/sops/cr-009) covers the full recruitment procedures including screening logs and eligibility documentation.

After REB approval, materials intended for display at the MUHC — walls, screens, emails to MUHC patients, social media posted on MUHC channels — require a separate approval from MUHC Communications at communications@muhc.mcgill.ca. This step happens outside Nagano and only after REB approval is confirmed. It is not run in parallel.

<Warning>
**Required on all recruitment materials:** The QI/PI's full name, title, and an MUHC or RI-MUHC phone number. Personal phone numbers and personal email addresses are not acceptable contact information on any recruitment material. All materials must be available in both English and French and carry the RI-MUHC or MUHC logo.
</Warning>

## Case Report Forms

Case Report Forms (CRFs) are the structured instruments used to record the data specified in the protocol. Every data point the protocol requires to be collected has a corresponding field in the CRF. The relationship is intentional: the CRF does not define what is collected — the protocol does — and any discrepancy between the two is a protocol deviation. The standards for how data is entered, corrected, and queried are defined in [SOP-CR-014](/sops/cr-014).

In modern clinical trials, CRFs are usually electronic (eCRFs), delivered through a sponsor-provided clinical data management platform such as REDCap, Medidata Rave, or similar systems. Paper CRFs still appear in older or lower-resource studies. Whether paper or electronic, the data integrity requirements are the same: entries must be attributable to a specific person, legible, contemporaneous, original, and accurate — the ALCOA principles that underpin GCP data management.

| ALCOA principle | What it means in practice |
|----------------|--------------------------|
| Attributable | Every entry can be traced to the person who made it, with a date and time. In eCRFs, system audit trails handle this automatically. In paper, initials and date are required. |
| Legible | The record can be read and understood, now and in the future. Paper CRFs with illegible handwriting are a frequent audit finding. |
| Contemporaneous | Data is recorded at the time the observation or activity occurs, not reconstructed later. |
| Original | The first recorded observation — or a certified copy — is what is on file. Transcription from one source to another requires a clear chain of custody. |
| Accurate | The data reflects what actually happened, with corrections documented (not obscured). |

Corrections to CRF data must never be made by overwriting, using correction fluid, or making the original entry unreadable. The standard is a single line through the error, the correction, the reason (if not obvious), and the initials and date of the person making the change. Electronic systems enforce these rules through audit trail functionality.

## Essential documents and the ISF

[ICH GCP E6(R3)](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/ICH_E6(R3)_Step4_FinalGuideline_2025_0106.pdf) defines a category of documents called essential documents — those that, taken together, allow an auditor or inspector to evaluate how the trial was conducted and whether the data can be relied upon. These documents must be maintained in the Investigator Site File (ISF) at the site. [SOP-CR-015](/sops/cr-015) governs how the ISF is organized, what it must contain, and who is responsible for keeping it current at RI-MUHC.

The ISF is not an archive created at study close-out. It is a living file that is built and maintained throughout the study. The test is simple: at any moment during the study, someone should be able to open the ISF and verify that the study is being conducted correctly, with the right people, using the right approved documents, at a site with the right qualifications. If that is not possible, the ISF is not current.

<Note>
**ISF readiness:** Monitors check the ISF at every monitoring visit, as described in [SOP-CR-013](/sops/cr-013). A disorganized or incomplete ISF signals broader data quality problems. Maintaining it continuously is far less work than reconstructing it under pressure before a visit.
</Note>

Essential documents fall into three phases: those required before the study begins (regulatory approvals, protocol, consent forms, QI CVs, lab certifications), those added during the study (updated IBs, protocol amendments, REB renewals, monitoring reports), and those required at close-out (final participant listing, drug accountability records, audit certificate if applicable). Study close-out procedures, including the final review and archiving of essential documents, are covered in [SOP-CR-016](/sops/cr-016).

## Amendments and version control

Protocols, consent forms, and the IB are living documents. When a significant change is required — a new inclusion criterion, a revised dose, an updated safety section — the sponsor issues an amendment. Amendments must go through the same approval chain as the original document: Health Canada authorization (where required) and REB approval before the change takes effect at the site. The process for tracking ongoing REB approvals is addressed in [SOP-CR-026](/sops/cr-026) (Ongoing REB Review and Reportable Events).

Version control exists for a reason that goes beyond paperwork compliance. When something goes wrong — an adverse event, an audit finding, a data query — investigators and inspectors need to know exactly which version of the protocol was in effect at the time. If documents lack clear version numbers and dates, that reconstruction becomes difficult or impossible. Every page of a protocol or consent form should carry the version number and date.

<Warning>
**Common error:** Implementing a protocol amendment at the site before REB approval has been received. The amendment may be authorized by Health Canada and the sponsor, but it cannot be implemented until the REB has also reviewed and approved it. These are two separate requirements.
</Warning>

When a new version of a document is approved, the previous version does not disappear from the ISF. Superseded versions are retained and clearly marked. Auditors routinely examine prior versions to verify that the approval sequence was followed correctly.

## Site responsibilities

The site does not write most of these documents — that is the sponsor's role. What the site is responsible for is receiving, reviewing, filing, and acting on them correctly. In practice this means:

- **Protocol review at activation** — Before the study opens, the QI and <Tooltip tip="The team member who handles day-to-day administration and coordination of the clinical trial." headline="Clinical Research Coordinator (CRC)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#crc">CRC</Tooltip> should read the full protocol, not just the summary. Questions about procedures, eligibility criteria, or safety monitoring requirements should be resolved with the sponsor before enrolment begins. The formal pre-activation review is structured by [SOP-CR-005](/sops/cr-005).
- **Consent form accuracy** — The site must ensure the consent form accurately reflects the approved protocol. If the sponsor provides a template that does not reflect the local context or RI-MUHC-specific requirements, the site is responsible for the correction before submission to the REB. [SOP-CR-006](/sops/cr-006) specifies what RI-MUHC consent forms must include.
- **CRF completion timeliness** — Sponsors set data entry windows (typically 24–48 hours for serious adverse events, 5–7 business days for routine visit data). Missing these windows generates queries and creates data integrity problems. See [SOP-CR-014](/sops/cr-014) for data management requirements.
- **ISF maintenance** — The designated CRC is typically responsible for keeping the ISF current. The QI is ultimately accountable for its accuracy and completeness. [SOP-CR-015](/sops/cr-015) defines the required contents and structure.
- **Amendment management** — When an amendment arrives, the site must document receipt, submit to the REB, and hold the amendment in a "pending approval" state until authorization is confirmed before implementing. [SOP-CR-026](/sops/cr-026) covers the ongoing REB review process.

The underlying principle is that the sponsor trusts the site to be a faithful executor of the protocol. Everything the site does with study documents — how it files them, tracks their versions, and acts on their contents — is either building or eroding that trust.

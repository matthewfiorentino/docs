The MUHC includes the Montreal Children's Hospital, and a significant portion of research conducted at the RI-MUHC involves pediatric participants. Research with children carries specific legal requirements under Quebec civil law that differ from those governing adult research — and from pediatric research rules in other Canadian provinces.

The core differences from adult research are: who can consent and when, the additional requirement for assent, heightened REB scrutiny of risk-benefit assessments, restrictions on compensation, and the absolute right of a child who understands the research to refuse participation. This page covers what is different, what is required, and how to operationalize pediatric consent and assent at the RI-MUHC. For general consent requirements, see [Informed Consent in Quebec](/kb/conduct/consent).

## Legal framework

Four sources of law and guidance govern pediatric research at the RI-MUHC:

**Civil Code of Qu&eacute;bec (CCQ)**

- **Art. 20 (proportionality):** Research affecting a person's integrity may only proceed when the risk is not disproportionate to the anticipated benefit. This applies to all participants but is scrutinized more closely when the participant is a child.
- **Art. 21 (minors and incapable adults):** Sets the rules for who may consent to research involving a minor. Requires that only an <Tooltip tip="A Research Ethics Board designated by the Ministre de la Sant&eacute; et des Services sociaux to review research involving minors and incapable adults under CCQ Art. 21." headline="MSSS-Designated REB" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#reb">MSSS-designated REB</Tooltip> may approve such research. Critically, if a minor who is capable of understanding the research objects to participation, they cannot be enrolled — even if a parent has consented.
- **Art. 24 (parental authority):** Both parents exercise parental authority jointly. In practice, this determines who has legal standing to consent on behalf of a child.
- **Art. 25 (compensation):** Compensation for research participation must not exceed what is necessary to cover expenses and inconvenience. This restriction is particularly relevant in pediatric research, where payment to parents must not create undue inducement to enroll their child.

**TCPS2 Chapter 4** — The Tri-Council Policy Statement dedicates Chapter 4 to research involving children, setting expectations for assent, parental consent, and the obligation to include children in research when the question is relevant to them.

**ICH E11 (R1)** — The ICH guideline on clinical investigation of medicinal products in the pediatric population provides the international regulatory framework for pediatric drug trials, including age-appropriate formulations, endpoint selection, and ethical safeguards. For any study involving a Health Canada-regulated <Tooltip tip="A drug, medical device, or natural health product being tested or used as a reference in a clinical trial." headline="Investigational Product (IP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ip">investigational product</Tooltip> in children, the study team should be familiar with ICH E11 and its addendum (R1), which addresses extrapolation, modeling, and simulation approaches in pediatric drug development.

<Note>
This page provides operational guidance for research teams. It is not legal advice. For questions about the interpretation of CCQ provisions in specific situations, consult the REB or the RI-MUHC legal affairs office.
</Note>

## Consent and assent hierarchy

The consent requirements for pediatric research depend on the child's age and the level of risk involved:

| Age | Consent requirement | Assent requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Under 14 | Parent or tutor must consent | Assent should be sought if the child can understand |
| 14-17 (minimal risk, REB-approved) | Minor may self-consent | Not applicable — participant is consenting directly |
| 14-17 (greater than minimal risk) | Parent or tutor must consent | Written assent from the minor |

<Warning>
**A child's objection overrides parental consent.** Under CCQ Art. 21, if a minor is capable of understanding the nature of the research and objects to participation, they cannot be enrolled — regardless of whether a parent or tutor has provided consent. This is not discretionary. Document the child's objection and do not proceed with any study procedures.
</Warning>

For minors aged 14 to 17, the <Tooltip tip="The ethics committee that reviews and approves research involving human participants. Also called CER at French-language institutions." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#reb">REB</Tooltip> may authorize the minor to consent independently, but only if the research presents no more than minimal risk. This exception requires explicit REB authorization in the approval letter — it is never assumed. If the approval letter does not mention it, parental consent is required.

## Parental consent scenarios

Parental consent in pediatric research is straightforward when both parents are involved and in agreement. It becomes more complex when family circumstances vary:

| Situation | Who consents | Documentation |
|---|---|---|
| Both parents available and in agreement | Either parent may sign the <Tooltip tip="The written document providing the participant with information essential to making an informed decision. Both parties must sign." headline="Informed Consent Form (ICF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#icf">ICF</Tooltip> | Document which parent signed and confirm the other does not object |
| Parents separated or divorced — joint custody | Either parent with parental authority may sign | Confirm parental authority status; note in source documents |
| Parents separated or divorced — sole custody | Only the parent with sole parental authority may sign | Request documentation of custody arrangement if not clear from the medical record |
| Tutor or legal guardian | The tutor signs the ICF | Tutorship documentation must be on file |
| One parent deceased or parental authority removed | The remaining parent or appointed tutor signs | Verify legal status; document in source |

<Tip>
When parental authority is unclear — particularly in complex custody situations — consult the REB before enrolling the child. It is better to delay enrollment by a few days than to discover after the fact that consent was obtained from a person without legal authority.
</Tip>

Under CCQ Art. 24, both parents exercise parental authority together. In practice, research teams typically obtain consent from one parent and document that the other parent is aware and does not object. If the protocol or REB requires both parents' signatures, this must be followed. Check the REB approval conditions for any protocol-specific requirements.

## Assent procedures by age band

Quebec law does not prescribe specific age thresholds for assent. The following are operational guidelines used at the RI-MUHC. The REB may set different requirements for a specific protocol based on the study population, the complexity of procedures, or the nature of the risks involved.

**Ages 7-8 (approximate)**
- Provide a simple verbal explanation of what will happen, using concrete terms the child can understand
- Ask the child if they are willing to participate
- Document verbal assent (or refusal) in the source documents, including who explained the study, what was said, and the child's response
- A separate written form is generally not required at this age, but the conversation must be documented

**Ages 9-12 (approximate)**
- Use a simplified written assent form with age-appropriate language and, where helpful, illustrations
- The form should cover: what will happen, how long it will take, whether anything might hurt or be uncomfortable, and the right to say no or stop at any time
- Read through the form with the child and allow questions
- The child signs or marks the assent form; the person obtaining assent co-signs

**Ages 13-17**
- Use a written assent form that covers the same elements as an adult ICF but in language appropriate to the participant's maturity
- Include: purpose of the study, procedures, duration, risks and discomforts, potential benefits, alternatives, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw
- The minor signs the assent form; the person obtaining assent co-signs

<Note>
These age bands are guidelines, not rigid cutoffs. A mature 8-year-old may be ready for a simplified written form; a 13-year-old with developmental delays may need a verbal assent process. The research team should assess each child's capacity individually. When in doubt, consult the REB.
</Note>

## REB review and heightened scrutiny

All research involving minors at the RI-MUHC falls under CCQ Art. 21, which means it must be reviewed by an MSSS-designated REB. The MUHC REB holds this designation.

When reviewing pediatric protocols, the REB expects the submission to address each of the following. Incomplete submissions will be returned for revision:

- **Risk-benefit justification:** Why the research cannot be conducted with adult participants, and why the potential knowledge to be gained justifies enrolling children. For studies involving more than minimal risk, the REB will require a detailed explanation of why the expected benefits to the child or to children with the same condition outweigh the risks.
- **Age-appropriate consent and assent procedures:** How consent will be obtained from parents or tutors, and how assent will be obtained from the child — including the specific assent form or process for each age group in the study. Submit the actual assent documents with the initial submission, not as a placeholder for later development.
- **Parental consent process:** Which parent(s) will be asked to consent, how parental authority will be verified, and how disagreements between parents will be handled.
- **Child's right to refuse:** How the study team will assess the child's understanding and ensure that a child's objection is respected regardless of parental consent. The protocol should describe what happens operationally when a child objects — including whether and how the objection is documented.
- **Ongoing monitoring of willingness:** How the child's continued willingness to participate will be assessed at subsequent visits, not only at enrollment. Children may initially agree but become distressed or reluctant over time. The protocol should describe how signs of reluctance or distress will be identified and acted upon.
- **Safety monitoring specific to pediatrics:** For studies involving an investigational product, describe how pediatric-specific safety signals will be monitored — including growth, development, and age-appropriate lab reference ranges.

<Warning>
If your protocol enrolls both adults and children, the pediatric consent and assent procedures must be described separately in the protocol and the REB submission. A single adult ICF with a parental signature line is not sufficient — the REB expects a dedicated assent document (or documented verbal assent process) for each pediatric age group.
</Warning>

## Pediatric ICF and assent form design

The assent form is a separate document from the parental ICF. It should be written at a reading level appropriate to the youngest participant who will use it. General guidance:

- **Ages 9-12:** target a Grade 3-4 reading level. Use short sentences, familiar words, and concrete descriptions. Avoid medical jargon.
- **Ages 13-17:** target a Grade 6-8 reading level. The form can be more detailed but should still avoid unnecessary complexity.

Every assent form should address four questions in language the child can understand:

1. **What will happen to me?** — describe the study procedures in concrete terms (e.g., "We will take a small amount of blood from your arm using a needle" rather than "A venipuncture will be performed")
2. **How long will it take?** — visit schedule, duration of each visit, and total time in the study
3. **Will anything hurt or be uncomfortable?** — honest description of any pain, discomfort, or inconvenience. Do not minimize or omit unpleasant aspects of procedures.
4. **Can I say no or change my mind?** — clear statement that participation is voluntary, the child can stop at any time without consequences, and saying no will not affect their medical care

For younger children (ages 9-12), consider using visual aids — simple diagrams of the visit schedule, illustrations of procedures, or a "what happens at each visit" chart. These can be included in the assent form itself or provided as a companion document.

<Tip>
Start from the RI-MUHC assent templates available through the CAE office. These templates have been reviewed for compliance with MSSS requirements and include mandatory provincial wording adapted for pediatric participants. Modifying a vetted template is faster and safer than building from scratch.
</Tip>

All participant-facing materials — including assent forms — must be available in both English and French. Submit assent forms to the REB in editable Word format, not PDF. A [Translation Certificate](https://portal.rimuhc.ca/pls/apex/MUHC2.download_my_file?p_file=249039073901978848) ([SOP-CR-006](/sops/cr-006#appendices), Appendix 2) must be completed for translated versions, just as with the adult ICF.

## Recruitment considerations

Recruiting children into research raises specific concerns about undue influence and voluntariness that go beyond those present in adult recruitment:

**Recruiting from schools, daycares, and community settings.** These environments create inherent power dynamics — children may feel they must participate if a teacher or authority figure is involved. The REB will scrutinize recruitment strategies that rely on institutional gatekeepers. Recruitment materials should be directed to parents, and the decision to participate must clearly rest with the family, not the institution. If a school or daycare is involved in distribution of recruitment materials, the study team should not receive any identifying information about families who choose not to respond.

**Recruiting from clinics within the Montreal Children's Hospital.** When a treating clinician identifies a potentially eligible patient, the initial approach should still follow the treating physician restriction described in [SOP-CR-008](/sops/cr-008). The clinician may inform the family that a study exists and ask permission to have the research team contact them, but the clinician should not obtain consent or create an expectation of participation.

**Therapeutic misconception in parents.** Parents of children with serious or chronic conditions may conflate research participation with access to treatment. Recruitment materials and the consent discussion must clearly distinguish between standard clinical care and the research intervention. Avoid language that implies guaranteed benefit or prioritized access to care.

**Age-appropriate recruitment materials.** If children themselves will see recruitment materials (posters in waiting rooms, for example), those materials should be age-appropriate and should not use incentives — toys, prizes, or other inducements — that could compromise the child's ability to make a voluntary decision.

**Parental gatekeeping.** The parent decides whether the child is approached about the study. A child should not be directly recruited before a parent has been informed and has agreed to let the research team discuss the study with the child.

<Note>
Under CCQ Art. 25, compensation in pediatric research must not exceed reimbursement of actual expenses and inconvenience. Offering payment to parents or gift cards to children that exceed this threshold is not permitted under Quebec law, even if the Sponsor's global protocol includes such provisions.
</Note>

## Team composition and delegation

Pediatric research requires team members with appropriate training and experience working with children. The <Tooltip tip="The physician responsible to the sponsor for the conduct of a clinical trial at the site. One QI per study per Canadian site." headline="Qualified Investigator (QI)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#qi">QI</Tooltip>/PI is responsible for ensuring the team is adequately qualified.

**Child life specialists.** For studies involving procedures that may cause distress — blood draws, imaging, prolonged visits — consider involving a child life specialist. Child life specialists are trained to prepare children for medical procedures and to provide support during and after them. Their involvement is not required by regulation but can improve both the child's experience and the quality of the data.

**Who can obtain assent.** The person obtaining assent from a child must be listed on the <Tooltip tip="Lists each team member, their qualifications, and specific trial tasks authorized by the QI." headline="Task Delegation Log (TDL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#tdl">Task Delegation Log</Tooltip> for that task. They should have experience communicating with children at the relevant developmental stage. A <Tooltip tip="The team member who handles day-to-day administration and coordination of the clinical trial." headline="Clinical Research Coordinator (CRC)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#crc">CRC</Tooltip> who is comfortable and effective with adult participants may not be the best person to explain a study to a 7-year-old.

**Pediatric dosing and medical oversight.** For studies involving an <Tooltip tip="A drug, medical device, or natural health product being tested or used as a reference in a clinical trial." headline="Investigational Product (IP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ip">investigational product</Tooltip>, the QI must have expertise in pediatric care or have a qualified pediatrician on the study team who can provide medical oversight for dosing decisions, safety assessments, and adverse event management in children.

**Pediatric nursing support.** Studies involving blood draws, IV access, or other invasive procedures in young children should have access to nurses experienced in pediatric venipuncture and procedural support. This is both a practical consideration — pediatric blood draws require different skills and equipment — and an ethical one, as minimizing distress is part of the research team's obligation to the child.

<Warning>
**Re-consent at age of majority.** When a participant who was enrolled as a minor reaches 18 during the study, legally effective consent must be obtained directly from the now-adult participant at the earliest opportunity. The parental consent obtained at enrollment is no longer sufficient. Use the adult ICF for re-consent and document the transition in the source documents. This is a common inspection finding in long-duration pediatric studies.
</Warning>

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Medical device and surgery studies (Level II)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Prospective studies not regulated by Health Canada (Level IV)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Retrospective and biobank studies (Level V)</span></div>

A deviation is any departure from the study's current documents approved by the Research Ethics Board (<Tooltip tip="A body, not affiliated with the sponsor, whose main mandate is to approve the start of biomedical research involving human participants and to review it periodically, to protect the participants' rights, safety and well-being." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#reb">REB</Tooltip>), the RI-MUHC's standard operating procedures (SOPs), good clinical practice or, where it applies, the ISO 14155 standard of the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). [Every deviation must be assessed, addressed and documented](/sops/cr-026#5-2-3), whatever its tier.

Do not implement a deviation from the approved protocol without the sponsor's prior agreement and the REB's approval. Two exceptions apply: a deviation needed to eliminate an immediate hazard to participants, and one for an administrative aspect of the study, such as a small editorial change.

If you deviate from the protocol to eliminate an immediate hazard, [notify the REB immediately](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/reportable-events#report-participant-safety-and-ethical-concerns). In a clinical trial of an investigational product, the investigator [should inform the sponsor promptly](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/applications-submissions/guidance-documents/international-council-harmonisation/guidelines.html) after a deviation made to eliminate an immediate hazard.

If the sponsor allows minor changes to the protocol, [its confirmation should be provided in writing](/sops/cr-026#5-3-3). The documentation of this waiver should be filed in the investigator site file and be available to the REB for review on request.

The tier decides whether the study should have a corrective and preventive action (<Tooltip tip="A series of actions taken to resolve a failure to comply with the study's requirements, good clinical practice or the applicable regulatory requirements, and to prevent it from happening again." headline="Corrective and Preventive Action (CAPA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#capa">CAPA</Tooltip>), and for most deviations how soon the REB hears of it. A good CAPA finds why the deviation happened and changes the process, so that it doesn't happen again.

Which deviations to report, to whom and by when is on [Reportable Events and Protocol Deviations](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/reportable-events#classify-and-report-protocol-deviations).

## Classification in practice

[The RI-MUHC's SOPs](/sops/cr-026#5-3-3) sort deviations into three tiers: minor, major and critical. [Reportable Events and Protocol Deviations](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/reportable-events#classify-a-deviation-as-minor-major-or-critical) gives each tier's definition, with examples.

The tiers are easy to tell apart at the extremes. A non-critical document filed late is minor, and fabricated data is critical. The difficulty is in the middle, where most real deviations fall.

### Decide the tier

The principal investigator, who leads the study at the MUHC, decides each deviation's tier, with input from the clinical research coordinator (<Tooltip tip="A nurse, health professional or other qualified member of the research team who handles most of the administrative responsibilities and day-to-day activities of a clinical trial." headline="Clinical Research Coordinator (CRC)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#crc">CRC</Tooltip>). The tier is recorded on the [Protocol/GCP/SOP Deviation Log](/sops/cr-026#appendix-1).

The tiers' definitions turn on three questions. Ask them in this order:

1. Did the deviation involve an unauthorized trial, or the adulteration, misrepresentation or falsification of records? If it did, it is critical.
2. Could it cause undue health risks to participants or others, or could it invalidate the study's data? If it could do neither, it is minor.
3. Did it result in fatal, life-threatening or unsafe conditions for participants? If it did, it is critical. If it didn't, it is major.

### Classify deviations that fall between tiers

These examples show how the questions apply to common cases. The tier still depends on what happened in the study.

| Situation | What to consider | Likely tier |
|---|---|---|
| A visit window missed by one day, with no safety assessment due | The protocol gives the window as a guideline, not a safety requirement. No data was lost. | Minor. Record it and watch for a pattern. |
| A visit window missed by one week, with safety labs due | The safety assessment was delayed. The participant may have continued with an undetected lab abnormality. | Major. A CAPA should be in place. |
| Consent obtained on a superseded consent form, found after procedures were done | The participant didn't receive the current risk information before the procedures, so the consent was not fully informed. Not using the current consent form is one of the deviations the REB wants reported whatever its tier. | Major. Assess whether the participant needs to be re-consented. |
| A blood sample drawn without the fasting the protocol required | The data point may be invalid. Assess whether the sample can be repeated or whether the data can't be recovered. | Major if the data point is a primary endpoint. Minor if it is secondary and the sample can be repeated. |
| A coordinator performed a task before being added to the <Tooltip tip="The investigator's list of the appropriately qualified people to whom the investigator has delegated significant study-related tasks." headline="Task Delegation Log (TDL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tdl">Task Delegation Log</Tooltip> | A person without documented delegation collected the data. Even if the coordinator was qualified, the delegation wasn't documented. | Major. The concern for data integrity is the missing documented delegation. |

When you are unsure between two tiers, consider choosing the higher one. A major deviation that turns out to be minor after investigation causes no harm, while a minor deviation that should have been major is a compliance gap.

## Working through a CAPA

Every major or critical deviation [should have a CAPA](/sops/cr-026#5-3-3), captured on the RI-MUHC's [CAPA form](/sops/cr-026#appendix-2). Its report to the REB should include the CAPA already implemented. It is best practice for the CAPA form to describe what has already been done, not what will be done.

The CAPA's number should be entered on the Deviation Log and on the CAPA form, so that the CAPA can be tracked and closed. The completed CAPA form should be filed in the investigator site file. Send a copy to [Quality Assurance (QA)](mailto:QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca), which tracks trends across studies to improve processes.

To complete a CAPA form:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Describe what happened">
    Explain the deviation: the dates and times, who identified it, the sequence of events, the participants' study numbers (never their names), the people involved and whether any participant was adversely affected. Add the equipment ID or the SOP number where it applies.

    Say how far the deviation extends: how many participants it affected, how many were harmed and how many could have been. Say too whether it could reach other studies that follow similar processes or share staff, and how you will check them. If there are several deviations, describe each one separately.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Find the causes">
    Explain how and why the deviation happened, and how you investigated it. A deviation often has causes at several levels, so look for every point where a process failed. Find the root causes with the "5 Whys" or a similar method, and state each root cause separately.

    The 5 Whys asks "why" again after each answer, until it reaches the underlying cause rather than the visible symptom. For example:

    - The deviation: a participant received the wrong dose of the investigational product.
    - Why? The research nurse read the wrong line on the dosing chart.
    - Why? The dosing chart listed several participants on one page.
    - Why? The site used a shared dosing log instead of individual participant worksheets.
    - Why? No standard template existed for individual dosing worksheets.
    - The root cause: no standard individual dosing worksheet.

    The root cause is rarely human error. If a person made a mistake, something in the system allowed it, and that is the root cause to fix.

    For a more complex deviation, consider a [fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram](https://www.ihi.org/library/tools/cause-and-effect-diagram), which groups the possible causes under categories such as people, methods, materials, equipment and environment.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Describe the corrective actions">
    Describe the immediate actions taken to resolve the issue and reduce the risk. Examples are a documented correction of errors or omissions in the source data, escalation to management and notifying the principal investigator. Describe any assessment of the deviation's impact on participants' health, safety or well-being, or on data integrity.

    Depending on the deviation, corrective actions can also include re-assessing the participant, annotating the data, notifying the sponsor or the REB, and re-consenting the participant.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Describe the preventive actions">
    Describe the new processes put in place to prevent the deviation from happening again. Be specific: who, what, when, where and how. Most effective CAPAs include new procedures, processes or workflows, and training on them. Attach the relevant documents, such as a checklist, a training log or an SOP. For the training, give who will be trained, when, who will lead it and how it will be given, and record it in a training log.

    A preventive action is specific enough to implement and check. "Staff will be more careful" is not one. "Individual dosing worksheets will be used for all participants, verified by a second person before administration" is.
  </Step>
  <Step title="List the other reports the deviation needs">
    Give any other reports the deviation requires, for example to the sponsor, a lead site, Health Canada or a funding agency. If there are none, write "Not applicable."
  </Step>
  <Step title="Plan how to check that the CAPA worked">
    Describe how you will evaluate whether the CAPA corrected the deviation and whether it will prevent it from happening again. Say when the evaluation will take place, what will be evaluated and who will do it. The evaluation can be an internal audit by the study team, or by QA on request. If the evaluation shows that the CAPA didn't solve the problem, say how you will notify the REB and update the CAPA.

    Give the CAPA's closing date, and whether it will be reviewed in six months. The reporter, the principal investigator, the study nurse or coordinator and the clinical research manager sign and date the form.
  </Step>
</Steps>

If a preventive action changes the study application, the protocol, a consent form or other study materials, you must also submit those changes to the REB as a separate amendment. Each evaluation of the CAPA should be well documented and kept in the study's regulatory files.

## Trend awareness

Every deviation [should be assessed to identify trends](/sops/cr-026#5-3-3), with corrective and preventive action on the processes where it applies. [QA](mailto:QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca) reviews the CAPA forms it receives for trends across studies.

Consider having the principal investigator and the coordinator review the Deviation Log regularly for trends.

A pattern of minor deviations of the same kind can signal a problem in the system even when no single deviation is major. Examples are repeated missed visit windows, recurring data entry errors or several errors with consent form versions. When you see a pattern, consider:

- Assessing whether the deviations share the same underlying cause.
- Assessing whether their combined effect changes their classification. A pattern of minor deviations in an area that matters for safety may call for a major classification of the trend as a whole.
- Documenting the trend assessment and any resulting actions on the Deviation Log or in a note to file.

### Track deviations across sites

If you are the sponsor-investigator of a study with more than one site, you [should conduct a risk analysis](/sops/cr-026#5-4-7) for each deviation identified at any site, and rate each one by its impact on the study and the probability that it happens again. Track the number and type of deviations at all sites, to identify trends that may call for an amendment to the protocol.

Deviations a monitor, an auditor or an inspector detects go on the Deviation Log too. [Monitoring, Audits, and Inspections](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/monitoring-audits) explains each kind of oversight.

## Preventing common deviations

Most deviations at research sites fall into a few recurring kinds. Targeted habits and tools can prevent them.

| Deviation | Why it happens | How to prevent it |
|---|---|---|
| Missed visit windows | Visit windows are tracked by hand, or in spreadsheets not updated in real time. With staff turnover, the person who scheduled a visit may not be the person confirming it. | Consider a central visit tracking system with automatic reminders, flagging visits that approach the edge of their window at least a week ahead, and one person per study responsible for the visit schedule. |
| Consent form version errors | The REB approves a new consent form, but the site keeps using the previous version, because copies were printed in advance or the old version stayed in the consent area. | When a new version is approved, consider removing every copy of the superseded version from use at once. Superseded versions kept in the same binder as the current one should be clearly marked as superseded: a line through the first page, the word "superseded", initials and the date. Consider checking the version number and date on the form itself before every consent discussion. |
| Gaps in the Task Delegation Log | A new team member starts tasks before the principal investigator adds them to the log, or a member's role changes and the log isn't updated. | The log [is to be completed before a team member conducts any study task](/sops/cr-002#5-6-1), and should be kept up to date throughout the study. Consider making the log part of the onboarding checklist, and reviewing it monthly against the team and their tasks. |
| Errors in investigational product accountability | Dispensing, storage temperature, or return and destruction logs fall behind in busy enrolment periods. | If an accounting inconsistency appears, [follow up with the participant or the pharmacy](/sops/cr-010#5-5-2) for the participant's safety, and document it. Consider completing accountability records at each transaction rather than in batches, and making accountability a specific delegated task of one team member per study. The rules for storage and temperature records are on [Investigational Products](/kb/conduct/study-operations/investigational-products). |
| Late or missed safety assessments | Laboratory tests, vital signs or electrocardiograms (ECGs) are missed because the visit schedule didn't flag them, or because the participant arrived late and procedures were rushed. | Consider a protocol-specific checklist for each visit that lists every required procedure, reviewed before the participant arrives and again before they leave. An assessment the protocol requires that isn't done is [a deviation from the protocol](/sops/cr-026#5-3-3). That deviation [must be assessed, addressed and documented](/sops/cr-026#5-2-3), and should be recorded on the Deviation Log. Consider also recording why the assessment couldn't be done. |

<Warning>
Do not perform a study task until your training for it is documented and the task has been delegated on the Task Delegation Log.
</Warning>

Health Canada inspects clinical trials to check that participants are protected and that the data are reliable. An inspection usually comes with notice, and the time between the notice and the visit is for preparing the team, the documents and the facility. After the visit, the qualified investigator, the sponsor or both should respond in writing to each observation still open in the inspector's final report.

The RI-MUHC's [guidance for hosting a Health Canada inspection](/sops/cr-017#appendix-1), Appendix 1 of [SOP-CR-017](/sops/cr-017), has the checklists and templates for each step. The guidance has appendices of its own, numbered 1 to 11. An optional course on TalentLMS, "Hosting a Health Canada Inspection", covers the same ground, and the RI-MUHC's standard operating procedures (SOPs) highly recommend that the whole study team complete it before hosting an inspection.

## Know what Health Canada inspects

Health Canada's Clinical Trial Compliance Program, in its Regulatory Operations and Enforcement Branch, inspects the parties that conduct clinical trials of drugs. It may inspect sponsors, qualified investigators' sites, contract research organizations and site management organizations, during a trial or after it ends. It also conducts shorter, remote compliance readiness inspections earlier in a study. As of the RI-MUHC's guidance of April 2024, Health Canada had begun pilot inspections of medical device studies.

A drug trial is inspected for compliance with Division 5 of the Food and Drug Regulations and the good clinical practice guideline of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH). A medical device trial is inspected for compliance with Part 3 of the Medical Devices Regulations and the ISO 14155 standard of the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). Where Health Canada's regulations and international good clinical practice documents differ, the regulations take precedence.

Before an inspection, Health Canada recommends reviewing these documents:

### How Health Canada selects a site

Health Canada selects trials and sites for inspection on criteria of risk. The criteria for a qualified investigator's site include:
- The type of sponsor and the type of site.
- The site's geographic location.
- The level of risk to Canadians, such as the therapeutic area.
- The type of investigational product, novel therapies, and the product's dosage form or route of administration.
- The complexity of the trial's design, including its participants.
- The inspection history of the qualified investigator and the sponsor.
- The number of trials at the site, the number of active participants and the number of serious adverse events or reactions at the site.
- Complaints, and other criteria that Health Canada's review directorates identify.

Before an inspection, Health Canada may ask the sponsor for information about each site: its location, the qualified investigator's name, the study's status, the numbers of participants enrolled, active and withdrawn, the number of serious unexpected adverse drug reactions or medical device incidents, and the parties involved. It may also ask the site's qualified investigator to complete a clinical trial information request form, such as the one in Appendix 2 of the RI-MUHC's guidance.

### Types of inspection and how long they take

Most inspections are announced. Health Canada may also inspect in person without notice, for example when there is an immediate risk to participants' health and safety, or a for-cause concern. A remote inspection always comes with notice.

An in-person inspection takes about five business days on average, and a remote inspection up to ten business days, although more or less time may be required.

### What happens during and after an inspection

During the visit, the inspector interviews the team, including the qualified investigator, the coordinators and other departments such as the pharmacy, reviews the investigator site file and the source documents, and may tour the facilities. At the end, the inspector presents the observations at an exit meeting and then issues the final inspection exit notice, the inspection report.

Where all observations are minor, Health Canada takes no compliance and enforcement action. In any case of non-compliance, the inspection's results go to the relevant Health Canada review directorate, with the Regulatory Operations and Enforcement Branch's recommendation. The actions can include suspending, or announcing the intent to suspend, the authorization to sell or import a drug for the trial, in whole or at one site, and assessing the validity of the data submitted. Health Canada may also investigate potential offences under the *Food and Drugs Act*, with the evidence-gathering powers of the Criminal Code, or refer a case to law enforcement. With limited exceptions, inspectors' powers under the Act don't apply in such an investigation.

## Before the inspection

### When you receive the notice

When Health Canada selects a site, the inspector may first contact the qualified investigator by phone or email to arrange a time for the visit. The inspector then sends the qualified investigator and the sponsor an official Notice of Inspection Letter, by email or on paper. The letter can include an inspection plan, and a copy of Health Canada's *Good Clinical Practice Pre-Inspection Information Package*. Later correspondence is usually by email.

In most cases, Health Canada gives notice before an inspection. Notice usually reaches the sponsor and the site at least 5 days, and up to 4 weeks, before the inspection, and typically about 4 weeks before a routine one. Whenever possible, leave enough time for everyone to prepare.

For a Health Canada inspection, the qualified investigator should promptly inform [QA](mailto:QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca) [within 24 hours of the inspection notice](/sops/cr-017#5-5-4). Telling QA promptly gives QA time to make sure the site knows the RI-MUHC's guidance and to review the documents the inspector requested. It also leaves the site time to put corrective action in place before the inspection.

### Prepare your team

Before the visit, the team should review the study protocol and every study-specific procedure thoroughly. Prepare the team with these steps from the RI-MUHC's guidance and SOP-CR-017:
- Review Health Canada's pre-inspection information, such as GUI-0100 and GUI-0043.
- Hold pre-inspection meetings with the key study personnel, such as the qualified investigator, the site manager and the coordinator, to discuss any issues found and confirm each person's role.
- If the investigator is a sponsor-investigator, review the monitoring plan's risk-based items, and make sure the plan has been followed and every corrective and preventive action is closed. If the investigator is a site's principal investigator, make sure every monitoring record is complete, initialled and dated by the investigator, and every corrective and preventive action is closed.
- Conduct mock interviews, with questions that cover why, who, what, when and where. Review the [conduct during interviews](#conduct-during-interviews-with-the-inspector) with the team before the mock interviews.
- Make sure the departments involved in the study, such as the REB, the pharmacy, the laboratory and information technology (IT) or REDCap data management, are available to answer questions.
- Make sure the qualified investigator and the site's staff will be available for interviews for at least part of the visit.
- Prepare and coordinate an action plan with the team.
- Obtain and review the documents the inspector requested, prepare answers to the inspector's questions, and brief staff before the inspector interviews them.

Instruct the team on their responsibilities and on how to handle an inspection. Staff must answer the inspector's questions only about their own job or their direct involvement in the study.

### Prepare your documents

Organize the documents in a format that suits the type of inspection, in person or virtual, and follow any guidance Health Canada gives on electronic tools and transfers. Identify the documents that will need certified copies. Review every study document for content, accuracy, legibility, traceability through version dates, attribution, timeliness and completeness. Make sure that the information in every document is complete, up to date and in agreement with the source documents.

The RI-MUHC's guidance and SOP-CR-017 list what to have ready:

#### Regulatory and study authorization documents

- The [investigator site file (ISF)](/kb/site-activation/isf), and for a sponsor-investigator the trial master file, checked against the expected document list in their index. Obtain any missing documents.
- Health Canada's authorizations, and for a sponsor-investigator all correspondence with Health Canada.
- The REB's attestation or initial approval letter, its annual approvals, its approvals and acknowledgments of ongoing review, such as amendments, serious adverse events (SAEs) and deviations, all REB correspondence and, if required, the REB's composition.
- The Qualified Investigator Undertaking form for the site, and the Clinical Trial Site Information forms if the investigator is a sponsor-investigator.
- The protocol signature pages and the Investigator's Brochure acknowledgment pages, where they apply.
- Every version of the protocol and of the <Tooltip tip="The written form that gives a participant the information essential to an informed decision about taking part in a study." headline="Informed Consent Form (ICF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#icf">informed consent forms</Tooltip>, in every language used.
- The research agreement, especially the qualified investigator's obligations, and the service, data transfer and material transfer agreements.
- The MUHC Authorization letter.

#### Team credentials and training

- The study team's CVs, initialled and dated every two years, their current licences where they apply, their qualifications and their research training, including those of vendors and third-party service providers.
- Proof of current licensure for every team member who is a health care professional, such as physicians, nurses, pharmacy technicians and respiratory therapists.
- Training on the ICH's good clinical practice guideline and on Division 5 for drug studies, and on the RI-MUHC's SOPs. The All-in-One file of the RI-MUHC's SOPs can be downloaded from TalentLMS.
- Records of protocol-specific training, such as the site initiation visit slides, sign-off sheets and training logs for every amendment, and the sponsor's SOPs.
- The [Task Delegation Log](/sops/cr-002#5-6-1), up to date. Have the qualified investigator correct any error by initialling and dating the change. Check that each team member completed the training the study requires before being delegated a protocol task or given access to study information.

#### Source documents and participant records

- Every source document, such as participants' records, pharmacy records and every signed informed consent form.
- Every completed consent form, on the correct version, signed and personally dated by the participant or their <Tooltip tip="The person with legal authority to consent for a participant who cannot consent." headline="Legally Appointed Representative (LAR)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#lar">legally appointed representative</Tooltip>, the witness where there is one, and the person who obtained consent.
- Every participant log, up to date: the screening, enrolment and participant identification logs.
- Source documents showing the sign-off of every study-related medical decision by the qualified investigator or a designated physician sub-investigator.
- Every case report form, with its queries resolved and the qualified investigator's sign-off, as required and as the sponsor requires. Any data clarification forms.
- Access to the health records of every enrolled participant, through the electronic medical record as institutional policies allow. Past inspectors have refused personal access to the electronic medical record and expected a coordinator to be present to show them the information, or the information printed for them.

#### Safety, deviations and monitoring

- Every serious adverse event report and serious unexpected adverse drug reaction report, with proof that each was reported to the sponsor and, where it applies, to the REB.
- The qualified investigator's assessments of adverse events, and their initials and dates on safety reports. A working instruction for reviewing and distributing reports of suspected unexpected serious adverse reactions (SUSARs), if it applies.
- The Protocol Deviation Log, with every deviation addressed, reported to the sponsor and, where it applies, to the REB. If the pre-inspection review finds deviations not reported before, document the corrective and preventive action and report them to the REB as required.
- The monitoring log, and every monitoring letter initialled and dated by the qualified investigator.
- The sponsor's safety management plan, and any data safety monitoring board reports.
- All correspondence, for example with the sponsor and the REB.

#### Investigational product, equipment and specimens

- The investigational product's accountability records. If the research pharmacy is involved, make sure it knows of the inspection and reviews its records first.
- Maintenance and calibration records for the hospital equipment used in the study. A study-specific equipment inventory with its certificates is preferable to asking the inspector to review the RI-MUHC's general inventory.
- Validation records for the study's electronic systems, such as the RI-MUHC's REDCap.
- For a study with biological specimens, the laboratory's training records, certifications and procedures.

Keep financial information, personnel records and audit reports apart from the study documents. Under the ICH's good clinical practice guideline, regulators should not routinely ask for audit reports.

Before the inspection, document and resolve every discrepancy you find. Late entries and corrections are dated and initialled by the person who made them, with the current date, the reason and the words "late entry". Never back-date an entry.

## Prepare the facility

The facility needs a confidential space for the inspector and the documents, and a way to make and track copies. The RI-MUHC's guidance has a full checklist in its Appendix 1, "Prior to inspection visit".

### In the weeks before the inspection

- Reserve a private room that locks, with washroom access and away from patient care areas and other studies' files, for the whole inspection. It needs reliable wifi, the equipment the inspector asked for, computers and access to the source documents and electronic case report forms.
- Arrange a visitor badge or pass, wifi and electronic access for the inspector, and the inspector's access to the areas they will tour, such as storage and laboratories.
- Gather the supplies for certified copies: a nearby photocopier, a china marker or grease pencil to redact, a "Certified Copy" stamp and a pen to initial and date.
- Prepare a Study Contact List (the guidance's Appendix 6), a Document Release Log (Appendix 7) and Attendance Records (Appendix 9), and have a source location guide or similar navigation document ready.
- Obtain any study documents stored off site.
- Arrange an interpreter, or the translation of documents, if needed.
- Set up a process to track the certified copies given to the inspector. For an on-site inspection, [QA](mailto:QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca) can track them for the team. Keep the tracking record with the study's essential documents.

### For a virtual inspection

- Agree with Health Canada on the platforms: one for meetings, such as Microsoft Teams, and a secure one for confidential documents, on a Health Canada platform or one the MUHC has approved, such as NextCloud. Teams is not considered confidential for sharing study documents.
- Make sure the platform used for confidential documents complies with Part 11 of Title 21 of the US Code of Federal Regulations, is validated, has documentation to show it, and does not fall under the US Cloud Act.
- Check the institution's policies, and the REB's, privacy and consent form requirements, on remote access to source data such as the electronic medical record and REDCap.
- Give the inspector the platform's details, log-ins and passwords securely, and place the computer used to share documents in a closed, confidential area.
- Scan the paper documents requested, and keep a scanner available during the inspection.

### In the days before the inspection

- Make sure every document required is accessible, and store unrelated materials out of view.
- Set up the computers or tablets with the software needed, and confirm access to REDCap or other electronic systems as institutional approvals allow.
- Make sure the teams that work near the inspection room know of the inspection and keep their own studies confidential.
- Review the facilities likely to be inspected, find any issues and fix them where possible.
- Find out which areas or facilities the inspector will want to visit or tour, and make sure suitable staff are available to accompany the inspector.

### On the morning of the inspection

- Put the "Health Canada Inspection in Progress" sign (the guidance's Appendix 5) on the room's door, so that only the people involved enter.
- Check that the wifi, photocopiers and computers work, and place the document release log, the stamps and the redaction tools in the room.
- Plan a route to the room through clear corridors, not past other sponsors' confidential information or busy coordinators' areas.

## During the inspection

### Welcome the inspector on day 1

- Before the inspector arrives, make sure the rooms and facilities they will use or see are tidy and confidential. The door must lock, and any window onto another internal room should be covered with paper. Lock the door whenever the inspector leaves the room.
- Meet the inspector on arrival and escort them to the meeting room, and on any tour, through areas with no other sponsor's confidential information on display.
- Give the inspector visitor identification and access to any database needed, such as REDCap, as sponsor or institutional approvals allow. Remove all access at the end of the last day of the inspection.
- Bring the Attendance Record (the guidance's Appendix 9), and arrange for someone to take notes.
- Offer a proposed agenda for the day. In general, the inspector prefers to set the agenda as the inspection proceeds.

## After the inspection

### Inspection Exit Notice

After the visit, the inspector issues a draft inspection exit notice. It gives the site's profile, the names of the site's officials, the protocol's title and number, and the investigational product, and it is written to protect participants' identities. Shortly after the inspection, Health Canada posts an initial report of the deficiencies found, and after the exit notice is issued, an inspection report card with the observations and the rating, on the [drug and health product inspections database](https://health-products.canada.ca/dhpid-bdipsa/index-eng.jsp).

Each observation is a deficiency or deviation from the regulations, and the inspector rates it under Health Canada's [GUI-0043](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/compliance-enforcement/good-clinical-practices/guidance-documents/risk-classification-observations-inspections-clinical-trials-guide-0043.html). GUI-0043 covers drug trials. Its definitions and sample observations are:

| Risk level | Health Canada's definition | Examples from GUI-0043's sample observations |
|---|---|---|
| Critical (risk 1) | A situation that results in fatal, life-threatening or unsafe conditions for trial participants, or where the integrity of the trial data is compromised. It presents an immediate or latent undue risk to participants' rights, health and safety. It can also refer to trials that are not authorized, and to adulteration, misrepresentation and falsification of records. | Written informed consent was not obtained before participants took part in the trial. The REB's approval of the protocol or the consent forms was not obtained before the trial started at the site. The qualified investigator had no licence to practise medicine in the province. |
| Major (risk 2) | A situation where a marked deviation or deficiency, other than a critical one, may result in undue health risks for trial participants or others, or could invalidate the data. | Participants signed the wrong version of the consent form. Study staff performed protocol activities before they were trained on the protocol. The site had no system to ensure the accuracy of the study drug accountability log. |
| Minor (risk 3) | A situation not classified as critical or major that shows a deficiency or deviation from Division 5 of the Food and Drug Regulations. | The clinical staff were not trained on the latest versions of the site's SOPs. The qualified investigator didn't sign the delegation log. The site kept no copy of the REB's signed and dated attestation or an equivalent REB letter. |

Health Canada may raise or lower an observation's risk level depending on its context, its nature or severity, and whether it recurs.

Once every observation is rated, the inspector gives the site an overall rating:
- A compliant (C) rating means the activities comply with the *Food and Drugs Act* and its regulations. It doesn't mean there are no observations or corrective actions required.
- A non-compliant (NC) rating means the regulated party hasn't shown that its activities comply.

One or more critical observations will likely lead to a non-compliant rating, and the inspector tells the regulated party before the exit meeting. Major observations usually lead to a compliant rating. They can lead to a non-compliant rating if they show that the trial lacks sufficient controls, or if most major observations of an earlier inspection recur. Where every observation is minor, the rating is compliant. Both ratings require corrective and preventive actions for the observations cited. The RI-MUHC's guidance adds that a non-compliant rating may come with conditions.

### Respond to the observations

Once the final inspection exit notice arrives, the qualified investigator, the sponsor or both should prepare a formal written response to every observation still open. The response is due within the time Health Canada sets: usually 30 calendar days, or 60 calendar days for a notice of suspension, unless the inspector specifies otherwise. [QA](mailto:QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca) can help with the response.

With the help of the sponsor or the contract research organization and of QA, the site should address each deficiency by describing the intended corrective actions and the projected timeframe for completing them.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Prepare the site's response">
    For each open observation, the response should include the corrective and preventive action plan. Documents that show evidence of the corrective and preventive actions should be submitted with the response.

    If the sponsor provides no response form, use the RI-MUHC's [Health Canada inspection response form](/sops/cr-017#appendix-2), Appendix 2 of SOP-CR-017. For each observation, the form asks for its root causes, the correction, the corrective actions and the preventive actions, each with a deadline. The form notes that the root cause should be attributed to a process, not blamed on a person.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Submit the response on time">
    Submit the site's response to the sponsor or the contract research organization, or to the regulator, within the requested time. The sponsor or sponsor-investigator then responds to Health Canada in writing on every observation, for the sponsor and the site. Ask to be copied on the sponsor's final response.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Answer any follow-up">
    If Health Canada needs more information, the inspector sends the site a follow-up letter that names the observations still open. Respond to those observations within the time required, with any further evidence. The observations stay open until every request has been met.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Complete the corrective actions">
    Complete every additional corrective and preventive action, as promised, and follow up as required. When Health Canada has reviewed and accepted every response, the inspector sends the qualified investigator and the sponsor a written acknowledgment, and the inspection is closed.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The sponsor or sponsor-investigator should also tell the REB and the regulators of a serious breach of the protocol or good clinical practice, if the law or the regulations require it.

At a later inspection, an inspector may rate a repeated observation at a higher risk level, if the earlier deficiency wasn't adequately addressed or the corrective action promised wasn't implemented. When a qualified investigator's or an institution's participation is ended for non-compliance, the sponsor or sponsor-investigator should inform the regulatory authorities, such as Health Canada, and the REB that approved the study, within 15 days after the termination.

## Conduct during interviews with the inspector

The RI-MUHC's guidance gives these tips for interviews with the inspector. Staff must answer only questions about their own job or their direct involvement in the study.

### What to do

- Provide a quiet, comfortable, temperature-controlled space near a washroom.
- Be honest, polite and cooperative.
- Be direct, factual, brief and precise.
- Be professional, confident and relaxed. You are the expert on your study.
- Make sure you understand each question, and ask for clarification if you need it.
- Answer only within your own expertise, and find an expert if needed.
- Say "I don't know. I will get back to you." when you don't know.
- If you need time to find information or a document, say so and give an estimate. Inspectors don't like to wait without a reason.
- Keep every promise you make to the inspector, without delay.
- Make a list of the documents and questions requested, and check that each document is the right one before releasing it.
- Ask for clarification of a finding if you need it, but don't debate its merits.
- Document everything.

### What not to do

- Take it personally, or become defensive.
- Guess at an answer. If you are unsure, wait until you have the correct information.
- Ask unsolicited or hypothetical questions, or delay a request.
- Express a personal opinion.
- Offer extra information, or point out a fault.
- Extend a discussion by asking more questions.
- Interpret the inspector's question. Take care with questions such as "What do you think?" or "How often does this happen?"

### What never to do

- Reply for someone else.
- Say "never".
- Give the inspector more than they ask for.
- Argue with the inspector. Step out of the room to discuss, agree, and return with the answer.
- Promise anything on the spot.
- Mislead the inspector, or give wrong or incorrect information.

<Warning>
Obstructing an inspector, or knowingly making a false or misleading statement to an inspector, orally or in writing, is a violation of the *Food and Drugs Act* and is subject to penalties. On conviction, the penalties range from a fine of $500 to $5,000,000, a term of imprisonment of three months to two years, or both.
</Warning>

## Check the study against common inspection findings

Health Canada publishes what its inspectors find. Its summary reports of clinical trial inspections and the inspection report cards on the [drug and health product inspections database](https://health-products.canada.ca/dhpid-bdipsa/index-eng.jsp) show the observations that recur. Reviewing earlier summary reports is helpful before an inspection. Use the table below to check the study before a notice arrives.

| Area | What inspectors may flag | Where the rules are | How to prevent it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Task Delegation Log | Team members performing tasks without an entry on the log, entries the qualified investigator didn't sign, and a log not updated after amendments or changes of staff. | [SOP-CR-002](/sops/cr-002#5-6-1) | Check the log regularly against the current team and every protocol version, for example every quarter. The log is to be completed before a team member conducts any study task, and kept up to date throughout the study. |
| Medical oversight by the qualified investigator | No evidence made at the time that the qualified investigator reviewed eligibility, adverse event assessments or deviations, and signatures applied in bulk after the fact. | [SOP-CR-002](/sops/cr-002), [SOP-CR-012](/sops/cr-012) | Every study document that needs the qualified investigator's oversight is initialled and dated by them. | Source documentation | Source not defined, data in the case report form that can't be traced to a source, notes not made at the time, and source changed without explanation. | [SOP-CR-014](/sops/cr-014#5-6-1) | Consider defining the source for every protocol data point, in the monitoring plan or a source document plan, before the study starts.

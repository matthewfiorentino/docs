Three kinds of oversight check a study while it runs and after it ends. The sponsor monitors it, the sponsor or the RI-MUHC's [Quality Assurance](mailto:QAClinicalResearch@muhc.mcgill.ca) (<Tooltip tip="The RI-MUHC's Quality Assurance department, which checks the credentials and training of each investigator who submits a study, audits research and maintains the RI-MUHC's SOPs." headline="Quality Assurance (QA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#qa">QA</Tooltip>) department may audit it, and Health Canada may inspect a study it regulates. ## Compare monitoring, audits and inspections

Each kind of oversight has its own authority and its own consequences:

|  | Monitoring | Audits | Inspections |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who | The sponsor or sponsor-investigator, through a monitor, often called a clinical research associate (CRA) | The sponsor, routinely or for cause, or the RI-MUHC's QA | Health Canada's Clinical Trial Compliance Program, or another regulator such as the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) |
| When | Throughout the study, as the monitoring plan sets out | At any time during the study or at its end | During the study or after it ends. In most cases with notice, but an inspection can be unannounced. |
| Why it happens | The sponsor's duty to monitor the study, at a frequency based on the study's risks | A routine check, a concern about the site, or a principal investigator's request to QA | Routine selection based on risk, or suspected non-compliance |
| What it checks | Compliance with the protocol, the data against the source documents, the investigational product and safety reporting | Compliance with the applicable standards, the quality of the data and participants' safety | Compliance with the *Food and Drugs Act* and its regulations, including good clinical practice, participants' rights and safety, and the data's validity and integrity |
| What follows | A monitoring letter, and a written response to each observation | An audit report, which may call for a corrective and preventive action (<Tooltip tip="A series of actions taken to resolve a failure to comply with the study's requirements, good clinical practice or the applicable regulatory requirements, and to prevent it from happening again." headline="Corrective and Preventive Action (CAPA)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#capa">CAPA</Tooltip>) | Observations rated critical, major or minor, and an overall rating. Every observation in the final report requires a CAPA. |
| Authority | The research agreement with the sponsor | The research agreement, or the institution, for QA | The *Food and Drugs Act*. Obstructing an inspector, or knowingly making a false or misleading statement to one, is a violation subject to penalties. |

## Monitoring

The sponsor or sponsor-investigator monitors the study through a monitor dedicated to it, from the sponsor's organization or from a contract or academic research organization. The monitor is usually the site's main contact with the sponsor, and a key study resource. [Any ambiguity in the protocol](/sops/cr-013#5-1-5), the study's conduct or its logistics should be clarified by the monitor.

The monitor belongs to the sponsor's or sponsor-investigator's team, not the site's, so the monitor is not listed on the site's Task Delegation Log. Quality control of the site's own work, such as checking its source document forms, should be done and initialled by the site, not the monitor, who stays independent of the study's operations.

### What the monitor verifies

During monitoring visits, the monitor verifies that the study follows the protocol the Research Ethics Board (<Tooltip tip="A body, not affiliated with the sponsor, whose main mandate is to approve the start of biomedical research involving human participants and to review it periodically, to protect the participants' rights, safety and well-being." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#reb">REB</Tooltip>) approved and that written consent was obtained before each participant took part. The monitor also checks the team's qualifications and training, the investigational product's management, the source documents, and the reports and notifications the site must make. A core activity is source data verification: the monitor compares the entries in the case report forms (<Tooltip tip="A printed, optical or electronic document designed to record all the protocol-required information to be reported to the sponsor on each trial participant." headline="Case Report Form (CRF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#crf">CRFs</Tooltip>) with the source documents.

The monitor tells the principal investigator and the team in writing about any deviation from the protocol, the RI-MUHC's standard operating procedures (SOPs), good clinical practice or the regulations, and makes sure that they put corrective and preventive actions in place.

[Make sure that every protocol deviation the monitor observes is recorded](/sops/cr-013#5-10-4) on the Protocol Deviation Log. Deviations the monitor observes should be reported to the sponsor and to the REB, [by the deadlines for deviations](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/reportable-events#classify-and-report-protocol-deviations). [Deviation Management and CAPA](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/deviation-management) explains how to classify a deviation and complete a CAPA.

### Risk-based monitoring under ICH E6(R3)

The sponsor or sponsor-investigator [should develop a risk-based monitoring plan](/sops/cr-013#5-1-1). The plan describes the monitoring strategy, which can be on-site monitoring, remote monitoring or a combination, and gives the reason for it. It is usually summarized at the site initiation visit, and the schedule is generally confirmed visit by visit as the study goes on.

Under the good clinical practice guideline of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH), [ICH E6(R3)](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/applications-submissions/guidance-documents/international-council-harmonisation/guidelines.html), the sponsor should decide the extent and nature of monitoring based on the risks identified. The factors to consider include the trial's objective, purpose, design and complexity, its blinding, the number of participants, the investigational product, what is known of its safety profile and the trial's endpoints.

Monitoring can be done at the site, on site or remotely, and centrally. Central monitoring evaluates the accumulated data. It can complement site monitoring and reduce its extent or frequency, or be used on its own, and it can help choose sites or processes for targeted site visits.

Once the feasibility visit and the site initiation visit have taken place, [the RI-MUHC's SOPs](/sops/cr-013#5-1-3) list factors that may increase how often the monitor visits a site:
- The type and complexity of the study, and the need for more training at the site.
- The rate of enrolment.
- The site's experience and its staff turnover.
- The number of monitoring observations, protocol deviations or data management queries.
- The number of serious adverse events.

A site may also receive targeted queries, or a visit, prompted by the sponsor's central review of the data. These are the expected output of risk-based monitoring, not a penalty.

### Prepare for a monitoring visit

The monitor usually confirms each visit in writing first. File the confirmation in the monitoring section of the investigator site file, and alert the other departments the monitor will visit, such as the pharmacy.

Before each visit:
- [Make sure the principal investigator is available](/sops/cr-013#5-8-1) at the time of the visit, and schedule the visit with every service involved.
- Keep the documentation up to date: the case report forms, the regulatory binder, the participant binders, the essential documents and the records kept in other departments.
- Make sure the principal investigator has initialled and dated the site initiation visit report and the earlier monitoring letters, to show medical oversight.
- Resolve the issues left from the last visit, and make the case report form corrections still pending, with the principal investigator's or a delegate's initials and date.
- Update the Protocol Deviation Log, make it available to the monitor, and make sure that every reportable deviation has been reported to the REB.
- Have the records available for review with their source documents.
- Prepare a monitoring log for the coordinator and the monitor to sign on the day of the visit.

The site should do its own quality control before, and independently of, the sponsor's monitoring.

During the visit, follow the institution's rules for third parties on site. Give the monitor a confidential work space and access only to the premises they need, and accompany the monitor wherever confidential material is stored. Make sure that no study document with participant identifiers leaves the institution. A copy the monitor needs for their files should be de-identified.

Before the monitor leaves, make sure the monitor and the coordinator sign the monitoring log. File the log in the investigator site file with the monitoring letters.

A monitor who reviews participants' medical records needs documented training and access to OACIS, the MUHC's electronic medical record system. Arrange that access with Medical Records well before the first visit, by following [the steps for external monitors](/apps/oacis#requesting-access-for-external-monitors).

For a remote visit, agree with the sponsor on a secure way to share documents. Data shared from the site should be redacted, with each participant's name replaced by their study code, and uploaded to a secure, password-protected, validated system.

### Respond to the monitoring letter

After each visit, the monitor sends the site a monitoring letter that summarizes the findings and the follow-up actions needed. Make sure the principal investigator [initials and dates the monitoring letter](/sops/cr-013#5-11-3), to document medical oversight.

If the letter doesn't arrive promptly after the visit, follow up with the monitor. Review it for significant findings, deviations and actions to take, clarify any action items with the monitor, and put remedial action in place at the site. Respond to each monitoring observation in writing, within the timelines of the research agreement. File the initialled and dated letter, with any CAPA response, in the investigator site file (<Tooltip tip="The qualified or principal investigator's collection of study documentation that demonstrates compliance with regulations and guidelines, so that the conduct and integrity of the study and the safety of participants at the site can be verified." headline="Investigator Site File (ISF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#isf">ISF</Tooltip>).

## Audits

An audit is an independent review of the site's compliance, deeper than the sponsor's routine monitoring.

### When an audit is announced or an auditor arrives without notice

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Studies regulated by Health Canada</span></div>

Routine audits usually come with advance written notice, but an auditor may arrive with little or no notice, especially for a for-cause audit. When an auditor gives notice of an audit, or arrives with little or no notice, [immediately inform](/sops/cr-017#5-1-4) the sponsor or sponsor-investigator and the research team, as required. Keep a record of every communication about the audit request with the study's essential documents.

### Sponsor audits

The sponsor or sponsor-investigator may audit the site. A routine audit checks that the study complies with the applicable standards, that its data are accurate and of good quality, and that participants' safety is maintained. It can take place at any time during or at the end of the study. An audit early in the study can correct errors before the study is completed.

A for-cause audit takes place when there is reason to believe that the site's data, procedures or compliance with good clinical practice or the regulations have problems. Such problems can show as weak compliance with the protocol, an overly low or high rate of adverse reactions or adverse events compared to other sites, or a high rate of recruitment compared to other sites. For-cause audits tend to be more intensive than routine audits.

The sponsor may also audit the site to prepare it for a Health Canada inspection.

## Health Canada inspections

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Studies regulated by Health Canada</span></div>

Health Canada's [Clinical Trial Compliance Program](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/compliance-enforcement/good-clinical-practices/guidance-documents/risk-classification-observations-inspections-clinical-trials-guide-0043.html) inspects the parties that conduct clinical trials of drugs. Health Canada has also begun inspecting medical device studies. A device trial is inspected against the Medical Devices Regulations and the ISO 14155 standard of the International Organization for Standardization, and a drug trial against Division 5 of the Food and Drug Regulations and the ICH's good clinical practice guideline.

[Hosting a Health Canada Inspection](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/hc-inspection-guide) covers how Health Canada selects a site and gives notice, and the steps of an inspection, from the notice to the response to the inspector's report. In a drug trial, the inspector [rates each observation in the inspection report critical, major or minor](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/hc-inspection-guide#inspection-exit-notice), using Health Canada's GUI-0043 as a guide. Health Canada's risk levels are not the RI-MUHC's [deviation tiers](/kb/conduct/safety-reporting/reportable-events#classify-a-deviation-as-minor-major-or-critical), although their wording is close.

### Inspections by the FDA and other regulators

The US Food and Drug Administration may inspect a Canadian site in a study under a US-held Investigational New Drug (IND) application or Investigational Device Exemption (IDE). The site should receive the FDA's form 482, "Notice of Inspection", and the two parties agree on a date that leaves the team time to prepare. If fraud is suspected, FDA inspectors can arrive without notice. A site can also be inspected by other regulators, or by Health Canada on behalf of or in partnership with another country's regulator, such as the European Medicines Agency.

## Being inspection-ready

An inspection tests whether the study has been conducted properly from its start. Notice can be short, and an inspection can be unannounced, so there is no time to catch up on documentation that was never done.

The practices behind good monitoring are what make an inspection manageable: a complete investigator site file, documentation made at the time, corrections signed and dated, and an intact audit trail. Records must also reach an inspector quickly, within a timeframe the RI-MUHC's SOPs set. For that reason, a record of the archived study documents and their location must be readily available at all times. [Data Integrity](/kb/conduct/data-documentation/data-integrity) gives the timeframe and the rules for records.

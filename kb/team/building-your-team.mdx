A study team is put together before the study is submitted, and the principal investigator, who leads the study at the MUHC, stays responsible for everything the team does. Who may join the team, and which tasks each person may be given, depends on their role, their credentials and their training. The RI-MUHC's standard operating procedures (SOPs) set most of these rules, and some come from Health Canada and Quebec law.

## Delegate every task on the Task Delegation Log

The principal investigator keeps ultimate responsibility for the conduct of the study at the site, however the tasks are shared out. [The principal investigator keeps oversight of every person or party given a task](/sops/cr-002#5-3-5), and remains responsible for supervising them.

Every task is delegated in writing on the <Tooltip tip="The investigator's list of the appropriately qualified people to whom the investigator has delegated significant study-related tasks." headline="Task Delegation Log (TDL)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#tdl">Task Delegation Log</Tooltip>. [The log is completed for each team member before they conduct any study task](/sops/cr-002#5-6-1), and it should be kept up to date throughout the study. Each person's training for a task must be documented before that person and the principal investigator sign the log.

The principal investigator's signature on the log is their assurance that the person given the task is qualified and trained to do it. Each team member signs and dates the log to accept the tasks delegated to them.

<Warning>
Do not perform a study task until your training for it is documented and the task has been delegated on the Task Delegation Log.
</Warning>

Changes to the team once the study is running, such as new members, people who leave, leaves of absence and a change of principal investigator, are covered in [Team Management During a Study](/kb/conduct/study-operations/team-management).

## Delegate only tasks the person is licensed to do

<div className="hub-scope"><span className="hub-scope-label">Applies to</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Drug studies (Level I)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Medical device and surgery studies (Level II)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Natural health product studies (Level III)</span><span className="hub-scope-tag">Prospective studies not regulated by Health Canada (Level IV)</span></div>

Quebec law reserves some activities to the members of professional orders. A study may need several of them, such as administering a prescribed medication or taking a specimen.

[Each team member must be appropriately qualified](/sops/cr-002#5-1-1) for their study tasks by education, general training, study-specific training and experience. A task that is a reserved activity goes only to a person the law allows to perform it.

The [Medical Act](https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/M-9), the [Nurses Act](https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/I-8) and the [Professional Code](https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/C-26) each reserve activities to the members of a profession. For each profession, the table gives the reserved activities a study may need, in the laws' own terms. Each law's full list is longer.

| Profession | Reserved activities a study may need | Law |
|---|---|---|
| Physicians | Diagnosing illnesses. Prescribing diagnostic examinations. Using diagnostic techniques that are invasive or entail risks of injury. Determining medical treatment. Prescribing medications and other substances. Prescribing treatment. Using techniques or applying treatments that are invasive or entail risks of injury. Providing clinical monitoring of the condition of patients whose state of health is problematic. | The Medical Act |
| Nurses | Assessing the physical and mental condition of a symptomatic person. Providing clinical monitoring of the condition of persons whose state of health is problematic. Performing invasive examinations and diagnostic tests, according to a prescription. Providing and adjusting medical treatment, according to a prescription. Applying invasive techniques. Administering and adjusting prescribed medications or other prescribed substances. | The Nurses Act |
| Licensed practical nurses, members of the Ordre professionnel des infirmières et infirmiers auxiliaires du Québec | Taking specimens, according to a prescription. Introducing an instrument into a peripheral vein to take a specimen, according to a prescription. Administering prescribed medications or other prescribed substances by routes other than the intravenous route. | The Professional Code |
| Medical technologists, members of the Ordre professionnel des technologistes médicaux du Québec | Taking specimens. Performing phlebotomies, according to a prescription. Introducing an instrument into a peripheral vein, according to a prescription. Once the order has issued them a training certificate, administering prescribed medications or other prescribed substances, including intravenously from a peripheral site. | The Professional Code |
| Respiratory therapists, members of the Ordre professionnel des inhalothérapeutes du Québec | Taking specimens, according to a prescription. Introducing an instrument into a peripheral vein, according to a prescription. Administering and adjusting prescribed medications or other prescribed substances. Testing cardiopulmonary function, according to a prescription. Assessing the cardiopulmonary condition of a symptomatic person. | The Professional Code |

The Professional Code reserves each of its activities to the members of these orders within the scope of the activities their own profession may engage in.

No one may engage in an activity the Professional Code reserves without a valid, appropriate permit and an entry on the roll of the order empowered to issue the permit, unless the law allows it. The Code also lets a person engage in these activities in accordance with a regulation made under paragraph h of its section 94.

No one but a physician may engage in the activities the Medical Act reserves, subject to the rights and privileges the law expressly grants other professionals. The Act also lets these people engage in them:

- A person acting under a regulation made under paragraph h of section 94 of the Professional Code.
- People who, because of their duties or training, help the sick free of charge in special circumstances.
- A person in a class of persons that a regulation of the board of directors of the Ordre des médecins du Québec allows to engage in some of these activities, under the regulation's conditions.
- Students, as part of a training program for the persons such a regulation covers, under the regulation's conditions.

No one but a nurse may engage in the activities the Nurses Act reserves, subject to the rights and privileges the law expressly grants other professionals. The Act also lets these people engage in them:

- A person acting under a regulation made under paragraph h of section 94 of the Professional Code.
- A person legally authorized to practise nursing outside Quebec whose contract requires them to accompany and care for a patient staying temporarily in Quebec, during that contract, provided they do not present themselves as holding a permit.

The [Regulation respecting the professional activities which may be performed by persons other than nurses](https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/rc/I-8,%20r.%202) is made under paragraph h of section 94 of the Professional Code. The regulation lets nursing students, nursing externs, persons eligible by equivalence and candidates for the profession of nursing carry out some of the activities nurses may perform, each under the regulation's conditions, such as the supervision of a nurse who is present in the care unit.

One item of the regulation's list for nursing externs lets them administer a prescribed medication or substance by the routes it names, other than a serum, a medication related to a research protocol, a substance related to allergy tests or a subcutaneous anesthetic substance.

A reserved activity can belong to more than one profession. The Medical Act and the Nurses Act both reserve invasive procedures and the clinical monitoring of persons whose state of health is problematic, each in its own terms. No item of the Medical Act's or the Nurses Act's list names taking vital signs.

Specialized nurse practitioners may also engage in some activities the Medical Act reserves to physicians, such as diagnosing illnesses and prescribing medications, if a regulation under the Nurses Act authorizes them, according to their class of specialization and on the regulation's terms and conditions.

For a blood sample, the Professional Code lets licensed practical nurses introduce an instrument into a peripheral vein to take a specimen, medical technologists perform phlebotomies, and respiratory therapists introduce an instrument into a peripheral vein, each according to a prescription.

No team member may perform a reserved activity without a law or regulation that authorizes them, whatever the Task Delegation Log or the protocol says. A study role, such as coordinator, research assistant or trainee, does not by itself authorize anyone to perform one.

Belonging to a professional order does not let a volunteer [contact, recruit or perform procedures on participants](#add-people-who-are-not-employees). The RI-MUHC's SOPs make no exception for members of a professional order. [Trainee](/roles/trainee#what-you-may-not-do) and [Volunteer and Observer](/roles/volunteer-observer#know-what-volunteers-and-observers-may-do) say what each of them may and may not do.

The RI-MUHC's SOPs have [a pharmacist dispense every medication](/sops/cr-010#1-1-1) to be administered to a patient, in care or in a study. In a drug or natural health product study, the MUHC Research Pharmacy may approve [an RI-MUHC research nurse or an MUHC nurse to dispense the investigational product](/kb/conduct/study-operations/investigational-products#ask-for-a-nurse-to-dispense-the-product) directly to participants.

A nurse or another professional who is not a physician or dentist may lead a study with [Researcher Status](/training/compliance-requirements#know-which-credential-you-need), which does not allow research that requires medical oversight.

## Fill the core roles

Most study teams have the roles below. The tasks each role may be given are the task categories of [the Task Delegation Log](/sops/cr-002#appendix-1).

| Role | Who can fill it | What the role does |
|---|---|---|
| Principal investigator | A physician or dentist with Human Research Privileges, or, for a study that does not require medical oversight, a researcher with Researcher Status. There is no more than one principal investigator at each study site. | Leads the study at the site and is responsible for its overall conduct. Medical care and medical decisions in the study are under the principal investigator's supervision. In a drug or natural health product trial, the principal investigator is the qualified investigator and signs the [Qualified Investigator Undertaking](/kb/conduct/study-operations/team-management). |
| Sub-investigator | A team member delegated some of the principal investigator's tasks. The sub-investigator named as the principal investigator's backup must hold the same Privileges or Researcher Status, qualifications and training as the principal investigator. | Carries out the tasks delegated on the log, including medical decisions delegated to a qualified and trained sub-investigator. The backup sub-investigator temporarily replaces the principal investigator if needed. Fellows and longitudinal residents may be sub-investigators, with [restrictions](#fellows-and-residents-on-a-research-team). |
| <Tooltip tip="A nurse, health professional or other qualified member of the research team who handles most of the administrative responsibilities and day-to-day activities of a clinical trial." headline="Clinical Research Coordinator (CRC)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/foundations/glossary#crc">Clinical research coordinator</Tooltip> or research nurse | An employee of the RI-MUHC, of the MUHC or of a principal investigator. Holds the study's level of institutional training. | Coordinates the study day to day with the principal investigator: the Research Ethics Board (REB) submission, source document forms, information for participants, appointments and monitoring visits, study procedures the principal investigator authorizes, and case report forms. Liaises with hospital departments and the REB. Supports the consent process and keeps the study's regulatory files. Does not perform [acts reserved to a licensed professional](#delegate-only-tasks-the-person-is-licensed-to-do), unless a law or regulation authorizes them. |
| Research assistant | An employee of the RI-MUHC, of the MUHC or of a principal investigator, or a research trainee. Holds the study's level of institutional training. | Administrative and data tasks, as delegated. What a research assistant may do depends on their qualifications. A research assistant may contact, recruit or perform procedures on participants only if [allowed to work with participants](#add-people-who-are-not-employees). |
| A department or a team on rotation, such as the pharmacy, a laboratory, nursing or imaging | At the principal investigator's request, the department's manager or supervisor must be added to the log. Each member of their staff need not be listed, provided that evidence of their qualification is available. | The manager signs [the Task Delegation to a Manager or Supervisor of a Team on Rotation form](/sops/cr-002#5-7-1), keeps the staff's CVs and licences in the department's files, and passes the training on to the staff before they do the task. The MUHC Pharmacy keeps its own Pharmacy Task Delegation Log. |

In a study with an investigational product, only the principal investigator and sub-investigators who are physicians can prescribe medication.

## Who needs Privileges or Researcher Status

Only the people who lead a study, or stand in for its leader, need a credential as well as training. [The RI-MUHC's SOPs](/sops/cr-500#2-0-scope) require these people to hold Human Research Privileges or Researcher Status before the study is submitted:

- The principal investigator.
- A sub-investigator delegated the principal investigator's backup tasks.
- A sponsor-investigator.

Other team members, such as clinical research coordinators, research assistants and trainees, do not need Privileges or Researcher Status. These team members need the institutional training for the study's level. [Training and Compliance Requirements](/training/compliance-requirements#human-research-privileges-and-researcher-status) says which credential each investigator needs, including the rule for pharmacists, and how to apply.

## Fellows and residents on a research team

Fellows and residents may work on a study, but they may not conduct research independently. None of them can be a sponsor-investigator or a principal investigator, or a sub-investigator delegated the principal investigator's responsibilities, and none can apply for Privileges or Researcher Status. [A medical assessment by a fellow or a resident must be co-signed](/sops/cr-002#5-3-3) by the principal investigator or a qualified sub-investigator with Privileges or Researcher Status.

Residents may be listed on research protocols, approach participants and access identifiable patient health records, such as those in OACIS.

Residents on rotation cannot be named sub-investigators.

Fellows and longitudinal residents can be named sub-investigators, but they cannot replace the principal investigator. The Task Delegation Log must show exactly which duties they may carry out.

[On the Task Delegation Log](/sops/cr-002#5-6-2), fellows and residents should generally not be named sub-investigators. The log can name them, and medical students, as a student, a fellow, a resident or a research assistant. For a grant application, fellows and residents can be listed as sub-investigators on the protocol or the grant proposal.

Some departments make an exception and give fellows and longitudinal residents the title "Sub-I (fellow)" or "Sub-I (longitudinal resident)". In that case:

- These fellows and residents must not be delegated the principal investigator's tasks without supervision, and they cannot be the principal investigator's backup.
- The principal investigator must ensure that any of the principal investigator's tasks these fellows and residents carry out is under the principal investigator's direct, documented supervision, with every medical assessment co-signed by the principal investigator or a sub-investigator with Privileges. The log should clearly show this supervision.
- The department should write its own SOP that says which tasks it delegates to fellows or longitudinal residents and how it supervises them.

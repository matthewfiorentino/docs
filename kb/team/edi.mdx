Funders ask how a study and its team account for equity, diversity and inclusion (EDI), and the answers are written into the study design, the eligibility criteria and the grant application.

The Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) [expects all applicants to integrate sex and gender](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/50833.html) into their research design and practices when appropriate. In 2023–24, about 85 per cent of the research CIHR funded through its Training and Career Support Program integrated sex and gender considerations. ## Review eligibility criteria and recruitment for inclusion

3CTN developed an [EDI Framework and Toolkit](https://3ctn.ca/equity-diversity-and-inclusion-in-clinical-trials/) for sites, research staff, sponsors and funders. Its recommendations aim to improve fair access to clinical trials for under-represented populations, and its toolkit points to resources, templates and guidelines for each recommendation. The framework was written for cancer trials, and it can be applied in other therapeutic areas.{/* TO CONFIRM (the EDI team): "applicable across therapeutic areas" is kept from the page as it stood. ### Check each eligibility criterion for unintended exclusion

The 3CTN framework recommends reviewing inclusion and exclusion criteria to assess potential biases against under-represented populations, and making the adjustments needed to promote inclusivity. For the criteria themselves, it recommends comprehensive age ranges, no sex and gender exclusions, and language on pregnancy and contraception that is inclusive and does not inadvertently exclude 2SLGBTQIA+ participants.

Consider also checking each criterion against these flags before the protocol is final, to find requirements that may exclude under-represented populations without a scientific reason:{/* TO CONFIRM (the EDI team): the flags and the "Consider instead" column are kept from the page as it stood, which gave them as "Key elements of the 3CTN framework". The framework (read in full, Nov. 16, 2026) does not hold them: only the age row echoes its "Incorporate comprehensive age ranges"; its item on pregnancy and contraception is about 2SLGBTQIA+ inclusion; the BMI, language, caregiver, visits and safety-mechanism rows are not in it (the b163 fact check, ED30, ED31). Kept as the Hub's advice: does the EDI team stand behind the rows? */}

| Flag | Who it can exclude | Consider instead |
|---|---|---|
| An age cap with no scientific reason | Older adults | Removing the cap, or justifying it by the disease's biology rather than convenience. |
| Excluding pregnant or lactating people with no known safety mechanism | Pregnant and lactating people | Excluding them only where a pharmacological risk has been identified, and citing the mechanism. |
| A contraception requirement that applies only to women | Women of childbearing potential | Applying it to every participant who could cause a pregnancy, or justifying the difference. |
| A BMI or weight cut-off with no mechanism linking weight to the study's outcomes | People with larger bodies | Tying the exclusion to a specific physiological reason, not to habit. |
| An English or French language requirement where translation is feasible | People who speak neither English nor French | Assessing whether interpretation or translated materials could include them. |
| Requiring a caregiver to come with the participant when this is not clinically needed | Participants without strong social support | Assessing whether the caregiver is clinically needed or is a habit that could be removed. |
| Frequent visits in person, with no assessment of telemedicine | Participants who live in rural areas, have low incomes or work shifts | Recording whether remote or hybrid visits were considered and, if they were rejected, why. |

## Plan sex and gender analysis from the design stage

Sex and gender come up twice: in the grant application, where CIHR expects them to be integrated, and in publication. The Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines, from the European Association of Science Editors, set out [how to report sex and gender](https://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/sager-guidelines/) in the study design, the data analyses, the results and the interpretation of findings. Many journals ask authors to follow them.{/* TO CONFIRM (the EDI team): the page as it stood said "Most major clinical research journals now require or strongly recommend SAGER-compliant reporting as a condition of publication". No source found for "most"; the SAGER authors' 2016 survey found that about 7 per cent of respondents' journals had sex and gender policies (PMC5793986; saved, run/web/sager-pmc5793986.txt). EQUATOR's page says "Many journals require completed checklists at submission" of reporting guidelines in general. Which journals, and how many? */}

Two choices made while the study is designed decide what can be reported later.

The first is how sex and gender are recorded. Sex refers to biological attributes, and gender to socially constructed roles, behaviours, expressions and identities. The SAGER guidelines say authors should use the two terms carefully so that they are not confused.

The 3CTN framework recommends that sex and gender not be conflated in study protocols and documents. For trial forms, it recommends questions and answer options that capture sex assigned at birth, sexual orientation, current gender identity, the inclusion of Two-Spirit and intersex characteristics. A study that records only one of sex and gender, or merges them, cannot analyze them separately afterwards.{/* TO CONFIRM (the EDI team): the last sentence is the reason the page as it stood gave ("Collecting only one, or conflating them, forecloses SAGER-compliant analysis after the fact"); no source states it. "Collect sex and gender as separate variables", a "must" on the page as it stood and "Consider …" in the draft, is now the 3CTN framework's recommendation in its own terms (pp. 8 and 12; the b163 fact check, ED40); SAGER's verb is "should" (ED39). */}

The second is the analysis. Where subjects can be differentiated by sex, the SAGER guidelines say the research should be designed and conducted in a way that can reveal sex-related differences in the results, even if none were expected. In the results:

- Where appropriate, data should be presented disaggregated by sex and gender.
- Sex- and gender-based analyses should be reported whether their outcome is positive or negative.
- In clinical trials, withdrawals and dropouts should also be reported by sex.
- If a sex and gender analysis was not conducted, the reason should be given.

The SAGER guidelines also say that authors should not conduct a gender-based analysis after the fact if the study design is not sufficient to support meaningful conclusions. If a sex and gender analysis was not conducted, the SAGER guidelines say authors should also discuss what its absence means for the interpretation of the results.{/* Changed (the b163 fact check, ED45; for MF): the page as it stood said "If your sample size does not support disaggregation, document this as a limitation", which the draft kept as the Hub's advice ("consider reporting that as a limitation"). SAGER's own "should" covers every case in which no analysis was conducted, a sample too small for one included: "Authors should further discuss the implications of the lack of such analysis on the interpretation of the results." */}

CIHR's [online training modules on sex and gender](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/50836.html) cover integrating sex and gender in research with human participants.

Funders ask how a study and its team account for equity, diversity and inclusion (EDI), and the answers are written into the study design, the eligibility criteria and the grant application.

The Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) [expects all applicants to integrate sex and gender](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/50833.html) into their research design and practices when appropriate. In 2023–24, about 85 per cent of the research CIHR funded through its Training and Career Support Program integrated sex and gender considerations. The RI-MUHC's [EDI team](mailto:edi.ri@muhc.mcgill.ca), part of People and Culture, helps researchers build these considerations in while the study is designed.

## Get help from the EDI team

The RI-MUHC's [EDI team](mailto:edi.ri@muhc.mcgill.ca) supports research teams at The Institute in three ways.

- Consultation on sex- and gender-based analysis plus (SGBA+), in French analyse comparative entre les sexes et les genres plus (ACS+), for grant proposals. The team helps researchers bring sex, gender and diversity into the study design, the eligibility criteria and the outcome measures.
- Support with recruitment and retention, including recruitment strategies that reach groups under-represented in research and retention strategies for a diverse group of participants. The team also supports inclusion within the research team.
- Tools and training: a self-assessment tool for EDI strategies aligned with the federal research agencies, a resource tool to find what fits your case, the Inclusive Writing Guide, toolkits on disability inclusion and neuroinclusion, online training on unconscious bias and inclusive leadership, and workshops with experts.

The team also helps develop accessible consent materials.

Consider writing to the EDI team while you design the study, before the protocol is final and before the grant application is submitted. The team's page on the [RI-MUHC Portal](https://portal.rimuhc.ca/pls/apex/f?p=106:399) has its tools.

## Find programs for equity-seeking groups

The [EDI team](mailto:edi.ri@muhc.mcgill.ca) runs scholarships, mentorship, institutional dialogues and affinity groups for researchers and team members from equity-seeking groups, including:

- Women.
- Racialized people.
- Indigenous people.
- 2SLGBTQIA+ people.
- People with disabilities.
- Ethnic minorities.

The team can connect researchers with the program, mentorship or resource that fits their needs.

## Funder EDI requirements

What a funder asks about EDI changes with each program and competition year. Most major funders now require EDI considerations in research teams and in study design. Check the current requirements in the funder's own call, and with the [Pre-Awards Office](mailto:pre-awards.rimuhc@muhc.mcgill.ca), before you submit.

| Funder | What it asks about EDI |
|---|---|
| CIHR | CIHR expects all applicants to integrate sex and gender into their research design and practices when appropriate. In a Project Grant, [the nominated principal applicant must complete a sex- and gender-based analysis training module](/kb/funding/funding-applications#choose-a-funder). |
| Fonds de recherche du Québec (FRQ) | The FRQ's EDI strategy sets a target of 80 per cent of FRQ-funded research groups reporting concrete actions to promote EDI by 2026. The target is not an application requirement. FRQ health competitions assess SGBA+ considerations with scientific merit, and most competitions require an EDI plan. The FRQ's research scholar awards (Chercheur-boursier Junior 1, Junior 2 and Senior) don't list an EDI plan among their required documents, so check each program's current rules and evaluation grid. |
| Canada Foundation for Innovation (CFI) | EDI considerations in the team's composition and the research environment. |
| The MUHC Foundation and private foundations | Review the Research Ethics Board (REB) billing and the EDI requirements with the [Research Facilitator](mailto:research.facilitator@muhc.mcgill.ca). |

Applicants to the FRQ are expected to show concrete EDI commitments, and reviewers assess whether the plan is part of the research design or only appended as a compliance item.

Some CIHR funding opportunities require the research team to include an EDI champion. In CIHR's Team Grant: Embracing Diversity to Achieve Precision and Health Equity, for example, [the team must include an EDI champion](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/53757.html) with expertise in sound EDI practices and principles, as nominated principal applicant, principal applicant or co-applicant.

Consider describing the EDI champion's planned activities in the application, and giving the champion access to the toolkit of the Canadian Cancer Clinical Trials Network (3CTN) and the consultations of the [EDI team](mailto:edi.ri@muhc.mcgill.ca).

## Review eligibility criteria and recruitment for inclusion

3CTN developed an [EDI Framework and Toolkit](https://3ctn.ca/equity-diversity-and-inclusion-in-clinical-trials/) for sites, research staff, sponsors and funders. Its recommendations aim to improve fair access to clinical trials for under-represented populations, and its toolkit points to resources, templates and guidelines for each recommendation. The framework was written for cancer trials, and it can be applied in other therapeutic areas. The [EDI team](mailto:edi.ri@muhc.mcgill.ca) can help you apply it to your protocol while you design the study.

### Check each eligibility criterion for unintended exclusion

The 3CTN framework recommends reviewing inclusion and exclusion criteria to assess potential biases against under-represented populations, and making the adjustments needed to promote inclusivity. For the criteria themselves, it recommends comprehensive age ranges, no sex and gender exclusions, and language on pregnancy and contraception that is inclusive and does not inadvertently exclude 2SLGBTQIA+ participants.

Consider also checking each criterion against these flags before the protocol is final, to find requirements that may exclude under-represented populations without a scientific reason:

| Flag | Who it can exclude | Consider instead |
|---|---|---|
| An age cap with no scientific reason | Older adults | Removing the cap, or justifying it by the disease's biology rather than convenience. |
| Excluding pregnant or lactating people with no known safety mechanism | Pregnant and lactating people | Excluding them only where a pharmacological risk has been identified, and citing the mechanism. |
| A contraception requirement that applies only to women | Women of childbearing potential | Applying it to every participant who could cause a pregnancy, or justifying the difference. |
| A BMI or weight cut-off with no mechanism linking weight to the study's outcomes | People with larger bodies | Tying the exclusion to a specific physiological reason, not to habit. |
| An English or French language requirement where translation is feasible | People who speak neither English nor French | Assessing whether interpretation or translated materials could include them. |
| Requiring a caregiver to come with the participant when this is not clinically needed | Participants without strong social support | Assessing whether the caregiver is clinically needed or is a habit that could be removed. |
| Frequent visits in person, with no assessment of telemedicine | Participants who live in rural areas, have low incomes or work shifts | Recording whether remote or hybrid visits were considered and, if they were rejected, why. |

### Adapt recruitment for under-represented groups

Consider these adaptations when you plan recruitment for groups that are often under-represented in clinical trials:

| Group | Recruitment considerations |
|---|---|
| Racialized communities | Recruitment through community partnerships. Materials in community languages. Relationships with community champions. Addressing historical mistrust of medical research. |
| Participants in rural and remote areas | Telemedicine. Decentralized visits. Partnerships with rural health centres. A realistic assessment of the travel burden in the eligibility criteria. |
| People with low socioeconomic status | Addressing practical barriers such as transportation, parking and childcare. Saying clearly that taking part doesn't affect access to standard care. Flexible visit times. |
| Older adults | Consent materials suited to their age, in larger type. Including caregivers where appropriate. Help with digital tools. |
| Gender-diverse participants | Data collection that affirms gender, with sex and gender recorded separately. Inclusive language in consent materials and questionnaires. Training for the study team on communication that affirms gender. |
| Immigrants and newcomers | Materials in the languages they use. Certified medical interpreters for consent. Help finding their way in a new culture. |

### Consider the composition of the team

The 3CTN framework recommends increasing the diversity of clinical research teams. Its recommendations include seeking candidates from diverse backgrounds, using bias prevention processes during selection, and incorporating patient partners and representatives as integral members of the clinical research team.

Research teams that include people with lived experience of the condition studied, or experience of the communities the study aims to enrol, have stronger recruitment, higher retention and more relevant outcomes.

## Plan sex and gender analysis from the design stage

Sex and gender come up twice: in the grant application, where CIHR expects them to be integrated, and in publication. The Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines, from the European Association of Science Editors, set out [how to report sex and gender](https://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/sager-guidelines/) in the study design, the data analyses, the results and the interpretation of findings. Many journals ask authors to follow them.

Two choices made while the study is designed decide what can be reported later.

The first is how sex and gender are recorded. Sex refers to biological attributes, and gender to socially constructed roles, behaviours, expressions and identities. The SAGER guidelines say authors should use the two terms carefully so that they are not confused.

The 3CTN framework recommends that sex and gender not be conflated in study protocols and documents. For trial forms, it recommends questions and answer options that capture sex assigned at birth, sexual orientation, current gender identity, the inclusion of Two-Spirit and intersex characteristics. A study that records only one of sex and gender, or merges them, cannot analyze them separately afterwards.

The second is the analysis. Where subjects can be differentiated by sex, the SAGER guidelines say the research should be designed and conducted in a way that can reveal sex-related differences in the results, even if none were expected. In the results:

- Where appropriate, data should be presented disaggregated by sex and gender.
- Sex- and gender-based analyses should be reported whether their outcome is positive or negative.
- In clinical trials, withdrawals and dropouts should also be reported by sex.
- If a sex and gender analysis was not conducted, the reason should be given.

The SAGER guidelines also say that authors should not conduct a gender-based analysis after the fact if the study design is not sufficient to support meaningful conclusions.

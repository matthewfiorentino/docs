Research involving vulnerable populations is not simply a matter of adding safeguards to an existing protocol. It requires a fundamentally different approach to consent, recruitment, risk assessment, and oversight. In Quebec, the *Civil Code of Québec* (CCQ) imposes specific legal obligations that go beyond what TCPS2 and <Tooltip tip="International standard for design, conduct, monitoring, recording, and reporting of clinical studies." headline="Good Clinical Practice (GCP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#gcp">GCP</Tooltip> require — and the <Tooltip tip="The ethics committee that reviews and approves research involving human participants. Also called CER at French-language institutions." headline="Research Ethics Board (REB)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#reb">REB</Tooltip> expects your protocol to reflect those obligations clearly. This page covers what you need to build into your protocol, your consent process, and your REB submission when your study involves persons or contexts that the law and TCPS2 recognize as requiring additional protections.

## Vulnerable persons vs. vulnerable contexts

TCPS2 Chapter 4 draws a critical distinction that shapes how your protocol should address vulnerability:

- **Vulnerable persons** have characteristics that may limit their ability to provide free and informed consent — cognitive impairment, age (minors, elderly), or an acute mental health condition.
- **Vulnerable contexts** arise from the situation surrounding the research — a dependent relationship with the researcher, institutionalization, economic disadvantage, or an emergency where prior consent is impossible.

A participant may not be inherently vulnerable but may be placed in a vulnerable context by the research situation itself. A graduate student asked to participate in their supervisor's study is not cognitively impaired, but the power imbalance creates a context where voluntariness is compromised. Your protocol must address both dimensions.

<Note>
The goal is neither to exclude vulnerable populations from research nor to treat vulnerability as a blanket barrier. TCPS2 emphasizes fairness: people should not bear disproportionate research burdens, but they also should not be denied the potential benefits of research participation without justification.
</Note>

## Legal framework in Quebec

Three bodies of law converge on research with vulnerable populations in Quebec:

| Source | Key provisions |
|---|---|
| **CCQ Art. 21** | Governs research involving minors and adults incapable of consenting. Consent must come from a legally appointed representative (LAR). Only MSSS-designated REBs may review Art. 21 research — the MUHC REB holds this designation. If a person who is incapable of consenting objects or shows resistance, they cannot be enrolled regardless of LAR consent. |
| **CCQ Art. 25** | Prohibits financial compensation beyond reimbursement for actual loss. In populations where economic disadvantage is a factor, this provision sets the ceiling: compensation must not constitute undue inducement. This is stricter than TCPS2 or ICH GCP. |
| **TCPS2 Chapter 4** | Requires that protocols involving vulnerable populations include additional protections proportionate to the nature and degree of vulnerability, while maintaining fair inclusion. |
| **Loi 25 (Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector)** | Imposes heightened privacy obligations for sensitive data. Data collected from vulnerable populations — particularly health, cognitive, or psychiatric data — may attract additional safeguards under this framework. See [Privacy and Confidentiality](/kb/privacy). |

<Warning>
CCQ Art. 21 is absolute on one point: if a person who lacks legal capacity to consent nonetheless objects to or resists participation, enrollment is prohibited — even if an LAR has given consent. This applies to adults with impaired capacity and to minors. There is no override.
</Warning>

## Categories requiring specific consideration

The following groupings reflect the most common scenarios at the RI-MUHC. Each requires distinct protocol-level provisions.

**Adults with impaired decision-making capacity** — including dementia, acquired brain injury, and acute psychiatric conditions. The protocol must specify how capacity will be assessed, who will provide LAR consent, and how fluctuating capacity will be managed over time.

**Persons in dependent relationships** — employees of the investigator, students, prisoners, and long-term care residents. The concern is not capacity but voluntariness. The protocol must describe specific measures to ensure that participation is genuinely free from coercion or undue influence — including who will recruit, who will obtain consent, and how the participant will be assured that refusal carries no consequences for their care, employment, or academic standing.

**Pregnant and lactating participants** — not automatically excluded. Exclusion must be scientifically justified and approved by the REB. Where an <Tooltip tip="A drug, medical device, or natural health product being tested or used as a reference in a clinical trial." headline="Investigational Product (IP)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#ip">investigational product</Tooltip> with unknown effects in pregnancy is involved, the protocol must address Exposure in Utero reporting.

**Economically disadvantaged participants** — CCQ Art. 25 limits compensation to reimbursement for actual loss. For populations where economic hardship is a factor, the REB will scrutinize whether the compensation structure creates undue inducement. Justify your compensation amounts in the protocol.

**Emergency research populations** — where prior consent is not possible because of the participant's acute condition (e.g., trauma, stroke, cardiac arrest). The protocol must describe emergency consent provisions in detail: what procedures will be performed before consent is obtained, the maximum time window allowed before consent or LAR consent must be secured, and what happens if consent cannot be obtained within that window. The REB must approve these provisions in advance — emergency consent cannot be improvised at the bedside.

<Tip>
For research involving **children and minors**, see the dedicated [Pediatric Research Considerations](/kb/pediatric-research) page, which covers age-specific consent and assent requirements, parental authority rules, and the CCQ Art. 21 framework as it applies to participants under 18.
</Tip>

## Identifying vulnerability in your study

Vulnerability is not always obvious from the eligibility criteria. Consider the following during protocol development:

- Does the disease or condition affect cognition, judgment, or the ability to understand risk? If so, a capacity assessment plan is needed.
- Are potential participants likely to be in a dependent relationship with any member of the research team? If so, recruitment and consent safeguards must be described.
- Does the study population face economic hardship that could make even modest compensation coercive? If so, justify your compensation structure explicitly.
- Could the research context itself create vulnerability — for example, recruiting in an emergency department, a psychiatric ward, or a detention facility? If so, context-specific protections must be built into the protocol.

If the answer to any of these is yes, the protocol must describe what additional protections apply and how they will be implemented. The REB will not approve a protocol that simply states the population is vulnerable without specifying the response.

## Capacity assessment

When a protocol involves participants whose decision-making capacity may be impaired, you need a documented plan for assessing capacity — not an assumption about it.

**When it is needed:** Any study enrolling participants with cognitive impairment, psychiatric conditions, acquired brain injury, neurodegenerative disease, or conditions where capacity may be affected by the illness or its treatment.

**Who conducts it:** The person assessing capacity must not be the same person who will obtain consent. This separation is essential to avoid conflicts of interest. The assessment is typically performed by a physician or other qualified professional who is independent of the research team. Name the role (not necessarily the individual) in your protocol so the REB can evaluate whether the arrangement is appropriate.

**What to document:** Record the method of assessment, the date, the name and qualifications of the assessor, and the determination (capable, not capable, or uncertain). If a validated tool was used (e.g., MacCAT-CR), note the instrument and the result. This documentation becomes part of the source documents and will be reviewed during monitoring visits.

**Fluctuating capacity:** Some conditions — delirium, relapsing-remitting psychiatric illness, early-stage dementia — produce capacity that varies over time. The protocol must specify:
- How often capacity will be reassessed
- What triggers a reassessment (clinical deterioration, change in treatment, study milestones)
- What happens if a previously capable participant loses capacity mid-study (transition to LAR consent, withdrawal, or continued participation under specified conditions)

<Note>
A determination that a person lacks capacity at one point in time does not permanently disqualify them. If capacity is restored — whether through recovery, treatment, or stabilization — the participant should be approached for their own informed consent as soon as practicable.
</Note>

## Consent modifications for vulnerable populations

Standard consent procedures require adaptation when vulnerability is present. The following modifications apply depending on the population and context.

**LAR consent for incapacitated adults** — When a participant lacks capacity to consent, consent must come from an LAR as defined under Quebec civil law. In Quebec, the LAR hierarchy follows the CCQ: mandatary (under a protection mandate), tutor, curator, or in the absence of a formal representative, a spouse, close relative, or person showing special interest in the person. The LAR's identity, legal authority, and relationship to the participant must be documented in the source records. The <Tooltip tip="The written document providing the participant with information essential to making an informed decision. Both parties must sign." headline="Informed Consent Form (ICF)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#icf">ICF</Tooltip> must include a section for LAR signature. See [SOP-CR-008](/sops/cr-008) for procedural requirements.

**Enhanced disclosure** — Vulnerable participants (or their LARs) may need additional information beyond what a standard ICF provides:
- A plain-language explanation of why the participant was selected
- A clear description of what alternatives to participation exist, including standard-of-care options
- How the power dynamic (if any) will be managed
- What additional protections are in place specifically because of the participant's situation
- An explicit statement that declining or withdrawing will have no effect on the participant's care, employment, academic standing, or institutional status

**Impartial witness** — Required when the participant cannot read the ICF. The witness must be present for the entire consent discussion, must attest that the information was accurately explained, and must sign the ICF. The witness must be independent of the research team.

**Independent consent monitoring** — For studies involving significant vulnerability (e.g., institutionalized populations, participants with fluctuating capacity), the REB may require appointment of an independent consent monitor — a person not otherwise involved in the study who observes the consent process, verifies that it was conducted appropriately, and may interview the participant separately to confirm understanding and voluntariness. The monitor's observations must be documented, and any concerns raised must be addressed before enrollment proceeds. If your study involves populations where consent validity is likely to be questioned at audit or inspection, proposing an independent consent monitor proactively strengthens the protocol.

**Re-consent triggers specific to vulnerability:**
- Capacity is restored in a participant who was enrolled through LAR consent — the participant's own legally effective consent must be obtained as soon as practicable
- The vulnerable context changes (e.g., a student completes the course taught by the investigator-instructor, a prisoner is released, an employee transfers departments)
- New safety information emerges that may affect the risk-benefit assessment for the specific vulnerability
- The LAR changes (e.g., new mandatary appointed, change in legal representation)

Re-consent for vulnerable populations follows the same documentation requirements as standard re-consent — use the Informed Consent Process Checklist ([SOP-CR-008](/sops/cr-008#appendices), Appendix 3) — but the reason for re-consent must be clearly recorded in the source documents.

## Recruitment safeguards

Recruiting from vulnerable populations requires additional procedural controls.

**Avoiding coercion in dependent populations** — When potential participants are in a power relationship with the researcher (students, employees, patients of the <Tooltip tip="The physician responsible to the sponsor for the conduct of a clinical trial at the site. One QI per study per Canadian site." headline="Qualified Investigator (QI)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#qi">QI</Tooltip>), recruitment must be conducted by someone other than the person who holds power. The QI's own patients should be approached by a <Tooltip tip="The team member who handles day-to-day administration and coordination of the clinical trial." headline="Clinical Research Coordinator (CRC)" cta="Full definition" href="/kb/glossary#crc">CRC</Tooltip> or another team member, not the QI directly. For studies recruiting from institutional settings (long-term care, psychiatric facilities), recruitment should occur through a process that does not single out individuals or make non-participation visible to staff or other residents.

**Compensation proportionality** — Under CCQ Art. 25, compensation must not exceed reimbursement for actual expenses and time lost. For economically disadvantaged participants, even amounts that seem modest to the research team may constitute undue inducement. Present your compensation rationale to the REB with specifics.

**Accessible recruitment materials** — Materials must be appropriate to the population: simplified language for participants with cognitive impairment, large print or audio formats where needed, translations beyond English and French if the target population requires them. All participant-facing materials must be submitted to the REB for approval, including any adapted formats.

**The treating physician restriction** — [SOP-CR-008](/sops/cr-008) prohibits the participant's treating physician from being the person who obtains consent. In vulnerable population research, this restriction extends more broadly: the person who recruits and the person who obtains consent should both be free of any pre-existing relationship that could compromise voluntariness. Document in your protocol who will approach potential participants and why that person was chosen.

<Warning>
The treating physician restriction is especially important in vulnerable population research. A participant who depends on their physician for ongoing care may perceive a research invitation from that physician as an expectation, not a choice. The consent discussion must be conducted by someone without a pre-existing clinical relationship with the participant.
</Warning>

## REB expectations

Research involving vulnerable populations triggers a higher level of REB scrutiny. Expect the following:

**Full board review** — Studies involving vulnerable populations are generally not eligible for delegated review. The full REB will review the protocol, consent documents, and all supporting materials.

**Additional documentation required** — Beyond the standard submission, the REB will expect:
- A capacity assessment plan specifying who will assess, what method will be used, and how often reassessment will occur (for studies involving impaired capacity)
- A process for identifying and documenting the LAR, including how legal authority will be verified (for studies requiring substitute consent)
- An independent consent monitoring plan describing who will monitor, how often, and how findings will be reported (where applicable)
- A justification for inclusion of the vulnerable population — why this population is necessary for the research question and why the research cannot be done with a less vulnerable group
- A description of additional safeguards tailored to the specific vulnerability, with an explanation of how each safeguard addresses the identified risk

**Continuing review** — The REB may impose more frequent continuing review intervals for vulnerable population studies — six months rather than twelve, for example. Annual renewal submissions must address how the safeguards are functioning in practice, not merely confirm that they exist. Be prepared to report:
- How many participants were enrolled under LAR consent vs. self-consent
- Whether any capacity reassessments resulted in a change in consent status
- Whether any participants objected or withdrew, and how those situations were handled
- Any deviations from the vulnerability safeguards described in the protocol

**Amendments** — Any change to a protocol involving vulnerable populations — particularly changes affecting consent procedures, capacity assessment, or recruitment methods — will receive heightened scrutiny. Submit amendments with a clear explanation of why the change is needed and how it affects participant protections.

**Reporting obligations** — Protocol deviations related to vulnerable population safeguards — a missed capacity reassessment, consent obtained by the wrong person, failure to document LAR authority — must be reported promptly. These are not minor administrative gaps; the REB treats them as substantive deviations because they affect the core protections the protocol was designed to provide. See [Reportable Events and Protocol Deviations](/kb/reportable-events).

<Tip>
Build your vulnerability safeguards into the protocol from the start. Retrofitting protections through amendments after the REB raises concerns adds delays and signals that the team did not fully consider the population's needs during study design.
</Tip>

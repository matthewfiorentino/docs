Patient engagement in research is the practice of involving patients, caregivers, and community members as active partners in the research process — not just as study participants, but as contributors to study design, conduct, and dissemination. In Canada, this is formalized through the Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research (SPOR), and increasingly expected by major funders.

## Why this matters

CIHR requires meaningful patient engagement for SPOR-funded competitions and strongly encourages it across all funding programs — particularly for clinical trials, where reviewers assess whether patient perspectives informed the study design. Beyond funder requirements, studies designed with patient input tend to ask more relevant questions, use more practical outcome measures, and achieve better recruitment and retention.

## The SPOR framework

Canada's Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research (SPOR) is a federal-provincial partnership led by CIHR that aims to ensure research is informed by patient priorities and produces outcomes that matter to patients.

SPOR defines patient-oriented research as research that:
- Engages patients as partners in the research process
- Focuses on patient-identified priorities
- Aims to improve patient outcomes through evidence-informed practice

SPOR operates through provincial SUPPORT Units (in Quebec: the Unité de soutien SSA Québec — formerly the Unité de soutien SRAP) that provide methodological and practical support for patient-oriented research.

## Levels of engagement

Patient engagement exists on a spectrum. The right level depends on the study, the research question, and the stage of the project.

The IAP2 Spectrum of Public Participation, adapted by CIHR for the SPOR Patient Engagement Framework, defines five levels:

| Level | What it looks like | When it's appropriate |
|---|---|---|
| **Inform** | Patients receive information about the study | Minimum for all studies — standard participant communication |
| **Consult** | Patients are asked for input on specific questions (e.g., reviewing consent forms for readability, commenting on outcome measures) | Early-stage study design; protocol refinement |
| **Involve** | Patients work directly with the research team to ensure concerns are understood and considered throughout the process | Studies where patient experience shapes design decisions |
| **Collaborate** | Patients participate in study governance — serving on steering committees, contributing to data interpretation, co-developing knowledge translation materials | Studies with direct patient impact; CIHR-funded patient-oriented research |
| **Empower** | Final decision-making authority is placed in the hands of patients | Community-based participatory research; studies addressing patient-identified priorities |

For most clinical trials at the RI-MUHC, engagement at the **consult** or **collaborate** level is realistic and productive. Even at the consult level, patient input on consent form language, visit burden, and outcome relevance can improve the study.

## Recruiting patient partners

Patient partners are not study participants. They are individuals with lived experience of a condition or the healthcare system who contribute their perspective to the research process.

**Where to find patient partners:**
- MUHC patient committees and patient advisory groups
- Disease-specific patient organizations (e.g., Diabetes Québec, Société canadienne du cancer)
- The Quebec SPOR SUPPORT Unit (Unité de soutien SRAP du Québec) maintains a network of trained patient partners
- Community organizations and advocacy groups
- Previous study participants who expressed interest in research involvement (with appropriate consent)

**Considerations when recruiting:**
- Seek diversity — patient partners should reflect the population affected by the research, including diversity in age, gender, language, socioeconomic background, and illness experience
- Be transparent about the time commitment, expectations, and what the partnership entails
- Patients with lived experience of the specific condition under study are ideal, but patients with general healthcare experience can also contribute (e.g., reviewing consent forms, assessing visit burden)

## Compensation

Patient partners invest time, energy, and expertise. Compensation acknowledges this contribution and removes financial barriers to participation.

CIHR and SPOR guidelines recommend that patient partners be compensated for their time and out-of-pocket expenses (travel, parking, childcare). Compensation rates should be fair and consistent:

- Include patient partner compensation as a line item in grant budgets
- Follow institutional and funder guidelines for compensation rates
- Reimburse expenses (travel, parking) separately from time compensation
- Consider accessibility needs — meeting times, virtual options, language support

<Note>
Patient partner compensation is distinct from participant compensation for study visits. They are budgeted and managed separately. For participant compensation, see [Recruitment, Screening, and Enrollment](/kb/recruitment-enrollment).
</Note>

## Integrating patient perspectives into study design

Patient engagement is most valuable when it happens early — during study design, before the protocol is finalized.

**Areas where patient input adds value:**

- **Research question and outcomes** — are the outcomes meaningful to patients? Would patients prioritize different endpoints?
- **Consent materials** — is the language clear? Are the risks described in a way patients understand? Is the consent form too long?
- **Visit schedule and burden** — is the number of visits practical? Are the assessments acceptable? Would patients tolerate the procedures described?
- **Recruitment strategy** — how would patients prefer to learn about the study? What would motivate them to participate? What barriers exist?
- **Knowledge translation** — how should results be communicated back to patients and the public?

## Documentation

For funded research requiring patient engagement, document the process:

- Who was engaged, at what level, and at what stage
- How patient input influenced study design decisions
- Meeting records, if formal advisory meetings were held
- Compensation provided

This documentation may be required for CIHR progress reports and final reporting.
